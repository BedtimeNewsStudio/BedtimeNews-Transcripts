**Cédric Villani** (born October 5, 1973, Brive-la-Gaillarde, France) is a French mathematician and politician who was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://www.britannica.com/science/Fields-Medal) in 2010 for his work in [mathematical](https://www.britannica.com/science/mathematical-physics) [physics](https://www.britannica.com/science/physics-science).

Villani studied [mathematics](https://www.britannica.com/science/mathematics) at the École Normale Supériere in Paris. He received a master’s degree in numerical analysis from Pierre and Marie Curie University in Paris in 1996 and a doctorate in mathematics from the University of Paris Dauphine in 1998. From 2000 to 2010 he was a professor of mathematics at the École Normale Supériere in Lyon, and in 2010 he became a professor of mathematics at the University of Lyon. In addition, he served as director of the Institut Henri Poncairé from 2009 to 2017.

Villani was awarded the Fields Medal at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Hyderabad, India, in 2010 for his work involving [entropy](https://www.britannica.com/science/entropy-physics). The amount of molecular disorder, or randomness, of a system is measured by entropy. Entropy always increases in a system until it is in thermal equilibrium with its environment. However, the rate at which entropy increases was unknown until 2005, when Villani and French mathematician Laurent Desvillettes determined that entropy did not increase at a constant rate. In 2009 Villani and French mathematician Clément Mouhot proved Soviet physicist [Lev Landau](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lev-Landau)’s conjecture that [plasma](https://www.britannica.com/science/plasma-state-of-matter) reaches equilibrium without increasing its entropy. Villani also found applications for the mathematical study of entropy in [differential geometry](https://www.britannica.com/science/differential-geometry) and in transport theory.

- What is the Fields Medal?

The [Fields Medal](https://www.britannica.com/science/Fields-Medal/Introduction#ref1) is awarded to mathematicians for outstanding research and potential future achievements. Often considered the mathematical equivalent of the [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/), the Fields Medal is granted every four years to mathematicians under the age of 40.

The award originated from funds raised by John Charles Fields, a mathematics professor at the University of Toronto. The first two medals were awarded in 1936, and, since 1966, up to four medals have been awarded at each congress. Medalists also receive a cash award. The [International Mathematical Union](https://www.mathunion.org/) (IMU) is broadly responsible for the award.

## Origins

[![John Charles Fields](https://cdn.britannica.com/27/18827-004-02969BB7/John-Charles-Fields.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/27/18827-004-02969BB7/John-Charles-Fields.jpg)

[John Charles Fields](https://cdn.britannica.com/27/18827-004-02969BB7/John-Charles-Fields.jpg)(more)

The Fields Medal originated from surplus funds raised by John Charles Fields (1863–1932), a professor of mathematics at the [University of Toronto](https://www.britannica.com/topic/University-of-Toronto), as organizer and president of the 1924 International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM) in [Toronto](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto). The Committee of the International Congress had $2,700 left after printing the conference proceedings and voted to set aside $2,500 for the establishment of two medals to be awarded at later congresses. Following an endowment from Fields’s estate, the proposed awards—contrary to his explicit request—became known as the Fields Medals.

The first two Fields Medals were awarded in 1936. An anonymous donation allowed the number of medals awarded at each congress to increase, from two to as many as four, starting in 1966. Medalists also receive a cash award.

## Selection process and prize recipients

The Fields Medal Committee, which is chosen by the Executive Committee of the International Mathematical Union (IMU), selects the winners of the Fields Medal. The chair of the Fields Medal Committee is publicly announced, but the other committee members are not identified until after the prize is awarded. Nominations, which are made to the committee’s chair, are [confidential](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/confidential) and, according to the prize’s statutes, “must not be disclosed to the candidate.” The statutes also express a “strong preference” for four prize recipients and encourage the committee to choose recipients that reflect the breadth of the study of [mathematics](https://www.britannica.com/science/mathematics).

Fields Medals have been presented at each ICM since 1936. The next ICM after 1936 was in 1950, and it has since been organized every four years. The congress is organized by the IMU, which is broadly responsible for the award. A related award, the Rolf Nevanlinna Prize, was also presented at each ICM from 1982 to 2018; it was replaced by the IMU [Abacus](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/Abacus) Medal in 2022. This prize is awarded to one young mathematician for work dealing with the mathematical aspects of [information science](https://www.britannica.com/science/information-science).

[Maryam Mirzakhani](https://cdn.britannica.com/15/181315-050-8853F093/Maryam-Mirzakhani-stanford-2014-Fields-Medal.jpg) Maryam Mirzakhani, 2014.(more)

Notable recipients of the Fields Medal include [Jean-Pierre Serre](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jean-Pierre-Serre), who won it in 1954 at age 27, making him the youngest winner. During the 1970s the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) prevented [Sergei Novikov](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sergey-Novikov) and [Gregory Margulis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gregory-Margulis) from traveling to receive the award. [Maryam Mirzakhani](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Maryam-Mirzakhani) was the first woman to win the Fields Medal (2014). In 2022 Maryna Viazovska, who was born in Ukraine, became the second woman to win; she received the award in Finland after that year’s ceremony was moved from its original location in Russia because of [Russia’s invasion of Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War).

[Andrew Wiles](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Andrew-Wiles), an English mathematician, is notable for not having received a Fields Medal. He devised a proof of [Fermat’s last theorem](https://www.britannica.com/science/Fermats-last-theorem), with the help of his former student [Richard Taylor](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Richard-Taylor-filmmaker), and published it in 1995. Because he was older than 40, he could not win a Fields Medal for this milestone achievement; the IMU instead presented him with a special silver plaque in 1998.

The Fields Medal is a useful indicator of active and emerging fields of mathematical research, as the winners have generally made contributions that opened up entirely new fields or [integrated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrated) technical ideas and tools from a wide variety of [disciplines](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/disciplines). A preponderance of winners have worked in highly abstract and integrative fields, such as [algebraic geometry](https://www.britannica.com/science/algebraic-geometry) and [algebraic topology](https://www.britannica.com/science/algebraic-topology). This is to some extent a reflection of the influence and power of the French [consortium](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/consortium) of mathematicians, writing since the 1930s under the pseudonym of [Nicolas Bourbaki](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nicolas-Bourbaki), which in its multivolume _Éléments de mathématiques_ has sought a modern, rigorous, and [comprehensive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprehensive) treatment of all of mathematics and mathematical foundations. However, medals have also been awarded for work in more classical fields of mathematics and for [mathematical physics](https://www.britannica.com/science/mathematical-physics), including a number for solutions to problems that [David Hilbert](https://www.britannica.com/biography/David-Hilbert) enunciated at the ICM in Paris in 1900.
