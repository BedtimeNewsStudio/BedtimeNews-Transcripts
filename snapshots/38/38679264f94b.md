SpaceX Raptor

**Raptor** is a family of [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine "Rocket engine") developed and manufactured by [SpaceX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX "SpaceX"). It was the first [full-flow staged combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staged_combustion_cycle#Full-flow_staged_combustion_cycle "Staged combustion cycle") engine to power a vehicle in flight, on the [Starhopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starhopper "Starhopper") test vehicle in July 2019.[&#8202;](#ref-back-hackaday201902-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-hackaday201902)[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-14) The engine is powered by [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic "Cryogenic") [liquid methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane#Rocket_propellant "Methane") and [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen"), a combination known as [methalox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_propellant#Methane "Liquid rocket propellant").

SpaceX's [super-heavy-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle "Super heavy-lift launch vehicle") rocket [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship") uses Raptor engines in its [Super Heavy booster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Super_Heavy "SpaceX Super Heavy") and in the [Starship second stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_(spacecraft) "SpaceX Starship (spacecraft)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-sx-pug202003-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-sx-pug202003) Starship missions include lifting payloads to Earth orbit and is also planned for missions to the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS "Starship HLS") and [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Mars_program "SpaceX Mars program").[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20131025-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-sn20131025) The engines are being designed for reuse with little maintenance.[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-17)

## Design

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Design")\]

Raptor is designed for extreme reliability, aiming to support the airline-level safety required by the point-to-point Earth transportation market.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20171015raptor-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-sn20171015raptor) [Gwynne Shotwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwynne_Shotwell "Gwynne Shotwell") claimed that Raptor would be able to deliver "long life... and more benign turbine environments".[&#8202;](#ref-back-shotwell201503317-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-shotwell201503317)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

### Full-flow staged combustion

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Full-flow staged combustion")\]

See also: [Liquid-propellant rocket §Engine cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket#Engine_cycles "Liquid-propellant rocket")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Full_flow_staged_rocket_cycle.png/250px-Full_flow_staged_rocket_cycle.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Full_flow_staged_rocket_cycle.png) Simplified full-flow staged combustion rocket diagram

Raptor is powered by [subcooled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcooling "Subcooling") [liquid methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_methane "Liquid methane") and subcooled [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") in a [full-flow staged combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staged_combustion_cycle#Full-flow_staged_combustion_cycle "Staged combustion cycle") cycle. This type of combustion is a twin-shaft staged combustion cycle that uses both oxidizer-rich and fuel-rich preburners. The cycle allows for the full flow of both propellants through the turbines without dumping any unburnt propellant overboard.

Full-flow staged combustion is a departure from the more traditional ["open-cycle" gas generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas-generator_cycle_(rocket) "Gas-generator cycle (rocket)") system and LOX/kerosene propellants used by its predecessor [Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlin_(rocket_engine_family) "Merlin (rocket engine family)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-fg20121122-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-fg20121122) Before Raptor, no full-flow staged combustion engine had ever been used inflight and only two designs had progressed sufficiently to reach test stands: the Soviet [RD-270](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RD-270 "RD-270") project in the 1960s, a full-scale test engine and the [Aerojet Rocketdyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerojet_Rocketdyne "Aerojet Rocketdyne") [Integrated Powerhead Demonstrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Powerhead_Demonstrator "Integrated Powerhead Demonstrator") in the mid-2000s, which only demonstrated the powerhead.[&#8202;](#ref-back-hackaday201902-1)[\[12\]](#refnote-hackaday201902)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-1)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20140307-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-nsf20140307) [RS-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25 "RS-25") engines (first used on the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle")) used a simpler form of staged combustion cycle.[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-23) Several Russian rocket engines, including the [RD-180](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RD-180 "RD-180")[&#8202;](#ref-back-fg20121122-1)[\[20\]](#refnote-fg20121122) and the [RD-191](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RD-191 "RD-191") did as well.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-2)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

Full-flow staged combustion has the advantage that the energy produced by the preburners, and used to power the propellant pumps, is spread among the entire fuel flow, meaning that the preburner exhaust driving the propellant turbopumps is as cool as possible, even cooler than other closed engine cycles that only preburn one propellant. This contributes to a long engine life. In contrast, an open-cycle engine in which the preburner exhaust bypasses the main combustion chamber tries to minimize the amount of propellant fed through the preburner, which is achieved by operating the turbine at its maximum survivable temperature.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Raptor_2_Full_Flow_Staged_Combustion_Cycle_Estimate.svg/500px-Raptor_2_Full_Flow_Staged_Combustion_Cycle_Estimate.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Raptor_2_Full_Flow_Staged_Combustion_Cycle_Estimate.svg)

[File:Raptor 2 Full Flow Staged Combustion Cycle Estimate.svg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Raptor_2_Full_Flow_Staged_Combustion_Cycle_Estimate.svg "File:Raptor 2 Full Flow Staged Combustion Cycle Estimate.svg")

Raptor 2 rocket engine cycle diagram with estimates from open-source information and analysis

An oxygen-rich turbine powers an oxygen turbopump, and a fuel-rich turbine powers a methane turbopump. Both oxidizer and fuel streams are converted completely to the [gas phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_phase "Gas phase") before they enter the [combustion chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine#Combustion_chamber "Rocket engine").[&#8202;](#ref-back-hackaday201902-2)[\[12\]](#refnote-hackaday201902) This speeds up mixing and combustion, reducing the size and mass of the required combustion chamber. Torch igniters are used in the preburners. Because of the high temperatures of the preburner exhaust, the main combustion chamber of Raptor 2 has no main igniter, which eliminate the need for Merlin's dedicated, consumable igniter fluid.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-3)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) Raptor 2 uses coaxial swirl injectors to admit propellants to the combustion chamber, rather than Merlin's [pintle injectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pintle_injector "Pintle injector").[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-24)

### Propellants

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Propellants")\]

Raptor is designed for [deep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcooling "Subcooling") [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic "Cryogenic") propellants—fluids cooled to near their [freezing points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freezing_point "Freezing point"), rather than their [boiling points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point "Boiling point"), as is typical for cryogenic rocket engines.[&#8202;](#ref-back-issconf20150707-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-issconf20150707) Subcooled propellants are denser, increasing propellant mass per volume[&#8202;](#ref-back-26-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-26) as well as engine performance. [Specific impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_impulse "Specific impulse") is increased, and the risk of [cavitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavitation "Cavitation") at inputs to the turbopumps is reduced due to the higher propellant fuel mass flow rate per unit of power generated.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-4)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) The [oxidizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizer "Oxidizer") to [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel "Fuel") mass ratio of the engine is approximately 3.6 to 1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-27) Methalox burns relatively cleanly, reducing carbon build-up in the engine.

Liquid methane and [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") propellants have been adopted by many companies, such as [Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Origin "Blue Origin") with its [BE-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BE-4 "BE-4") engine, as well as Chinese startup Space Epoch's [Longyun-70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longyun_(rocket_engine) "Longyun (rocket engine)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-28)

### Manufacturing and materials

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Manufacturing and materials")\]

Many components of early Raptor prototypes were manufactured using [3D printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_printing "3D printing"), including turbopumps and injectors, increasing the speed of development and testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-issconf20150707-1)[\[24\]](#refnote-issconf20150707) The 2016 subscale development engine had 40% (by mass) of its parts manufactured by 3D printing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-5)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

### Conception

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Conception")\]

SpaceX's [Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Merlin "SpaceX Merlin") and [Kestrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Kestrel "SpaceX Kestrel") rocket engines use an [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1 "RP-1") and liquid oxygen ("kerolox") combination. Raptor has about triple the thrust of SpaceX's [Merlin 1D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlin_(rocket_engine_family)#Merlin_1D "Merlin (rocket engine family)") engine, which powers the [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust "Falcon 9 Full Thrust") and [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") launch vehicles.

In October 2012, SpaceX announced concept work on an engine that would be "several times as powerful as the [Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Merlin "SpaceX Merlin") 1 series of engines, and won't use Merlin's [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1 "RP-1") fuel".[&#8202;](#ref-back-fg20121017-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-fg20121017)

### Development

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Development")\]

In November 2012, SpaceX founder and CEO Elon Musk announced that SpaceX was working on [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane")-fueled rocket engines, that Raptor would be methane-based,[&#8202;](#ref-back-fg20121120-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-fg20121120) and that methane would fuel Mars colonization.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20140307-1)[\[21\]](#refnote-nsf20140307) Because of the presence of [underground water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_on_Mars "Water on Mars") and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") in [Mars atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Mars "Atmosphere of Mars"), methane, a simple [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon "Hydrocarbon"), could be synthesized on Mars using the [Sabatier reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabatier_reaction "Sabatier reaction").[&#8202;](#ref-back-31-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-31) [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") analysis found [in-situ resource production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-situ_resource_utilization "In-situ resource utilization") on Mars to be viable for oxygen, water, and methane production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa20151108-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-nasa20151108)

In early 2014 SpaceX confirmed that Raptor would be used for both first and second stages of its next rocket. This held as the design evolved from the [Mars Colonial Transporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Colonial_Transporter "Mars Colonial Transporter")[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20140307-2)[\[21\]](#refnote-nsf20140307) to the [Interplanetary Transport System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interplanetary_Transport_System "Interplanetary Transport System"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20160927-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-sn20160927) the [Big Falcon Rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Falcon_Rocket "Big Falcon Rocket"), and ultimately, Starship.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20171015-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-sn20171015)

The concept evolved from a family of Raptor-designated rocket engines (2012)[&#8202;](#ref-back-201211raptorSpecifics-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-201211raptorSpecifics) to focus on the full-size Raptor engine (2014).[&#8202;](#ref-back-tss20140321-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-tss20140321)

The first version was intended to operate at a chamber pressure of 250 bars (25MPa; 3,600psi).[&#8202;](#ref-back-musk20170929-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-musk20170929) In April 2024, Musk shared the performance achieved by SpaceX with the Raptor 1 engine (sea level 185 tf, RVac 200 tf) and Raptor 2 engine (sea level 230 tf, RVac 258 tf) along with the target specifications for the upcoming Raptor 3 (sea level 280 tf, RVac 306 tf)[&#8202;](#ref-back-ars20240408-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-ars20240408)[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20240406-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-sn20240406) and said SpaceX would aim to ultimately achieve over 330 tonnes of thrust on the sea-level booster engines.[&#8202;](#ref-back-musk20240406-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-musk20240406)

Raptor 1 and 2 engines require a heat shroud to protect pipes and wiring from the heat of high-velocity atmospheric re-entry, while Raptor 3 integrates its sensors and controllers inside the engine's thermal protection, eliminating the need for individual engine shrouds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sx20260512-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-sx20260512)

### Testing

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Testing")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/SpaceX%27s_Raptor_oxygen_preburner_testing_at_Stennis_%282015%29.jpg/250px-SpaceX%27s_Raptor_oxygen_preburner_testing_at_Stennis_%282015%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SpaceX's_Raptor_oxygen_preburner_testing_at_Stennis_(2015).jpg) Testing of the Raptor 's oxygen [preburner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preburner "Preburner") at [Stennis Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Stennis_Space_Center "John C. Stennis Space Center") in 2015[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Raptor-test-9-25-2016.jpg/250px-Raptor-test-9-25-2016.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Raptor-test-9-25-2016.jpg) First test firing of a Raptor development engine on 25 September 2016 in [McGregor, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_launch_facilities#SpaceX_rocket_development_and_test_facility,_McGregor,_Texas "SpaceX launch facilities")

Initial [development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_product_development "New product development") [testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine_testing "Rocket engine testing")[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa201509-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-nasa201509) of Raptor components was done at NASA's [Stennis Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stennis_Space_Center "Stennis Space Center"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20131025-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-sn20131025)[&#8202;](#ref-back-pa20131023-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-pa20131023) beginning in April 2014. Testing focused on startup and shutdown procedures, as well as hardware characterization and [verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verification_and_validation "Verification and validation").[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-6)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

SpaceX began testing injectors in 2014 and tested an oxygen [preburner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preburner "Preburner") in 2015. 76 hot-fire tests of the preburner, totaling some 400 seconds of test time, were executed from April–August.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa201509-1)[\[40\]](#refnote-nasa201509)

By early 2016, SpaceX had constructed an engine test stand at their [McGregor test site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_McGregor "SpaceX McGregor") in central Texas for Raptor testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-7)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20131025-2)[\[15\]](#refnote-sn20131025) The first Raptor was manufactured at the [SpaceX Hawthorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Hawthorne "SpaceX Hawthorne") facility in California. By August 2016 it was shipped to McGregor for development testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-ars20160810-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-ars20160810) The engine had 1MN (220,000lbf) thrust.[&#8202;](#ref-back-musk20200817c-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-musk20200817c) It was the first-ever full-flow staged combustion methalox engine to reach a test stand.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-8)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

A subscale development engine was used for design validation. It was one-third the size of the engine designs that were envisioned for flight vehicles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-9)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) It featured 200 bars (20MPa; 2,900psi) of chamber pressure, with a thrust of 1 meganewton (220,000lbf) . It was tested on a [ground test stand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine_test_facility "Rocket engine test facility") in [McGregor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_McGregor "SpaceX McGregor"), firing briefly.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-10)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) To eliminate [flow separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_separation "Flow separation") problems while testing in Earth's atmosphere, the test nozzle expansion ratio was limited to 150.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-11)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

By September 2017, the subscale engine had completed 1200 seconds of firings across 42 tests.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20180809-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-nsf20180809)

SpaceX completed many static fire tests on a vehicle using Raptor 2s, including a 31 engine test (intended to be 33) on 9 February 2023,[&#8202;](#ref-back-46-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-46) and a 33 engine test on 25 August 2023.[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-47)

SpaceX completed its [first integrated flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_1 "SpaceX Starship integrated flight test 1") of Starship on 20 April 2023. The rocket had 33 Raptor2 engines, but three of those were shut down before the rocket lifted off from the launch mount. The flight test was terminated after climbing to an altitude of ~39km over the [Gulf of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico "Gulf of Mexico"). Multiple engines were out before the flight termination system (FTS) destroyed the booster and ship.[&#8202;](#ref-back-spacex-starship-flight-test-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-spacex-starship-flight-test)

On the [second integrated flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_2 "SpaceX Starship integrated flight test 2"), all 33 booster engines remained lit until boostback burn startup, and all six Starship engines remained lit until the FTS was activated.[&#8202;](#ref-back-49-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-49)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:45-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-:45)

On the [third integrated flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_3 "SpaceX Starship integrated flight test 3"), all 33 booster engines once again remained lit until main engine cutoff, and then following hot-staging, 13 successfully relit to perform a boostback for full duration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:4-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-:4) On the booster's landing burn, only 3 engines of the planned 13 lit, with 2 shutting down rapidly, the other remained lit until the booster was destroyed ~462 metres above sea level.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:4-1)[\[50\]](#refnote-:4) The ship successfully kept all 6 engines lit until second stage / secondary engine cutoff without issues, however a planned in-space Raptor re-light was cancelled due to rolling during coast.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:4-2)[\[50\]](#refnote-:4)

The [seventh flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_7 "Starship flight test 7") featured the first reflown Raptor engine,[&#8202;](#ref-back-52-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-52) which was successfully flown during Super Heavy Booster 14's ascent burn and was recovered after its successful catch by the launch tower. A further 29 raptors were reflown on B14 for Starship's [ninth test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_9 "Starship flight test 9").[&#8202;](#ref-back-53-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-53)

### Starship

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Starship")\]

Main article: [SpaceX Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship")

#### Original configuration

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Original configuration")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/BFR_in_flight_%28cropped%29-2018_version.png/250px-BFR_in_flight_%28cropped%29-2018_version.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:BFR_in_flight_(cropped)-2018_version.png) [Big Falcon Rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Falcon_Rocket "Big Falcon Rocket") during ascent (artist's concept)

In November 2016, Raptor was projected to power the proposed [Interplanetary Transport System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interplanetary_Transport_System "Interplanetary Transport System") (ITS), in the early 2020s.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-12)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) Musk discussed two engines: a sea-level variant (expansion ratio 40:1) with thrust of 3,050kN (690,000lbf) at sea level  for the first stage/booster, and a vacuum variant (expansion ratio 200:1) with thrust of 3,285kN (738,000lbf) in space. 42 sea-level engines were envisioned in the high-level design of the first stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-13)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

Three [gimbaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal#Rocket_engines "Gimbal") sea-level Raptor engines would be used for [landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retropropulsive_landing "Retropropulsive landing") the second stage. Six additional, non-gimbaling vacuum-optimized Raptor Vacuum engines would provide primary thrust for the second stage, for a total of nine engines.[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-54)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-14)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003) Raptor Vacuums were envisioned to contribute a [specific impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_impulse "Specific impulse") of 382s (3,750m/s), using a [nozzle extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine_nozzle#Vacuum_use "Rocket engine nozzle").[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-:0)

In September 2017 Musk said that a smaller Raptor engine—with slightly over half as much thrust as the previous designs—would be used on the next-generation rocket, a 9m (30ft)-diameter launch vehicle termed Big Falcon Rocket (BFR) and later renamed [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship").[&#8202;](#ref-back-56-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-56) The redesign was aimed at Earth-orbit and [cislunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cislunar_space "Cislunar space") missions so that the new system might [pay for itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_return "Rate of return"), in part, through economic spaceflight activities in the near-Earth space zone.[&#8202;](#ref-back-issR&Dconf20170719-49:48-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-issR&Dconf20170719-49:48) With the much smaller launch vehicle, fewer Raptor engines would be needed. BFR was then slated to have 31 Raptors on the first stage and 6 on the second stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20170929-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-sn20170929)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20161003-15)[\[19\]](#refnote-nsf20161003)

By mid-2018, SpaceX was publicly stating that the sea-level Raptor was expected to have 1,700kN (380,000lbf) thrust at sea level with a specific impulse of 330s (3,200m/s), with a nozzle exit diameter of 1.3m (4.3ft). Raptor Vacuum would have specific impulse of 356s (3,490m/s) in vacuum[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20180809-1)[\[44\]](#refnote-nsf20180809) and was expected to exert 1,900kN (430,000lbf) force with a specific impulse of 375s (3,680m/s), using a nozzle exit diameter of 2.4m (7.9ft).[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20180809-2)[\[44\]](#refnote-nsf20180809)

In the [BFR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Falcon_Rocket "Big Falcon Rocket") update given in September 2018, Musk showed a video of a 71-second fire test of a Raptor engine, and stated that "this is Raptor that will power BFR, both the ship and the booster; it's the same engine. \[&#8202;](#ref-back-SpaceX20180917-1)[...\] approximately a 200 (metric) tons engine aiming for roughly 300 bar chamber pressure. \[...\] If you had it at a high expansion ratio, has the potential to have a specific impulse of 380."[\[9\]](#refnote-SpaceX20180917) SpaceX aimed at a lifetime of 1000 flights.[&#8202;](#ref-back-59-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-59)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Starship_SN20_getting_a_tile_inspection.jpg/250px-Starship_SN20_getting_a_tile_inspection.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Starship_SN20_getting_a_tile_inspection.jpg) [Starship SN20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_SN20 "Starship SN20") has its tiles inspected

### Proposed Falcon 9 upper stage

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Proposed Falcon 9 upper stage")\]

In January 2016, the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Air_Force "US Air Force") (USAF) awarded a US$33.6 million development contract to SpaceX to develop a Raptor prototype for use on the [upper stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_stage "Upper stage") of the [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") and [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy"). The contract required double-matching funding by SpaceX of at least US$67.3 million.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dod20160113-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-dod20160113)[&#8202;](#ref-back-snd20160113-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-snd20160113) Engine testing was planned for NASA's [Stennis Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stennis_Space_Center "Stennis Space Center") in [Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi "Mississippi") under US Air Force supervision.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dod20160113-1)[\[59\]](#refnote-dod20160113)[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20160113-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-sn20160113) The USAF contract called for a single prototype engine and ground tests.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dod20160113-2)[\[59\]](#refnote-dod20160113)

In October 2017 USAF awarded a US$40.8 million modification contract for a Raptor prototype for the [Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_Expendable_Launch_Vehicle "Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle") program.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dod20171019-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-dod20171019) It was to use [liquid methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_methane "Liquid methane") and [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") propellants, a [full-flow staged combustion cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-flow_staged_combustion "Full-flow staged combustion"), and be reusable.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20160113-1)[\[61\]](#refnote-sn20160113)

### Production

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Production")\]

In July 2021, SpaceX announced a second Raptor production facility, in central Texas near the [existing rocket engine test facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_McGregor "SpaceX McGregor"). The facility would concentrate on serial production of Raptor 2, while the California facility would produce Raptor Vacuum and new/experimental Raptor designs. The new facility was expected to eventually produce 800 to 1000 rocket engines each year.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dallasnews202107-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-dallasnews202107)[&#8202;](#ref-back-spacex20210710-0)[\[64\]](#refnote-spacex20210710) In 2019 the (marginal) cost of the engine was stated to be approaching US$1 million. SpaceX planned to mass-produce up to 500 Raptor engines per year, each costing less than US$250,000.[&#8202;](#ref-back-66-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-66)

### Raptor Vacuum

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Raptor Vacuum")\]

Each version of the engine has a corresponding Raptor Vacuum (RVac) variant[&#8202;](#ref-back-space20200924-0)[\[68\]](#refnote-space20200924) with an extended, [regeneratively-cooled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_cooling_(rocketry) "Regenerative cooling (rocketry)") nozzle for higher specific impulse in space. The vacuum-optimized Raptor targets a specific impulse of ≈380s (3,700m/s).[&#8202;](#ref-back-raptor-sep-2019-isp-1)[\[8\]](#refnote-raptor-sep-2019-isp) A full-duration test of version 1 of Raptor Vacuum was completed in September 2020 at McGregor.[&#8202;](#ref-back-space20200924-1)[\[68\]](#refnote-space20200924) The first in-flight ignition of a Raptor Vacuum was on S25 during the [second integrated flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_2 "SpaceX Starship integrated flight test 2").[&#8202;](#ref-back-:45-1)[\[49\]](#refnote-:45)

### Raptor 2

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Raptor 2")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/A_person_viewing_Raptor_Vacuum.jpg/250px-A_person_viewing_Raptor_Vacuum.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:A_person_viewing_Raptor_Vacuum.jpg) A NASA employee standing between two Raptor 2 vacuum-optimized engines (background) and a Raptor 2 sea-level (foreground).

Raptor 2 is a complete redesign of the Raptor 1 engine.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[69\]](#refnote-:1) The turbomachinery, chamber, nozzle, and electronics were redesigned. Many [flanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flange "Flange") were converted to [welds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welding "Welding"), while other parts were deleted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20220211-0)[\[70\]](#refnote-nsf20220211) Simplifications continued after production began. On 10 February 2022, Musk showed Raptor 2 capabilities and design improvements.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20220211-1)[\[70\]](#refnote-nsf20220211)[&#8202;](#ref-back-72-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-72)

By 18 December 2021, Raptor 2 had started production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-73-0)[\[72\]](#refnote-73) By November 2022, SpaceX produced more than one Raptor a day and had created a stockpile for future launches.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Isaacson2023-0)[\[73\]](#refnote-Isaacson2023) Raptor 2s are produced at SpaceX's [McGregor engine development facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_McGregor "SpaceX McGregor").

Raptor 2s were achieving 230 [tf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ton-force#Tonne-force "Ton-force") (510,000 [lbf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_(force) "Pound (force)")) of thrust consistently by February 2022. Musk indicated that production costs were approximately half that of Raptor1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nsf20220211-2)[\[70\]](#refnote-nsf20220211)

### Raptor 3

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Raptor 3")\]

Raptor 3 is aimed to ultimately achieve 300 [tf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ton-force#Tonne-force "Ton-force") (2.9 [MN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit) "Newton (unit)")) of thrust in the booster/sea-level configuration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20240406-1)[\[38\]](#refnote-sn20240406) In April 2024, SpaceX's stated target for Raptor 3 was 280tf (2,700kN) at sea level.[&#8202;](#ref-back-ars20240408-1)[\[37\]](#refnote-ars20240408)[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20240406-2)[\[38\]](#refnote-sn20240406) In its May 2026 Starship V3 update, SpaceX stated that the flight engines produce 250tf (2,500kN; 550,000lbf) at sea level (up from 230tf for Raptor 2) and 275tf (2,700kN; 610,000lbf) for the vacuum variant (up from 258tf), with the sea-level engine mass reduced to 1,525kg (3,362lb) from 1630kg; SpaceX said simplification of the engine and vehicle-side hardware saves about one tonne per engine at vehicle level, and that all variants use a redesigned ignition system.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sx20260512-3)[\[6\]](#refnote-sx20260512)[&#8202;](#ref-back-75-0)[\[74\]](#refnote-75)

The main goal of Raptor 3 was to eliminate protective engine shrouds by moving the majority of the plumbing and sensors into the engine's main structure,[&#8202;](#ref-back-ars20240408-2)[\[37\]](#refnote-ars20240408)[&#8202;](#ref-back-musk20240406-1)[\[39\]](#refnote-musk20240406) taking advantage of the already present regenerative cooling. On 2 August 2024, Raptor 3 SN1 was revealed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Raptor3SN1-0)[\[75\]](#refnote-Raptor3SN1) The reduction in externally visible components resulted in the CEO of [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance"), [Tory Bruno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tory_Bruno "Tory Bruno") mistakenly accusing SpaceX of revealing a "partially assembled" engine while comparing it to "fully assembled" engines.[&#8202;](#ref-back-77-0)[\[76\]](#refnote-77)[&#8202;](#ref-back-78-0)[\[77\]](#refnote-78)

Many bolted joints in Raptor 2 have been eliminated/replaced by single parts and/or welds. However, this makes servicing more difficult, as some parts may lie beneath welds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-tea20240622-0)[\[78\]](#refnote-tea20240622):42:19–45:50

On 22 May 2026, [Flight 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_12 "Starship flight test 12"), the first flight of Starship V3, was also the first flight of Raptor 3.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sx-f12-0)[\[79\]](#refnote-sx-f12) One of the booster's 33 Raptor 3 engines shut down about 1 minute 42 seconds into ascent; after stage separation several engines failed to relight for the boostback burn, which ended early, and the booster made a hard splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico. One of the ship's three Raptor Vacuum engines also shut down during ascent, but the ship reached its planned trajectory.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sx-f12-1)[\[79\]](#refnote-sx-f12)[&#8202;](#ref-back-sn20260522-0)[\[80\]](#refnote-sn20260522)[&#8202;](#ref-back-82-0)[\[81\]](#refnote-82) The FAA-accepted mishap report cited heat effects on propulsion system components during ascent and erroneous engine alarm system settings as the most probable root causes of the booster loss.[&#8202;](#ref-back-83-0)[\[82\]](#refnote-83)

On 24 July 2026, [Flight 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_13 "Starship flight test 13") kept all 33 booster Raptor 3 engines running through ascent and completed the high-thrust portion of the boostback burn on all 33 engines for the first time with a V3 booster, though only a subset relit for the landing burn. The ship relit a single Raptor in space and performed a landing burn with all three sea-level engines before a soft splashdown in the Indian Ocean.[&#8202;](#ref-back-84-0)[\[83\]](#refnote-84)[&#8202;](#ref-back-85-0)[\[84\]](#refnote-85)

On 28 September 2026, [Flight 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_14 "Starship flight 14") became the first Starship flight to reach orbit. One booster engine shut down during ascent, 31 of 33 relit for boostback and 11 of 13 for the landing burn. A Raptor Vacuum engine on the ship shut down early during ascent, which the remaining five engines compensated for by burning longer; a single sea-level Raptor was then relit for the orbit-insertion burn.[&#8202;](#ref-back-86-0)[\[85\]](#refnote-86)[&#8202;](#ref-back-87-0)[\[86\]](#refnote-87)

### LEET

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=SpaceX_Raptor&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: LEET")\]

In October 2021, SpaceX initiated an effort to develop a conceptual design for a new rocket engine with the goal of keeping cost below US$1,000 per ton of thrust. The project was called the 1337 engine, to be pronounced "LEET" (after a [coding meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leet "Leet")).[&#8202;](#ref-back-Isaacson2023-1)[\[73\]](#refnote-Isaacson2023)

Although the initial design effort was halted in late 2021, the project helped define an ideal engine, and likely generated ideas that were incorporated into Raptor 3. Musk stated then that "We can't make life multiplanetary with Raptor, as it is way too expensive, but Raptor is needed to tide us over until 1337 is ready."[&#8202;](#ref-back-Isaacson2023-2)[\[73\]](#refnote-Isaacson2023)

In June 2024, the LEET concept was clarified as a total tearup of the Raptor 3 design, with Musk stating that SpaceX will "probably do that at some point. ... \[Raptor 3\] looks like a LEET engine, but its way more expensive because it still has [printed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_printing "3D printing") parts, for example."[&#8202;](#ref-back-tea20240622-1)[\[78\]](#refnote-tea20240622)
