# How to Decide if Your Country Is Rich

How to decide if the world has become a better place?

We can ask about changes in poverty, literacy, democracy, vaccination, and child mortality rates.

Our _World in Data_ had some of the answers:

![the richest countries](https://econlife.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/SCR-20240708-tbdd.png)

### Richest Countries

Meanwhile, also asking how we are doing, the _Economist_ identified the world’s richest nations. Seemingly simple, the question requires deciding which variables to use among a slew of possibilities.

_The Economist’s_ criteria focused on three ways to measure income. They looked at GDP per person, dollar income per hour worked, and income adjusted for local prices:

![richest countries](https://econlife.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/SCR-20240708-tjgn.png)

As you can see, for all three measures, Qatar is #3. Meanwhile, Luxembourg had two #1 slots with Norway at the top for income from hours worked. Then, with a divided #2 rank, Switzerland was second for [per capita](https://econlife.com/glossary/per-capita/) GDP, Singapore for purchasing power, and Luxembourg, hours worked.

So, where are we?

### Our Bottom Line: Economic Systems

Whether looking at the three basic economic systems–the [market](https://econlife.com/glossary/market/), central planning, tradition– or their permutations, we can revert to the three questions that all economies have to answer. They need to determine _what_ [goods](https://econlife.com/glossary/goods/) and [services](https://econlife.com/glossary/services/) will be produced, _how_ they will be made, and _who_ will receive the income for making them.

Indeed, we can better understand who is richest by returning to the basics– to different economic systems and their ability to create and spread wealth.
