# Revisiting the Federal School Choice Agenda

![Author](https://www.aei.org/wp-content/themes/aei-2025/assets/img/icons/avatar.svg?x57549)by Anthony de Nicola, Peter Murphy

![Date](https://www.aei.org/wp-content/themes/aei-2025/assets/img/icons/calendar.svg?x57549)March 24, 2025

_This report was updated on March 6, 2026._

**Key Points**

- President Donald Trump’s reelection and supportive Republican majorities in both chambers of Congress present the best opportunity to enact federal private school choice legislation.
- The Educational Choice for Children Act (ECCA) is the only near-term means to expand school choice in all 50 states.
- The ECCA uses the tax code to encourage philanthropic support to expand educational freedom while avoiding increased federal spending, bureaucracy, and mandates.

While driving innovation in education through new ideas and policies is essential, we believe there is equal value in reflecting on past proposals, assessing their relevance in today’s context, and identifying opportunities for improvement. With that in mind, we followed up with Peter Mur­phy, coauthor of the 2022 “Sketching a New Conservative Education Agenda” report _A Federal School Choice Agenda_. [1](#footnote-section) To frame the discussion, we asked the authors a series of questions, and those questions and their responses follow.

_Why is now the time to revisit the federal school choice agenda?_

It has been said by many in this century, including President Donald Trump, that school choice is the civil rights issue of our time. [2](#footnote-section)

Since 2020, more than two dozen states have passed private school choice laws or expanded existing ones, nearly doubling the number of students participating in school choice programs to more than one million in 2024. [3](#footnote-section) However, of the 49.5 million students in public elementary or secondary schools, 49 percent reside in one of the 17 states without a state private school choice program. [4](#footnote-section) That is likely because these legislatures did not enact such a law, courts struck it down, or teachers unions overwhelmingly financed its defeat in a referendum.

President Trump’s reelection and supportive Republican majorities in both chambers of Congress present the best opportunity to enact federal private school choice legislation.

_What is the best way to pass federal school choice, and how would it work?_

The Educational Choice for Children Act (ECCA) is the only near-term means to expand school choice to children in all 50 states. The ECCA builds on previous federal legislation sponsored by former Senator (and current Secretary of State) Marco Rubio and President Trump’s first-term education secretary, Betsy DeVos, to fulfill President Trump’s 2016 campaign plan to expand school choice. Like the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which overcame certain states’ political opposition to equality under the law, this school choice legislation would transcend state-level resistance to create or expand K–12 educational options that families desire in lieu of their children being consigned to failing schools based on their zip code.

The ECCA would create a federal income tax credit for individuals and corporations that donate to scholarship-granting organizations (SGOs). The SGOs, in turn, would distribute scholarships to students to access independent and religious schools of their parents’ choosing, including homeschools and SGO-approved supplemental services. Currently, 21 states already use this scholarship tax-credit donation incentive to varying degrees. [5](#footnote-section)

_How would the ECCA be passed into law, and what’s its current status?_

The legislative path to enacting the ECCA is to pass it through budget reconciliation, a process for measures related to taxes, spending, or the debt limit. The budget reconciliation process is restricted to provisions that directly affect federal revenue or expenditures. [6](#footnote-section) Since the ECCA introduces a new tax credit, thereby influencing federal tax and revenue, it is eligible to be included in reconciliation, which requires a simple majority in the Senate to pass.

Given the narrow Republican majority in both chambers, Republicans should have the votes needed to advance the ECCA through reconciliation. However, school choice supporters, especially parents, should take nothing for granted and must be vigilant in advocating with Republican and Democratic members of Congress to pass the ECCA as part of reconciliation.

The ECCA was reintroduced in January 2025 by Representative Adrian Smith (R-NE) and Senator Bill Cassidy (R-LA). [7](#footnote-section) Both the House and Senate bills propose up to $10 billion in annual tax credits for corporations and individuals that make charitable donations to SGOs, which these lead sponsors project will benefit an estimated two million students. [8](#footnote-section) While it’s ideal for the ECCA to be passed through reconciliation, the reconciliation process for 2025 remains uncertain.

_How would the ECCA be administered?_

The ECCA directs the US Treasury Department to develop a system that tracks how donors use tax credits on a first-come, first-served basis and provides information regarding the availability of tax credits to donors, similar to already-existing “clean vehicle” tax credits. [9](#footnote-section) Such tracking systems are also already used in individual state tax-credit programs. For example, Georgia administers its Qualified Education Expense Tax Credit through a web portal that tracks donation activity and requires participating scholarship organizations and taxpayer-donors to register. Pennsylvania administers a similar process for its Educational Improvement Tax Credit. [10](#footnote-section)

Because the ECCA establishes a federal tax credit, the IRS is the arm of the Treasury Department best equipped to create a system to administer the credits. Since private donations, not government funding, finance the scholarships, there is little to no risk of the federal government attaching strings to the funding. Moreover, the ECCA would not be subject to annual appropriation battles in Congress or involvement from the Department of Education, which has no role in the legislation.

_How would the ECCA interplay with existing state private school choice programs_?

The ECCA would add to existing benefits in the 33 states that have private school choice (15 of which have universal school choice), meaning choice would be available to more students. [11](#footnote-section) This is meaningful because even among the states that have universal school choice, some still contain limitations, including financial caps. [12](#footnote-section) For example, the Hope Scholarship program in West Virginia grants scholarships that are equivalent to the average per-pupil funding the state provides public schools, but this does not include local spending. In the 2023–24 school year, the approximate scholarship amount was $4,300, which cannot cover tuition in many schools. [13](#footnote-section) In Mississippi, state voucher programs are limited to students with disabilities. [14](#footnote-section) Kansas’s scholarship tax-credit program is only $10 million annually, and student eligibility is limited to households with income levels of up to 250 percent of the federal poverty rate, benefiting fewer than 1,500 students. [15](#footnote-section) Under the ECCA, an SGO in any state could determine scholarship amounts based on family income and existing state scholarships, education savings accounts, or voucher amounts to determine any supplemental scholarship levels from ECCA-related donations.

_What would happen in the states that have tax-credit programs similar to the ECCA?_

First, the ECCA would attract billions of dollars of extra capital each year to SGOs to fund more scholarships for more children, regardless of whether their state has a school choice law. The 100 percent federal income tax credit to the taxpayer-donor would be reduced by the state credit amount to ensure any combined federal and state tax benefits do not exceed the set donation limit.

Second, SGOs would have to navigate differences between federal and state tax-credit provisions. The ECCA requires SGOs to be not-for-profit entities, use no more than 10 percent of donations for administration, and conduct annual audits by an independent certified public accountant. SGOs otherwise have the flexibility to determine family income eligibility (excluding families with income levels above 300 percent of the regional median income), individual scholarship amounts, and the number of schools and services from which to choose. (SGOs must offer benefits to students attending more than one school and ensure not all their scholarship recipients attend the same school.) This flexibility contrasts with state provisions for tax-credit scholarships, which tend to be more restrictive.

_What would you say to ECCA critics who argue that passing a federal school choice bill could slow state-level momentum behind school choice or “reward” states that haven’t taken action on school choice?_

Under the ECCA, states could continue their efforts to expand school choice. A federal tax credit would complement existing state programs, potentially encouraging more states to take action. Moreover, as students and families experience firsthand the transformative power of school choice, they may be inspired to advocate for further state-level reforms.

Additionally, concerns about “rewarding” states that have yet to pass school choice miss the mark—this is about ensuring families and children in every state can access a quality education. The success and popularity of school choice and tax-credit scholarship programs across the country bodes well for the ECCA; more parents will be empowered to determine the best academic setting for their children in all 50 states, not just the 33 with private school choice laws on the books. [16](#footnote-section)

_Is there a risk that wealthy individuals and corporations in more-populated states could claim a disproportionate share of or all the available tax credits each year? Does the ECCA address this concern?_

The ECCA includes several mechanisms to fairly allocate tax credits. First, the ECCA provides an effective minimum allocation per state by stipulating that the Treasury Department must divide 10 percent of the annual tax-credit volume cap into equal amounts among the 50 states and DC. Resident taxpayers and businesses in each state can then make donations to SGOs. (The recipient SGO need not be based in the donor’s residential state.) If the annual volume cap is $10 billion, then $1 billion would be evenly divided 51 ways such that resident taxpayers in each state and DC would have at least $19.6 million in credits available for donations to SGOs. This provision ensures that donors in states with smaller populations, such as Vermont or Wyoming, can receive credits for donations, with any unused amount rolling into the next year’s state allotment.

Second, the ECCA contains an “escalator” provision that would increase the available tax credits by 5 percent for the following year if 90 percent of the volume cap is claimed. This provision accommodates increased parental demand for school choice.

Third, the corporate donor provision limits corporate donors’ donations to 5 percent of taxable income to keep tax credits to a single entity reasonable. Similarly, individuals’ donations are limited to 10 percent of their adjusted gross income.

_Could the government interfere in private schools’ operational autonomy or religious liberty if they enroll students who received a scholarship through the ECCA?_

The ECCA contains specific provisions that would protect the religious liberty and operational autonomy of participating SGOs, homeschool settings, and private schools, both faith based and secular.

For example, the bill would not “permit, allow, encourage, or authorize” any government entity or officer to “mandate, direct, or control any aspect” of any SGO or private or religious elementary or secondary school. The ECCA also prohibits the federal government from imposing “any conditions or requirements that would exclude” expenses such as tuition funded by a scholarship for use at a private or religious school. Furthermore, no government entity or officer “shall exclude, discriminate against, or otherwise disadvantage” any school with respect to qualified expenses (e.g., paying tuition) “based in whole or in part on the \[school’s\] religious character or affiliation, including religiously based or mission-based policies or practices.” [17](#footnote-section)

_President Trump and the Republican Party platform proposed “universal school choice.” Does the ECCA fulfill that promise, and if so, how?_

The ECCA is as close to a universal school choice bill as any that could pass in a closely divided Congress; specifically, it would be accessible to students in all 50 states, and it would be available to more than 90 percent of all households. [18](#footnote-section)

Regarding income eligibility for scholarship families, the ECCA would allow SGOs discretion on awarding scholarships for students with family income levels of up to 300 percent of the median income, as measured by the US Department of Housing and Urban Development, for each metropolitan and nonmetro statistical area to reflect variations in the cost of living nationwide. This income threshold excludes upper-income households, but it would still capture more than 90 percent of households nationwide.

The ECCA can accommodate increasing parental demand for K–12 educational options with an escalator provision to increase the amount of credits annually available. If taxpayer-donors claim 90 percent of the annual available tax credits in a given year, the ECCA contains a provision for an automatic 5 percent increase in this volume cap for the following tax year. This feature was modeled after the Florida Tax Credit Scholarship program, which has expanded annually to generate more scholarships for children. [19](#footnote-section)

_Why is the ECCA the best option for enacting federal school choice?_

Ultimately, the ECCA is about expanding K–12 educational freedom and opportunity for children in every state to respond to parental demand. [20](#footnote-section) The ECCA would create greater parental freedom and choice to determine their child’s K–12 education trajectory and future. The ECCA uses the tax code to encourage philanthropic support to expand educational freedom and is a realistic approach that avoids increased federal spending, bureaucracy, and mandates. That is a vision conservatives and all Americans should embrace.
