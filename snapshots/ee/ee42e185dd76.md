In 2006, a broad circle of parties entered into a very important agreement for the Danes and the Danish economy.

The agreement was called the “Welfare Agreement” and was mainly about a gradual raising of the Danes’ retirement age.

It is to be raised every 5 years, so that younger generations can only retire when they turn 75. This is unlikely to happen, but we will return to that.

The retirement age in Denmark is currently 67 for citizens born before December 31, 1962.

For citizens born between 1 January 1963 and 31 December 1966, it is 68 years and for citizens born between 1 January 1967 and 31 December 1970, it is 69 years.

This, at least, was until this morning.

**Work longer, pay more tax**

Folketinget today raised the retirement age to 70 years for all people born after 31 December 1970. The law will take effect from 2040.

It was approved with 81 votes in favor and 21 against.

According to the government, this ensures a large tax base for the Danish treasury, which must finance free school, free education, free hospitals and many other publicly paid welfare services.

This is the backbone of the Danish model (and Scandinavian) – and it has great political and popular support.

**Socialdemokratiet wants something else**

A year ago something happened that has made the retirement age a hot topic and will certainly be in an upcoming national election campaign.

Denmark’s largest party, the Social Democrats, announced that support for raising the retirement age to 70 is over.

It is not fair with an automatic increase, the party wants “something else”, said Prime Minister and party chair Mette Frederiksen at a press conference in August 2024.

**Angry trade union movement**

The trade union movement will agree that it is not fair.

It believes that by raising the retirement age without considering worn-out workers and the working environment that wears people down, politicians are not keeping their promises.

“This is a breach of promise beyond all limits. When politicians pull such pranks, they shouldn’t blame us ordinary wage earners afterwards. We simply won’t put up with that,” says the chairman of Denmark’s largest trade union 3F, Henning Overgaard, [to Fagbladet3F.](https://fagbladet3f.dk/3f-formand-slaar-i-bordet-er-loeftebrud-ud-over-alle-graenser)

Overgaard represents blue collar workers – and many international members.

It is his members who have the worst disadvantages of hard work. It is his members who experience that opportunities for early retirement have been reduced.

“I am glad that the prime minister will do away with the automatic increase in the retirement age. This has meant that we have finally opened the pension debate again. We can show that there is a need for more equality and security for wage earners who entered the labor market early,” says Henning Overgaard.

**Government in disarray**

Yet, Mette Frederiksen not only faces pressure from her friends in the trade union movement, who traditionally support a Social Democratic prime minister in Denmark.

Her government partners directly disagree that the welfare settlement should be terminated. It is economically irresponsible, they believe.

“We have repeatedly said that the welfare settlement from 2006 is one of the settlements that is responsible for the Danish economy being as strong as it is. We are therefore cautious about this settlement,” writes Venstre’s political spokesman, Jan E. Jørgensen, in a comment [to Berlingske](https://www.berlingske.dk/politik/nye-beregninger-nuvaerende-pensionsmodel-giver-unge-faerre-aar-paa-pension).

The third government party, Moderaterne, is also not in favor of altering the Welfare Settlement.

Right now, the government has put off further discussion of the retirement age until the autumn or winter.

**Retirement age to dominate general election**

Hardly a day goes by without political reporters asking the Social Democrats when they will talk about their plans for the retirement age.

“I think we’ll probably say more about it before the day before we vote,” says Minister of Employment Ane Halsboe-Jørgensen to Berlingske.
