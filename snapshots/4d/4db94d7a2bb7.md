**Lina Maliha Khan**[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2) (born March 3, 1989) is an American legal scholar who was the chair of the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission "Federal Trade Commission") (FTC) from 2021 to 2025. She is also an associate professor at [Columbia Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Law_School "Columbia Law School").[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3)

While a student at [Yale Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Law_School "Yale Law School"), she became known for her work in [antitrust and competition law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitrust_law_in_the_United_States "Antitrust law in the United States") after publishing the essay "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" in 2017.[&#8202;](#ref-back-amazons-antitrust-paradox-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-amazons-antitrust-paradox) President [Joe Biden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biden "Joe Biden") nominated her to the FTC in March 2021, and after her confirmation she became the youngest FTC chair ever that June.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FTC_Appointment-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-FTC_Appointment)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:3-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-:3) Khan was a co-chair of [Zohran Mamdani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohran_Mamdani "Zohran Mamdani")'s mayoral transitional team, and was appointed by Mamdani as the chair of the [New York City Economic Development Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Economic_Development_Corporation "New York City Economic Development Corporation").

## Early life and education

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Early life and education")\]

Khan was born on March 3, 1989, in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London"), to [Pakistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistanis "Pakistanis") immigrant parents.[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-7)[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYT_7_September_20182-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-NYT_7_September_20182)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Khan-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-Khan) She grew up in [Golders Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golders_Green "Golders Green") in the [London Borough of Barnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Barnet "London Borough of Barnet"). Her parents, a management consultant and an employee of [Thomson Reuters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomson_Reuters "Thomson Reuters"), moved to the United States when she was 11 years old. The family settled in [Mamaroneck, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamaroneck,_New_York "Mamaroneck, New York"), where she and her two siblings attended public school.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212)[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)

At [Mamaroneck High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamaroneck_High_School "Mamaroneck High School"), Khan was involved in the student newspaper.[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12) After high school, she studied [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science "Political science") at [Williams College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williams_College "Williams College") in [Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts "Massachusetts"). She spent her junior year studying at [Exeter College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_College,_Oxford "Exeter College, Oxford") through the [Williams-Exeter Programme at Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williams-Exeter_Programme_at_Oxford_(WEPO) "Williams-Exeter Programme at Oxford (WEPO)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13) She was the editor of the Williams College student newspaper and wrote her senior thesis on [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt "Hannah Arendt"). She graduated in 2010 with a Bachelor of Arts.

## Advocacy and academic career

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Advocacy and academic career")\]

From 2010 to 2014, Khan worked at the [New America Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_America_(organization) "New America (organization)"), where she engaged in anti-monopoly research and writing for [Barry Lynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_C._Lynn "Barry C. Lynn") at the Open Markets Program.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-1)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212) Lynn was looking for a researcher without a background in economics, and he began critiquing market consolidation with Khan's help.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-2)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212)

As a result of her work at the Open Markets Institute, Khan was offered a reporting position at _[The Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal "The Wall Street Journal")_, where she would have covered commodities. During the same period, she was offered admission into [Yale Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Law_School "Yale Law School"). Describing it as "a real 'choose the path' moment", she ultimately chose to enroll at Yale.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-3)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212) She was a submissions editor for the _[Yale Journal on Regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Journal_on_Regulation "Yale Journal on Regulation")_. She graduated from Yale in 2017 with a [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor "Juris Doctor") degree.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYT_7_September_20182-1)[\[8\]](#refnote-NYT_7_September_20182)[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14)

### "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox"

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: \"Amazon's Antitrust Paradox\"")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Lina_Khan_2016.jpg/250px-Lina_Khan_2016.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lina_Khan_2016.jpg) Khan in 2016, speaking on a panel about [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_(company) "Amazon (company)") and [antitrust law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_antitrust_law "United States antitrust law")

In 2017, during her third year at Yale Law School, the _[Yale Law Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Law_Journal "Yale Law Journal")_ published Khan's student article "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox".[&#8202;](#ref-back-amazons-antitrust-paradox2-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-amazons-antitrust-paradox2) The article made a significant impact in American legal and business circles, and _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ described it as "reframing decades of monopoly law".[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYT_7_September_20182-2)[\[8\]](#refnote-NYT_7_September_20182)

In the article, Khan argued that the current American antitrust law framework, which focuses on keeping consumer prices down, cannot account for the anticompetitive effects of platform-based business models such as that of [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_(company) "Amazon (company)"). The title of her piece was a reference to [Robert Bork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bork "Robert Bork")'s 1978 book _[The Antitrust Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Antitrust_Paradox "The Antitrust Paradox")_, which established the consumer-welfare standard that she critiqued.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-4)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212) She proposed alternative frameworks for antitrust policy, including "restoring traditional antitrust and competition policy principles or applying [common carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_carrier "Common carrier") obligations and duties."[&#8202;](#ref-back-amazons-antitrust-paradox2-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-amazons-antitrust-paradox2)[&#8202;](#ref-back-NewYorker20212-5)[\[10\]](#refnote-NewYorker20212)

For "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox", Khan won the Antitrust Writing Award for "Best Academic Unilateral Conduct Article" in 2018,[&#8202;](#ref-back-:02-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-:02) the Israel H. Peres Prize by Yale Law School,[&#8202;](#ref-back-:02-1)[\[16\]](#refnote-:02) and the Michael Egger Prize from the _Yale Law Journal_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:02-2)[\[16\]](#refnote-:02)

#### Reception

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Reception")\]

The article was met with both acclaim and criticism. As of September 2018, it had received 146,255 hits, "a runaway best-seller in the world of legal treatises," according to _The New York Times_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYT_7_September_20182-3)[\[8\]](#refnote-NYT_7_September_20182) [Makan Delrahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makan_Delrahim "Makan Delrahim"), then serving as [Assistant Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Assistant_Attorney_General "United States Assistant Attorney General") for the [Antitrust Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitrust_Division "Antitrust Division") under [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), praised Khan for her "fresh thinking on how our legal tools apply to new digital platforms".[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17)

[Joshua Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_D._Wright "Joshua D. Wright"), who served on the FTC from 2013 to 2015, and while representing companies including Amazon as a paid consultant[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18), derided Khan's work as " [hipster antitrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipster_Antitrust "Hipster Antitrust")" and argued it "reveal\[ed\] a profound lack of understanding of the consumer welfare model and the [rule of reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_reason "Rule of reason") framework."[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19) [Herbert Hovenkamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Hovenkamp "Herbert Hovenkamp") wrote that Khan's claims were "technically undisciplined, untestable, and even incoherent", and that her work "never explains how a nonmanufacturing retailer such as Amazon could ever recover its investment in below cost pricing by later raising prices, and even disputes that raising prices to higher levels ever needs to be a part of the strategy, thus indicating that it is confusing predation with investment."[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-20)

### Open Markets Institute and Columbia Law School

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Open Markets Institute and Columbia Law School")\]

After graduating from law school, Khan worked as legal director at the Open Markets Institute (OMI). The institute split from [New America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_America_(organization) "New America (organization)") after Khan and her team criticized Google's market power, prompting pressure from Google, a funder of New America.[&#8202;](#ref-back-The_Atlantic_12_June_20182-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-The_Atlantic_12_June_20182) During her time at OMI, Khan met with Senator [Elizabeth Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren "Elizabeth Warren") to discuss anti-monopolistic policy ideas.[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22)

Initially planning to clerk for [Judge Stephen Reinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Reinhardt "Stephen Reinhardt") on the [Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Ninth_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit"), Khan joined [Columbia Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Law_School "Columbia Law School") as an academic fellow, where she pursued research and scholarship on antitrust law and competition policy, especially relating to digital platforms.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:02-3)[\[16\]](#refnote-:02)[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23) She published "The Separation of Platforms and Commerce" in the _[Columbia Law Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Law_Review "Columbia Law Review")_, making the case for structural separations that prohibit dominant intermediaries from entering lines of business that place them in direct competition with the businesses dependent on their networks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24) In July 2020, Khan joined the school's faculty as an associate professor of law.[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-25)

Khan has described herself as belonging to the [New Brandeis movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Brandeis_movement "New Brandeis movement"), a political movement that seeks a revival in antitrust enforcement.[&#8202;](#ref-back-khan2018-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-khan2018)

### Early government service

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Early government service")\]

In 2018, Khan worked as a legal fellow at the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission "Federal Trade Commission") in the office of Commissioner [Rohit Chopra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohit_Chopra "Rohit Chopra").[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-27) In 2019, she began serving as counsel to the House Judiciary Committee's [Subcommittee on Antitrust, Commercial, and Administrative Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Judiciary_Subcommittee_on_Antitrust,_Commercial_and_Administrative_Law "United States House Judiciary Subcommittee on Antitrust, Commercial and Administrative Law"), where she led the congressional investigation into digital markets.[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28)

## Chair of the FTC

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Chair of the FTC")\]

See also: [Federal Trade Commission §Biden administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission#Biden_administration "Federal Trade Commission")

On March 22, 2021, [Joe Biden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biden "Joe Biden") announced that he was nominating Khan to be a commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission to a term ending September 26, 2024.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FTC_Appointment-1)[\[5\]](#refnote-FTC_Appointment)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-:1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:2-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-:2) On June 15, 2021, her nomination was confirmed by the [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_senate "US senate") by a vote of 69 to 28.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FTC_Appointment-2)[\[5\]](#refnote-FTC_Appointment)[&#8202;](#ref-back-31-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-31) Khan was confirmed with bipartisan support, mainly attributed to her "influential anti-Amazon views" being widely reflected in Congress.[&#8202;](#ref-back-32-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-32) Biden then appointed her chairperson of the FTC.[&#8202;](#ref-back-33-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-33) Upon taking office, Khan became the third [Asian American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Americans "Asian Americans") to serve on the FTC, after [Dennis Yao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Yao "Dennis Yao") (who served from 1991 to 1994) and her former boss [Rohit Chopra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohit_Chopra "Rohit Chopra") (who served from 2018 to 2021).[&#8202;](#ref-back-34-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-34)

### Actions and policies

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Actions and policies")\]

In April 2024, the FTC issued a landmark regulation that banned the enforcement of existing [non-compete agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-compete_clause "Non-compete clause") on employees other than senior executives, and prohibited new non-compete agreements against all categories of employees. The non-compete regulation was struck down in August 2024 by a federal court, which ruled that it was an overreach of statutory authority on the part of the FTC to issue such a regulation and that the regulation was arbitrary and capricious.[&#8202;](#ref-back-WSJaug20-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-WSJaug20)

Khan has been outspoken about potential perils from business monopolies,[&#8202;](#ref-back-36-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-36) and also expanding anti-trust regulation and enforcement,[&#8202;](#ref-back-37-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-37) among merger filings, of which only two percent receive added scrutiny.[&#8202;](#ref-back-38-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-38) During Khan's tenure, the FTC voted unanimously in 2021 to enforce the [right to repair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_repair "Right to repair") as policy and to consider action against companies that limit the type of repair work that can be done at independent repair shops;[&#8202;](#ref-back-39-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-39) has pursued lawsuits against companies to lower drug prices,[&#8202;](#ref-back-40-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-40) including for insulin and inhalers;[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-41)[&#8202;](#ref-back-42-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-42) and adopted the " [click to cancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Click_to_cancel "Click to cancel")" rule in 2024 for consumers to efficiently end subscription services.[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-43)

During her tenure, the FTC had a mixed record in its attempts to block mergers and acquisitions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Khanservative-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-Khanservative)[&#8202;](#ref-back-CNN-heralded-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-CNN-heralded) Through the first half of her tenure, the FTC lost multiple high-profile merger challenges resolved in court, including the [Microsoft-Activision Blizzard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquisition_of_Activision_Blizzard_by_Microsoft "Acquisition of Activision Blizzard by Microsoft") merger and Meta's acquisition of [Within](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Within_(company) "Within (company)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-wsjJuly12-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-wsjJuly12)[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-47) In December 2023, the FTC won its first major challenge in court by blocking [Illumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illumina,_Inc. "Illumina, Inc.")'s acquisition of [Grail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grail_(company) "Grail (company)") after a 3-year legal battle.[&#8202;](#ref-back-48-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-48) In 2024, the FTC won two major merger challenges in court, blocking [Tapestry, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapestry,_Inc. "Tapestry, Inc.")'s attempted acquisition of [Capri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capri_Holdings "Capri Holdings") in November and [Kroger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kroger "Kroger")'s attempted acquisition of [Albertsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertsons "Albertsons") in December.[&#8202;](#ref-back-49-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-49)[&#8202;](#ref-back-50-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-50)

### Accusations of bias

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Accusations of bias")\]

Following her appointment as chairperson, both [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_(company) "Amazon (company)") and [Meta Platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta_Platforms "Meta Platforms") filed petitions with the FTC seeking her [recusal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recusal "Recusal") from investigations of the companies, suggesting that her past criticism of the companies left her unable to be impartial.[&#8202;](#ref-back-51-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-51)[&#8202;](#ref-back-52-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-52) According to legal scholar [Eleanor Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Fox "Eleanor Fox"), the standard for recusal is very high and unlikely to be met for Khan.[&#8202;](#ref-back-53-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-53) Senator [Elizabeth Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren "Elizabeth Warren") and other supporters of Khan argued that the recusal demands amount to an attempt by these companies to intimidate Khan in order to curtail regulatory scrutiny.[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-54) According to leaked documents, the FTC's Designated Agency Ethics Official (DAEO), Lorielle Pankey, did not believe Khan had violated any ethical standards,[&#8202;](#ref-back-55-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-55) but still recommended that she recuse herself from the case with [Meta Platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta_Platforms "Meta Platforms") to avoid the appearance of bias; this recommendation was rejected by Khan and the FTC.[&#8202;](#ref-back-56-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-56) Pankey was later revealed to have owned Meta stock at that time, prompting concerns about Pankey's own conduct.[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-57) In response, Khan and the FTC released a unanimous statement in support of Pankey.[&#8202;](#ref-back-58-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-58) Earlier in February 2023, Republican FTC Commissioner [Christine Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_S._Wilson "Christine S. Wilson") announced her plan to resign from the agency citing her opposition to Khan's leadership, including her refusal to recuse from the FTC lawsuit against Meta.[&#8202;](#ref-back-59-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-59)

### Public debate over policies and role

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Public debate over policies and role")\]

In July 2023, Republicans had Khan testify before the [House of Representatives Judiciary Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Committee_on_the_Judiciary "United States House Committee on the Judiciary").[&#8202;](#ref-back-60-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-60)[&#8202;](#ref-back-61-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-61) Democrats on the committee defended Khan and the actions of the agency, arguing that she was taking steps that protected user privacy.\[ _[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_\][&#8202;](#ref-back-62-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-62)[&#8202;](#ref-back-63-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-63)[&#8202;](#ref-back-64-0)[\[64\]](#refnote-64)

[Ken Buck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Buck "Ken Buck"), a [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States) "Republican Party (United States)"), praised Khan's actions, and highlighted members of [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress "United States Congress") using insider knowledge for stock trading, and noted the disparity between permitting such conduct, and seeking to limit Khan because she "wrote a law review article."[&#8202;](#ref-back-65-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-65)[&#8202;](#ref-back-66-0)[\[66\]](#refnote-66) [Matt Gaetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Gaetz "Matt Gaetz"), a Republican, praised Khan's efforts, citing several offenses by the [Ring Doorbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_Doorbell "Ring Doorbell") company, and asked Khan to continue investigating them. Similarly, Gaetz noted that [Kochava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochava "Kochava") had been selling customer's personal data, and asked Khan to continue investigating and to let Congress know what new laws might be needed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-67-0)[\[67\]](#refnote-67)

Khan was noted by both critics and supporters for her aggressive approach to regulation as FTC chair, invoking novel arguments and pursuing non-traditional cases but also risking more losses in court.[&#8202;](#ref-back-CNN-heralded-1)[\[45\]](#refnote-CNN-heralded)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Sonnenfeld-0)[\[68\]](#refnote-Sonnenfeld)[&#8202;](#ref-back-69-0)[\[69\]](#refnote-69) Khan and the FTC argued that the increase in agency action resulted in an additional deterrence effect, leading to some businesses dropping attempted mergers and acquisitions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Guardian-prevail-0)[\[70\]](#refnote-Guardian-prevail) Among others, [Lockheed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin "Lockheed Martin")'s attempted acquisition of [Aerojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerojet "Aerojet"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-71-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-71) as well as [Sanofi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanofi "Sanofi")'s attempted acquisition of Maze Therapeutics,[&#8202;](#ref-back-72-0)[\[72\]](#refnote-72) were both withdrawn following FTC scrutiny. Khan and her supporters have pointed to these abandoned deals as enforcement victories outside of a judicial environment.[&#8202;](#ref-back-CNN-heralded-2)[\[45\]](#refnote-CNN-heralded)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Sonnenfeld-1)[\[68\]](#refnote-Sonnenfeld) During her tenure, House Republicans accused Khan of bringing weak cases in order to push Congress to expand antitrust enforcement authority.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Bork-0)[\[73\]](#refnote-Bork) In a 2023 Congressional hearing, Khan denied accusations that she brought cases that she expected to lose but acknowledged risks in her aggressive approach in opposing mergers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-74-0)[\[74\]](#refnote-74)

### Agency morale under Khan

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Agency morale under Khan")\]

In the first two years of Khans tenure, FTC employees reported declines in metrics such as employee satisfaction and faith in leadership, according to data from the 2021 and 2022 Federal Employee Viewpoint Surveys conducted by the [Office of Personnel Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Personnel_Management "Office of Personnel Management"). Prior to Khan's tenure, the FTC consistently ranked at the top of federal agencies in workplace rankings in both Democratic and Republican administrations. 94.3% of FTC staff had favorable views of senior leadership in 2020, declining to 51.7% in 2021, and to 46.6% in 2022. After Khan's appointment, the FTC switched from ranking first in favorable views of senior leadership among federal agencies to first in unfavorable views.[&#8202;](#ref-back-sinkingjuly13-0)[\[75\]](#refnote-sinkingjuly13)[&#8202;](#ref-back-agencyapril27-0)[\[76\]](#refnote-agencyapril27)[&#8202;](#ref-back-77-0)[\[77\]](#refnote-77)[&#8202;](#ref-back-nymag1-0)[\[78\]](#refnote-nymag1)

FTC officials have attributed the decline in employee satisfaction under Khan to a lack of a clear strategy on achieving objectives, a lack of knowledge on agency operations, and disrespect and sidelining of career staff. One of Khan's first acts at the FTC, a ban on public speaking for FTC staff, was widely unpopular with employees and retracted in 2022 with an apology.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nymag1-1)[\[78\]](#refnote-nymag1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-79-0)[\[79\]](#refnote-79)[&#8202;](#ref-back-80-0)[\[80\]](#refnote-80) Khan said it was a priority to improve staff morale following the initial Federal Employee Viewpoint Survey results during congressional testimony in July, 2023.[&#8202;](#ref-back-81-0)[\[81\]](#refnote-81) Improvements in employee satisfaction were measured in 2023, reclaiming over half of its decline in morale since 2021.[&#8202;](#ref-back-82-0)[\[82\]](#refnote-82)

### Termination

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Termination")\]

Despite early bipartisan support for her continuing in her role as chairman under President [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), including positive comments in 2024 from Republican vice presidential candidate [JD Vance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JD_Vance "JD Vance") and former Trump advisor [Steve Bannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Bannon "Steve Bannon"), Khan was replaced by [Andrew N. Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_N._Ferguson "Andrew N. Ferguson") in 2025.[&#8202;](#ref-back-83-0)[\[83\]](#refnote-83)[&#8202;](#ref-back-84-0)[\[84\]](#refnote-84)

## Reputation and influence

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lina_Khan&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Reputation and influence")\]

In 2018, _[Politico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politico "Politico")_ described Khan as "a leader of a new school of antitrust thought" as part of its " _Politico_ 50" list of influential thinkers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:02-4)[\[16\]](#refnote-:02) _[New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(magazine) "New York (magazine)")_ magazine said she was "indisputably the most powerful figure in the anti-monopoly vanguard".[&#8202;](#ref-back-91-0)[\[91\]](#refnote-91) She was also listed as one of _[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy "Foreign Policy")_'s "Global Thinkers",[&#8202;](#ref-back-92-0)[\[92\]](#refnote-92) _[Prospect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_(magazine) "Prospect (magazine)")_'s "Top 50 Thinkers",[&#8202;](#ref-back-93-0)[\[93\]](#refnote-93) _[Wired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wired_(magazine) "Wired (magazine)")_'s WIRED25,[&#8202;](#ref-back-Wired-0)[\[94\]](#refnote-Wired) the _[National Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Journal "National Journal")_'s 50,[&#8202;](#ref-back-95-0)[\[95\]](#refnote-95) _[Washingtonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washingtonian_(magazine) "Washingtonian (magazine)")_'s list of most influential women,[&#8202;](#ref-back-96-0)[\[96\]](#refnote-96) and _[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")_'s " [Next Generation Leaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Next_Generation_Leaders "Time Next Generation Leaders")".[&#8202;](#ref-back-Semuels-0)[\[97\]](#refnote-Semuels)

Khan's practices at the FTC have been met with bipartisan praise as well as some criticism. _The Wall Street Journal_ published at least 124 critical pieces of Khan during her antitrust tenure.[&#8202;](#ref-back-98-0)[\[98\]](#refnote-98) Ankush Khadori of _New York_ magazine wrote in December 2023 that failed lawsuits against Meta and [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft") led to reduced morale and high attrition among FTC employees;[&#8202;](#ref-back-nymag1-2)[\[78\]](#refnote-nymag1) however, Khan has gained praise for her tactics from members of both the Democratic and Republican parties. In 2024 [JD Vance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JD_Vance "JD Vance"), then a senator from Ohio and current vice president, cited Khan's campaigns against large technology companies as a success for antitrust efforts in the US, beliefs echoed by former Democratic representative [David Cicilline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cicilline "David Cicilline"), who expressed his confidence that Khan would ultimately prevail against large companies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-99-0)[\[99\]](#refnote-99)[&#8202;](#ref-back-100-0)[\[100\]](#refnote-100) Former colleague [Matt Stoller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Stoller "Matt Stoller") described Khan as the best chair the FTC has ever had.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Guardian-prevail-1)[\[70\]](#refnote-Guardian-prevail)
