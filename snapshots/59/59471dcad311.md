**Valentinian III** (born July 2, 419, Ravenna \[Italy\]—died March 16, 455, Rome) was a Roman emperor from 425 to 455. At no time in his long reign were the affairs of state personally managed by Valentinian. He was the son of the patrician Flavius Constantius (who ruled as [Constantius III](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Constantius-III) in 421) and [Galla Placidia](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Aelia-Galla-Placidia). When his uncle, the emperor Honorius, died, the usurper John ruled for two years before he was deposed. Then Placidia controlled the West in her young son’s name until 437, although the powerful patrician [Flavius Aetius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Flavius-Aetius) became the effective ruler toward the end of this regency. The most important political event of these years was the landing of the [Vandals](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Vandal-Germanic-people) in Africa in 429; 10 years later they threw off the overlordship of Valentinian’s government. Valentinian was utterly unable to stop their attacks on Italy.

On October 29, 437, Valentinian married Licinia Eudoxia, the daughter of [Theodosius II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Theodosius-II) (Eastern emperor, 408–450) and Eudocia. Little is known of Valentinian in the years after his marriage. He spent his life in the pursuit of pleasure while Aetius controlled the government. In 444 Valentinian, acting in conjunction with Pope [Leo I the Great](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Saint-Leo-I), issued the famous Novel 17, which assigned to the bishop of Rome supremacy over the provincial churches. During the closing years of Valentinian’s reign, the [Huns](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hun-people) invaded Gaul (451) and northern Italy (452), but it is not known whether Valentinian personally played any significant part in meeting these crises.

As a result of false information that made him doubt Aetius’s loyalty, Valentinian murdered the great patrician with his own hands in the imperial palace at Rome on September 21, 454. The following year, two barbarians, Optila and Thraustila, who had been retainers of Aetius, avenged their master by murdering the emperor in the Campus Martius.

### How long did the Roman Empire last?

The Roman Empire was founded in 27 BCE, and in 395 AD it split into Western and Eastern empires. The Western Roman Empire fell in 475, when the German chieftain [Odoacer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer) deposed [Romulus Augustulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Romulus-Augustulus). The Eastern Empire, always richer and stronger, continued as the [Byzantine Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Byzantine-Empire) until 1453.

### Who was the first Roman emperor?

[Augustus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Augustus-Roman-emperor) was the first Roman emperor, reigning from 27 BCE to 14 CE. He was the great-nephew and adopted son of [Julius Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Julius-Caesar-Roman-ruler), who had established a dictatorship but was launching a series of political and social reforms when he was assassinated in 44 BCE. Several men sought to become his successor, and Augustus eventually came to power following military successes. His autocratic regime is known as the principate because he was the _princeps_, the first citizen, at the head of that array of outwardly revived republican institutions that alone made his autocracy palatable. With great patience, skill, and efficiency, he overhauled every aspect of Roman life and brought durable peace and prosperity to the Greco-Roman world.

### Why did the Roman Empire fall?

The fall of the Western Roman Empire was a multifaceted process, attributable to a number of causes. The empire’s ability to maintain political and military integrity was challenged by “barbarian invasions,” particularly in the 5th century CE. Internal governmental issues also played a significant role, including corruption, abuse of power, undisciplined and poorly equipped soldiers, and the alienation of urban elites from public service. In 410 Rome was sacked by the [Visigoths](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Visigoth), and in 476 the German chieftain [Odoacer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer) deposed [Romulus Augustulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Romulus-Augustulus), the last Roman emperor of the West. The East, richer and stronger, continued as the [Byzantine Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Byzantine-Empire) until 1453.

## Rise and consolidation of imperial Rome

A period of unrest and civil wars in the 1st century bce marked the transition of Rome from a republic to an empire. This period [encompassed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encompassed) the career of [Julius Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Julius-Caesar-by-Shakespeare), who eventually took full power over Rome as its dictator. After his assassination in 44 bce, the [triumvirate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/triumvirate) of [Mark Antony](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mark-Antony-Roman-triumvir), [Lepidus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Aemilius-Lepidus-Roman-statesman-died-13-12-BCE), and Octavian, Caesar’s nephew, ruled. It was not long before Octavian went to war against Antony in northern Africa, and after his victory at [Actium](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Actium-ancient-Roman-history) (31 bce) he was crowned Rome’s first [emperor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/emperor-title), [Augustus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Augustus-Roman-emperor). His reign, from 27 bce to 14 ce, was distinguished by stability and peace. (Read: [A timeline of the Roman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Timeline-of-the-Roman-Empire))

[![Marble statue of Augustus stands between two columns in a stone niche at the Roman theatre in France, with ancient masonry visible behind.](https://cdn.britannica.com/46/91346-050-6B750522/Statue-Augustus-Roman.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/46/91346-050-6B750522/Statue-Augustus-Roman.jpg)

[statue of the Roman emperor Augustus](https://cdn.britannica.com/46/91346-050-6B750522/Statue-Augustus-Roman.jpg) Statue of the Roman emperor Augustus, 1st century ce.(more)

Augustus established a form of government known as a principate, which combined some elements from the republic with the traditional powers of a monarchy. The [Senate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Senate-Roman-history) still functioned, though Augustus, as [princeps](https://www.britannica.com/topic/princeps), or first citizen, remained in control of the government..

With a mind toward maintaining the structure of power entrusted to his rule, Augustus began thinking early about who should follow him. Death played havoc with his attempts to select his successor. He had no son and his nephew [Marcellus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Claudius-Marcellus-Roman-official-died-23-BCE), his son-in-law [Agrippa](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Vipsanius-Agrippa), and his grandsons [Gaius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaius-Caesar) and Lucius each predeceased him. He eventually chose Tiberius, a scion of the ultra-aristocratic Claudia gens, and in 4 ce adopted him as his son.

[Tiberius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tiberius) (reigned 14–37) became the first successor in the [Julio-Claudian dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Julio-Claudian-dynasty) and ruled as an able administrator but cruel tyrant. His great-nephew [Caligula](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Caligula-Roman-emperor) (37–41) reigned as an absolutist, his short reign filled with reckless spending, [callous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/callous) murders, and humiliation of the Senate. [Claudius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Claudius-Roman-emperor) (41–54) centralized state finances in the imperial household, thus making rapid strides in organizing the imperial [bureaucracy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bureaucracy), but was ruthless toward the senators and [equites](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eques). [Nero](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nero-Roman-emperor) (54–68) left administration to capable advisers for a few years but then asserted himself as a vicious [despot](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/despot). He brought the [dynasty](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dynasty) to its end by being the first emperor to suffer _damnatio memoriae_: his reign was officially stricken from the record by order of the Senate.

Following a war of succession, [Vespasian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vespasian) became emperor, and the [Flavian dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Flavian-dynasty) was established. His reign (69–79) was noted for his reorganization of the army, making it more loyal and professional; for his expansion of the membership of the Senate, bringing in administrators with a sense of service; for his increase and systematization of taxation; and for his strengthening of the frontiers of the empire (though little new territory was added). The brief but popular reign of his son [Titus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Titus) (79–81) was followed by the [autocracy](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/autocracy) of [Domitian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Domitian) (81–96), Vespasian’s other son, who fought the senatorial class and instituted taxes and confiscations for costly buildings, games, and shows. A reign of terror in his final years was ended by his assassination. The Flavian dynasty, like the Julio-Claudian, ended with an emperor whose memory was officially damned.
