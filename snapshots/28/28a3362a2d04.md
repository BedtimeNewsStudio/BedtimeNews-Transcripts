The **Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act**–also known as the **Copyright Term Extension Act**, **Sonny Bono Act**, or (derisively) the **Mickey Mouse Protection Act[&#8202;](#ref-back-lessig-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-lessig)**–is a 1998 law pertaining to [copyright in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_in_the_United_States "Copyright in the United States"). It is the most recent of several acts extending [copyright terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_term "Copyright term"), the length of time a work retains its [copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright "Copyright") before entering the [public domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain "Public domain").[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)

Following the [Copyright Act of 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Act_of_1976 "Copyright Act of 1976"), copyright would last for the life of the author plus 50 years (or the last surviving author), or 75 years from publication or 100 years after creation, whichever is shorter for a work of [corporate authorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_authorship "Corporate authorship") ( [works made for hire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_made_for_hire "Works made for hire")) and anonymous and pseudonymous works. The 1976 Act also increased the renewal term for works copyrighted before 1978 that had not already entered the public domain from 28 years to 47 years, giving a total term of 75 years.[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3) The 1998 Act extended these terms to life of the author plus 70 years and for works of corporate authorship to 95 years from publication or 120 years after creation, whichever end is earlier.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4) For works published before January 1, 1978, the 1998 act extended the renewal term from 47 years to 67 years, granting a total of 95 years.

This law effectively froze the advancement date of the [public domain in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain_in_the_United_States "Public domain in the United States") for works covered by the older fixed term copyright rules. Under this Act, works made in 1923 or afterwards that were still protected by copyright in 1998 would not enter the public domain until January 1, 2019, or later. Mickey Mouse specifically, having first appeared in 1928 in _[Steamboat Willie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat_Willie "Steamboat Willie")_, entered the public domain in 2024,[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5) with other works following later in accordance with the product's date. Unlike [copyright extension legislation in the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_law_of_the_European_Union#Duration_of_protection "Copyright law of the European Union"), the Sonny Bono Act did not revive copyrights that had already expired, and therefore is not retroactive in that sense. The Act did extend the terms of protection set for works that were already copyrighted and were created before it took effect, so it is retroactive in that sense; however, works created before January 1, 1978, but not published or registered for copyright until recently, are addressed in a special section ( [17U.S.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_17_of_the_United_States_Code "Title 17 of the United States Code") [§303](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/303)) and may remain protected until the end of 2047. The Act became [Pub. L.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Congress#Public_law,_private_law,_designation "Act of Congress") [105–298 (text)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/plaw/105/public/298?link-type=html) [(PDF)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/plaw/105/public/298?link-type=pdf&.pdf) on October 27, 1998.

## Background

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Background")\]

Prior to the passage of the 1976 Copyright Act, Congress passed nine incremental extensions between 1962 and 1974 for works that were in their renewal term whose copyright began between September 19, 1906, and December 31, 1918.[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[a\]](#refnote-6)[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[b\]](#refnote-7) In the _[Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Ashcroft "Eldred v. Ashcroft")_ decision, the Supreme Court noted that these extensions "were all temporary placeholders subsumed into the systemic changes effected by the 1976 Act."[&#8202;](#ref-back-8-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-8) As a result, these works entered the public domain on January 1, following the end of the 75th calendar year after their publication.

Under the international [Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berne_Convention_for_the_Protection_of_Literary_and_Artistic_Works "Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works") of 1886, the signatory countries are required to provide copyright protection for a minimum term of the life of the author plus fifty years. Additionally, they are permitted to provide for a longer term of protection. The Berne Convention did not come into force for the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") until it was ratified on March 1, 1989, but the U.S. had previously provided for the minimum copyright term the convention required in the [Copyright Act of 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Act_of_1976 "Copyright Act of 1976").

After the United States' accession to the Berne convention, a number of copyright owners successfully [lobbied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobbying "Lobbying") the [U.S. Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_the_United_States "Congress of the United States") for another extension of the term of copyright, to provide for the same term of protection that exists in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"). Since the 1993 [Directive on harmonising the term of copyright protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_on_harmonising_the_term_of_copyright_protection "Directive on harmonising the term of copyright protection"), member states of the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union "European Union") implemented protection for a term of the author's life plus seventy years.

The act was named in memory of the late Congressman [Sonny Bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Bono "Sonny Bono"), who died nine months before the act became law: he had previously been one of twelve sponsors of a similar bill.

House members sympathetic to restaurant and bar owners, who were upset over [ASCAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCAP "ASCAP") and [BMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_Music_Incorporated "Broadcast Music Incorporated") licensing practices, almost derailed the Act. As a result, the bill was amended to include the [Fairness in Music Licensing Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_in_Music_Licensing_Act_of_1998 "Fairness in Music Licensing Act of 1998"), which exempted smaller establishments from needing a public performance license to play music.[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-9)

Both houses of the United States Congress passed the act as Public Law 105-298 with a [voice vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_vote "Voice vote").[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-10)[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-11) President [Bill Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton "Bill Clinton") signed the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998 on October 27, 1998.[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-12)

As a result of extensions, including the 1976 and 1998 extensions, a small number of [renewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_renewal_in_the_United_States "Copyright renewal in the United States") works, within a span of 40 years, entered the public domain.

| 1907 | [1976 Copyright Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Copyright_Act "1976 Copyright Act") | January 1, 1983 |

From 2019 onwards, works published in a given year enter the public domain at the end of the 95th calendar year after publication. For example, works published in 1930 entered the public domain on January 1, 2026.

## Political climate

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Political climate")\]

### Senate Report 104-315

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Senate Report 104-315")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Sonny_Bono.jpg/250px-Sonny_Bono.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sonny_Bono.jpg) US Congressman [Sonny Bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Bono "Sonny Bono"), sponsor of the Copyright Term Extension Act

The Senate Report[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-14) gave the official reasons for passing copyright extension laws and was originally written in the context of the Copyright Term Extension Act of 1995.[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-15)

> The purpose of the bill is to ensure adequate copyright protection for American works in foreign nations and the continued economic benefits of a healthy surplus [balance of trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_trade "Balance of trade") in the exploitation of copyrighted works. The bill accomplishes these goals by extending the current U.S. copyright term for an additional 21 years. Such an extension will provide significant trade benefits by substantially harmonizing U.S. copyright law to that of the European Union while ensuring fair compensation for American creators who deserve to benefit fully from the exploitation of their works. Moreover, by stimulating the creation of new works and providing enhanced economic incentives to preserve existing works, such an extension will enhance the long-term volume, vitality and accessibility of the public domain.

The authors of the report believed that extending copyright protection would help the United States by providing more protection for their works in foreign countries and by giving more incentive to digitize and preserve works since there was an exclusive right in them. The report also included minority opinions by [Herb Kohl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herb_Kohl "Herb Kohl") and [Hank Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hank_Brown "Hank Brown"), who believed that the term extensions were a financial windfall to current owners of copyrighted material at the expense of the public's use of the material.

### Support

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Support")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Mickey_Mouse.svg/250px-Mickey_Mouse.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mickey_Mouse.svg) [Disney's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company "The Walt Disney Company") support for the bill led to detractors nicknaming it the " [Mickey Mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mouse "Mickey Mouse") Protection Act".[Play video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Steamboat_Willie_(1928)_by_Walt_Disney.webm "Play video") Duration: 7 minutes and 41 seconds.7:41Subtitles available.CC_[Steamboat Willie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat_Willie "Steamboat Willie")_, one of the earliest Mickey Mouse cartoons, was delayed entry into the public domain for several decades because of the act.

Since 1990, [The Walt Disney Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company "The Walt Disney Company") had lobbied for copyright extension.[&#8202;](#ref-back-dlobby1-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-dlobby1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-dlobby2-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-dlobby2) The legislation delayed the entry into the public domain of the earliest [Mickey Mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mouse "Mickey Mouse") cartoons, leading detractors to the nickname "The Mickey Mouse Protection Act".[&#8202;](#ref-back-lessig-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-lessig)

In addition to Disney, California congresswoman [Mary Bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Bono "Mary Bono") (Sonny Bono's widow and Congressional successor), and the estate of composer [George Gershwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gershwin "George Gershwin") supported the act. Mary Bono, speaking on the floor of the [United States House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives "United States House of Representatives"), said:

> Actually, Sonny wanted the term of copyright protection to last forever. I am informed by staff that such a change would violate the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Clause "Copyright Clause"). ... As you know, there is also \[then- [MPAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Association_of_America "Motion Picture Association of America") president\] [Jack Valenti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Valenti "Jack Valenti")'s proposal for term to last forever less one day. Perhaps the Committee may look at that next Congress.[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-18)

Other parties that lobbied in favor of the Bono Act were [Time Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WarnerMedia "WarnerMedia"), [Universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Pictures "Universal Pictures"), [Viacom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viacom_(1952%E2%80%932006) "Viacom (1952–2006)"), [ASCAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCAP "ASCAP"), the major professional sports leagues ( [NFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NFL "NFL"), [NBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA "NBA"), [NHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHL "NHL"), [MLB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MLB "MLB")), and the family of slain singer [Selena Quintanilla-Pérez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selena_Quintanilla-P%C3%A9rez "Selena Quintanilla-Pérez").[&#8202;](#ref-back-dlobby1-1)[\[14\]](#refnote-dlobby1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-dlobby2-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-dlobby2)

Proponents of the Bono Act argue that it is necessary given that the [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy "Life expectancy") of humans has risen dramatically since Congress passed the original [Copyright Act of 1790](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Act_of_1790 "Copyright Act of 1790"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-Orr-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-Orr) that a difference in copyright terms between the United States and Europe would negatively affect the international operations of the [entertainment industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_industry "Entertainment industry"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-Orr-1)[\[17\]](#refnote-Orr)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Fein-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-Fein) and that some works would be created under a longer copyright that would never be created under the existing copyright. They also claim that copyrighted works are an important source of income to the US[&#8202;](#ref-back-Fein-1)[\[18\]](#refnote-Fein)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Leh-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-Leh) and that media such as [VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS "VHS"), [DVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD "DVD"), [cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television "Cable television") and [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite "Satellite") have increased the value and commercial life of movies and television series.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Fein-2)[\[18\]](#refnote-Fein)

Proponents contend that Congress has the power to establish a copyright term of any length it wants because the language "to promote the progress of science and useful arts" in the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States "Constitution of the United States") is not a substantive limitation on legislative authority, leaving the sole restriction that copyrights must only last for "limited times". However, in what respect the granted time must be limited has never been determined, thus arguably even an absurdly long, yet finite, duration would still be a valid _limited time_ according to the letter of the Constitution as long as Congress was ostensibly setting this limit to promote the progress of science and useful arts. This was one of the arguments that prevailed in the _[Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Ashcroft "Eldred v. Ashcroft")_ case, when the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the CTEA. It is also pointed out by proponents that the extension did not prevent all works from going in the public domain. They note that the 1976 Copyright Act established that [unpublished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unpublished "Unpublished") works created before 1978 would still begin entering the public domain January 1, 2003, and that the provision remained unaffected by the 1998 extension.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Scott-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-Scott) They also claim that Congress has actually increased the scope of the public domain since, for the first time, unpublished works will enter the public domain.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Scott-1)[\[20\]](#refnote-Scott)

Proponents believe that copyright encourages progress in the arts. With an extension of copyright, future artists have to create something original, rather than reuse old work. However, had the act been in place in the 1960s, it is unlikely that [Andy Warhol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Warhol "Andy Warhol") would have been able to sell or even exhibit any of his work, since it all incorporated previously copyrighted material. Proponents contend that it is more important to encourage all creators to make new works instead of just copyright holders.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Scott-2)[\[20\]](#refnote-Scott)

Proponents say that copyright better preserves intellectual property like movies, music and television shows.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Leh-1)[\[19\]](#refnote-Leh)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Scott-3)[\[20\]](#refnote-Scott) One example given is the case of the classic film _[It's a Wonderful Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_a_Wonderful_Life "It's a Wonderful Life")_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Leh-2)[\[19\]](#refnote-Leh) Before [Republic Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_Pictures "Republic Pictures") and [Spelling Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling_Entertainment "Spelling Entertainment") (who owned the motion picture rights to the short story and the music even after the film itself became public domain) began to assert their rights to the film, various local TV stations and cable networks broadcast the film endlessly. As _[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ reporter Bill Carter put it: "the film's currency was being devalued."[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-23)

Many different versions of the film were made and most if not all were in horrible condition.[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-24) After underlying rights to the film were enforced, it was given a high quality restoration that was hailed by critics. In addition, proponents note that once a work falls into the public domain there is no guarantee that the work will be more widely available or cheaper. Suggesting that quality copies of public domain works are not widely available, they argue that one reason for a lack of availability may be due to publishers' reluctance to publish a work that is in the public domain for fear that they will not be able to recoup their investment or earn enough profit.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Leh-3)[\[19\]](#refnote-Leh)

Proponents reject the idea that only works in the public domain can provide artistic inspiration. They note that opponents fail to take into account that copyright applies only to expressions of ideas and not the ideas themselves.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Scott-4)[\[20\]](#refnote-Scott) Thus artists are free to get ideas from copyrighted works as long as they do not [infringe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_infringement "Copyright infringement"). Borrowing ideas and such are common in film, TV and music even with copyrighted works (see _[scènes à faire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sc%C3%A8nes_%C3%A0_faire "Scènes à faire")_, [idea-expression divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idea-expression_divide "Idea-expression divide") and [stock character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_character "Stock character")). Works such as [parody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parody "Parody") benefit from [fair use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_use "Fair use").

Proponents also question the idea that extended copyright is "corporate welfare". They state that many opponents also have a stake in the case, claiming that those arguing against copyright term extension are mostly businesses that depend on distributing films and videos that have lost their copyright.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Leh-4)[\[19\]](#refnote-Leh)

One argument against the CTEA is focused on the [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution "First Amendment to the United States Constitution"). In _[Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper_&_Row_v._Nation_Enterprises "Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises")_, however, the court explained how a copyright "respects and adequately safeguards the [freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech") protected by the First Amendment."[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-:0) In following this approach, courts have held that copyrights are "categorically immune from challenges under the First Amendment."[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-1)[\[23\]](#refnote-:0)

### Opposition

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Opposition")\]

Critics of the CTEA argue that it was never the original intention for copyright protection to be extended in the United States. Attorney Jenny L. Dixon mentions that "the United States has always viewed copyright primarily as a vehicle for achieving social benefit based on the belief that encouragement of individual effort by personal gain is the best way to advance the public welfare;"[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1) however, "the U.S. does not consider copyright as a 'natural right.'"[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-1)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1) Dixon continues that with increased extensions on copyright protections, authors receive the benefits, while the public have more difficulty accessing these works, weakening public domain.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-2)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1) One such extension Dixon mentions is the protection of a copyrighted work for the author's life followed by two generations, which opponents argue that there is no legislation nor intention for this copyright protection.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-3)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1) "These constitutionally-grounded arguments 'for limitations on proprietary rights' are being rejected time and time again."[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-4)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1)

[Dennis S. Karjala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_S._Karjala "Dennis S. Karjala"), a law professor, led an effort to try to prevent the CTEA from being passed. He testified before the Committees on the Judiciary arguing "that extending the term of copyright protection would impose substantial costs on the United States general public without supplying any public benefit. The extension bills represent a fundamental departure from the United States philosophy that intellectual property legislation serve a public purpose."[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-27)

An editorial in _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ argued against the copyright extension on February 21, 1998. The article stated "When Senator Hatch laments that [George Gershwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gershwin "George Gershwin")'s _[Rhapsody in Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhapsody_in_Blue "Rhapsody in Blue")_ will soon 'fall into the public domain,' he makes the public domain sound like a dark abyss where songs go, never to be heard again. In fact, when a work enters the public domain it means the public can afford to use it freely, to give it new currency."[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-28)

Opponents of the Bono Act consider the legislation to be [corporate welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_welfare "Corporate welfare") and have tried (but failed) to have it declared [unconstitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconstitutional "Unconstitutional") in the process ( _[Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Ashcroft "Eldred v. Ashcroft")_), claiming that such an act is not "necessary and proper" to accomplishing the Constitution's stated purpose of "promot\[&#8202;](#ref-back-29-0)[ing\] the progress of science and useful arts".[\[27\]](#refnote-29)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Encyc-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-Encyc) They argue that most works bring most of the profits during the first few years and are pushed off the market by the publishers thereafter. Thus there is little economic incentive in extending the terms of copyrights except for the few owners of franchises that are wildly successful, such as Disney. They also point out that the [Tenth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution "Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution") can be construed as placing limits on the powers that Congress can gain from a treaty. More directly, they see two successive terms of approximately 20 years each (the [Copyright Act of 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Act_of_1976 "Copyright Act of 1976") and the Bono Act) as the beginning of a "slippery slope" toward a [perpetual copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_copyright "Perpetual copyright") term that nullifies the intended effect and violates the spirit of the "for limited times" language of the [United States Constitution, Article I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution/Article_One "United States Constitution/Article One"), section 8, clause 8.[&#8202;](#ref-back-United_States_Constitution-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-United_States_Constitution)

Some opponents have questioned the proponents' life expectancy argument, making the comparison between the growth of copyright terms and the term of patents in relation to the growth of life expectancies. Life expectancies have risen from about 35 years in 1800[&#8202;](#ref-back-Life_Expectancies_in_1800s-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-Life_Expectancies_in_1800s) to 77.6 years in 2002.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Life_Expectancies_in_2000s-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-Life_Expectancies_in_2000s) While copyright terms have increased threefold, from only 28 years total (under the [Copyright Act of 1790](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Act_of_1790 "Copyright Act of 1790")), life expectancies have roughly doubled. Moreover, life expectancy statistics are skewed due to historically high infant mortality rates. Correcting for infant mortality, life expectancy has only increased by fifteen years between 1850 and 2000.[&#8202;](#ref-back-34-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-34)
In addition, copyright terms have increased significantly since the 1790 act, but [patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent "Patent") terms have not been extended in parallel, with 20-year terms of protection remaining the (presumably under the laws) adequate compensation for innovation in a technical field.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Patent_Laws-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-Patent_Laws)
Seventeen prominent economists and [libertarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian_perspectives_on_intellectual_property "Libertarian perspectives on intellectual property"), including [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nobel_Memorial_Prize_laureates_in_Economics "List of Nobel Memorial Prize laureates in Economics") laureates ( [George Akerlof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_A._Akerlof "George A. Akerlof"), [Kenneth Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Arrow "Kenneth Arrow"), [James Buchanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_M._Buchanan "James M. Buchanan"), [Ronald Coase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Coase "Ronald Coase"), and [Milton Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman "Milton Friedman")), submitted an [amicus brief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicus_curiae "Amicus curiae") opposing the bill when it was challenged in court. They argued that the [discounted present value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_present_value "Discounted present value") of the extension was only a 1% increase for newly created works, while the increase in [transaction costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost "Transaction cost") created by extending the terms of old works would be very large and without any [marginal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalism "Marginalism") benefit.[&#8202;](#ref-back-36-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-36) According to [Lawrence Lessig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Lessig "Lawrence Lessig"), when asked to sign the brief, Friedman had originally insisted that it include "the word 'no-brainer' in it somewhere," but still agreed to sign it even though his condition was not met.[&#8202;](#ref-back-37-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-37)

Another argument against the CTEA is focused on the [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution "First Amendment to the United States Constitution") "because of the prospective and retrospective application of the CTEA."[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-2)[\[23\]](#refnote-:0) The plaintiffs in _[Eldred v. Reno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Reno "Eldred v. Reno")_ believed that "the CTEA failed to sustain the intermediate level of scrutiny test afforded by the First Amendment because the government did not have an 'important' interest to justify withholding speech."[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-3)[\[23\]](#refnote-:0)

Opponents also argue that the Act encourages "offshore production," in which [derivative works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_work "Derivative work") could be created outside the United States in areas where copyright would have expired, but US law would prohibit these works from being shown to US residents. For example, a cartoon of Mickey Mouse playing with a computer could be legally created in Russia, but the cartoon would be refused admission for importation by US Customs due to infringing US copyrights.[&#8202;](#ref-back-38-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-38)

Opponents identify another possible harm from copyright extension: loss of productive value of private collections of copyrighted works. A person who collected copyrighted works that would soon "go out of copyright", intending to re-release them on copyright expiration, lost the use of their capital expenditures for an additional 20 years when the Bono Act passed. This is part of the underlying argument in _[Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Ashcroft "Eldred v. Ashcroft")_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-39-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-39) The Bono Act is thus perceived to add an instability to commerce and investment, areas which have a better legal theoretical basis than intellectual property, whose theory is of quite recent development and is often criticized as being a corporate chimera. Conceivably, if one had made such an investment and then produced a derivative work (or perhaps even re-released the work _in ipse_), he could counter a suit made by the copyright holder by declaring that Congress had unconstitutionally made, [ex post facto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_post_facto "Ex post facto"), a restriction on the previously unrestricted.

[Howard Besser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Besser "Howard Besser") questioned the proponents' argument that "new works would not be created", which implies that the goal of copyright is to make the creation of new works possible. However, the Framers of the United States Constitution evidently thought that unnecessary, instead restricting the goal of copyright to merely "promot\[&#8202;](#ref-back-Besser_1998-0)[ing\] the progress of science and useful arts". In fact, some works created under time-limited copyright would not be created under perpetual copyright because the creator of a distantly derivative work does not have the money and resources to find the owner of copyright in the original work and purchase a license, or the individual or privately held owner of copyright in the original work might refuse to license a use at any price (though a refusal to license may trigger a fair use safety valve). Thus they argue that a rich, continually replenished, public domain is necessary for continued artistic creation.[\[38\]](#refnote-Besser_1998)

### March 25, 1998, House debate

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: March 25, 1998, House debate")\]

The House debated the Copyright Term Extension Act (House Resolution 390) on March 25, 1998.[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-41) The term extension was almost completely supported, with only the mild criticism by [Jim Sensenbrenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Sensenbrenner "Jim Sensenbrenner") (Wisconsin) of "H.R. 2589 provides a very generous windfall to the entertainment industry by extending the term of copyright for an additional 20 years."[&#8202;](#ref-back-42-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-42) He suggested that it could be balanced by adding provisions from the [Fairness in Music Licensing Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_in_Music_Licensing_Act "Fairness in Music Licensing Act") (H.R. 789). [Lloyd Doggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_Doggett "Lloyd Doggett") (Texas) called the 'Fairness in Music Licensing Act' the 'Music Theft Act' and claimed that it was a mechanism to "steal the intellectual property of thousands of small businesspeople who are song writers in this land."[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-43) The majority of subsequent debate was over Sensenbrenner's House Amendment 532[&#8202;](#ref-back-44-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-44) to the CTEA. This amendment was over details of allowing music from radio and television broadcasts in small businesses to be played without licensing fees. An amendment to Sensenbrenner's amendment was proposed by [Bill McCollum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_McCollum "Bill McCollum").[&#8202;](#ref-back-45-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-45) The key differences between Sensenbrenner's proposal and McCollum's amendment were 1) local arbitration versus court lawsuits in rate disagreements, 2) all retail businesses versus only restaurants and bars, 3) 3500 square feet of general public area versus 3,500 square feet (330m2) of gross area, 4) which music licensing societies it applied to (all versus ASCAP and BMI), and 5) freedom from vicarious liability for landlords and others leasing space versus no such provision.[&#8202;](#ref-back-46-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-46) After debate (and the first verse of American Pie[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-47)) the McCollum Amendment was rejected by a vote of 259 to 150[&#8202;](#ref-back-48-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-48) and the Sensenbrenner amendment was passed by 297 to 112.[&#8202;](#ref-back-49-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-49) The Copyright Term Extension Act H.R. 2589 was passed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-50-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-50)

The term extension was supported for two key reasons. First, "copyright industries give us \[the United States\] one of our most significant trade surpluses." Second, the recently enacted legislation in the European Union had extended copyright there for 20 years, and so EU works would be protected for 20 years longer than US works if the US did not enact similar term extensions. [Howard Coble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Coble "Howard Coble") also stated that it was good for consumers since "When works are protected by copyright, they attract investors who can exploit the work for profit."[&#8202;](#ref-back-51-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-51) The term extension portion was supported by [Songwriters Guild of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songwriters_Guild_of_America "Songwriters Guild of America"), [National Academy of Songwriters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Songwriters "National Academy of Songwriters"), the [Motion Picture Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Association_of_America "Motion Picture Association of America"), the Intellectual Property Law Section of the American Bar Association, the [Recording Industry Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_Industry_Association_of_America "Recording Industry Association of America"), [National Music Publishers Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Music_Publishers_Association "National Music Publishers Association"), the [Information Technology Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Technology_Association_of_America "Information Technology Association of America") and others.[&#8202;](#ref-back-52-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-52)

## Challenges

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Challenges")\]

### Legal

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Legal")\]

Publishers and librarians, among others, brought a lawsuit, _[Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldred_v._Ashcroft "Eldred v. Ashcroft")_, to obtain an injunction on enforcement of the act. Oral arguments were heard by the [U.S. Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States "Supreme Court of the United States") on October 9, 2002. On January 15, 2003, the court held the CTEA constitutional by a 7–2 decision.[&#8202;](#ref-back-53-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-53)

In 2003, the plaintiffs in the _Eldred_ case began to shift their effort toward the U.S. Congress in support of a bill called the [Public Domain Enhancement Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Domain_Enhancement_Act "Public Domain Enhancement Act") that would make the provisions of the Bono Act apply only to copyrights that had been registered with the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress").

In May 2022, Senator [Josh Hawley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Hawley "Josh Hawley") (R- [MO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri "Missouri")) introduced a bill that would roll back the copyright term for new works to match to the 1909 Copyright Act, but also applies retroactively to works by a group of large companies specifically designed to target Disney. [Sarah Jeong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Jeong "Sarah Jeong") of _The Verge_ criticized the bill for obviously violating international agreements and the [Fifth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution "Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution") protections against [eminent domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminent_domain "Eminent domain"), as an attempt to punish Disney for opposing [Florida House Bill 1557](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_House_Bill_1557 "Florida House Bill 1557"), and because it was unlikely to pass in a Congress where Democrats control both houses.[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-54)[&#8202;](#ref-back-55-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-55) It died in committee and Hawley did not reintroduce the bill.[&#8202;](#ref-back-56-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-56)

### Empirical testing

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Copyright_Term_Extension_Act&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Empirical testing")\]

In 2012, law professors Christopher Buccafusco and [Paul J. Heald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_J._Heald "Paul J. Heald") performed tests of three key justifications of copyright extension, namely: that public domain works will be underutilized and less available, will be oversaturated by poor quality copies, and poor quality derivative works will harm the reputation of the original works. They compared works from the two decades surrounding 1923 made available as audiobooks. They found that copyrighted works were significantly less likely to be available than public domain ones, found no evidence of overexploitation driving down the price of works, and that the quality of the audiobook recordings did not significantly affect the price people were willing to pay for the books in print.[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-57) Heald's later experiment analyzing a random sample of newly posted works on [Amazon.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon.com "Amazon.com") revealed that public domain works from 1880 were posted at double the rate of copyrighted works from 1980.[&#8202;](#ref-back-58-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-58)
