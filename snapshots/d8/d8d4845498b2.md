The Portuguese navigator and explorer [Ferdinand Magellan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ferdinand-Magellan) headed the first Spanish foray to the Philippines when he made landfall on [Cebu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cebu) in March 1521; a short time later he met an untimely death on the nearby island of [Mactan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mactan-Island). After King [Philip II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Philip-II-king-of-Spain-and-Portugal) (for whom the islands are named) had dispatched three further expeditions that ended in disaster, he sent out [Miguel López de Legazpi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Miguel-Lopez-de-Legazpi), who established the first permanent Spanish settlement, in Cebu, in 1565. The Spanish city of [Manila](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manila) was founded in 1571, and by the end of the 16th century most of the coastal and lowland areas from [Luzon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Luzon) to northern [Mindanao](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mindanao) were under Spanish control. Friars marched with soldiers and soon accomplished the [nominal](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/nominal) conversion to [Roman Catholicism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-Catholicism) of all the local people under Spanish administration. But the Muslims of Mindanao and Sulu, whom the Spanish called [Moros](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Moro), were never completely subdued by [Spain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain).

Spanish rule for the first 100 years was exercised in most areas through a type of tax farming imported from the Americas and known as the [_encomienda_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/encomienda). But abusive treatment of the local tribute payers and neglect of religious instruction by _encomenderos_ (collectors of the tribute), as well as frequent withholding of revenues from the crown, caused the Spanish to abandon the system by the end of the 17th century. The governor-general, himself appointed by the king, began to appoint his own civil and military governors to rule directly.

Central government in Manila retained a [medieval](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/medieval) cast until the 19th century, and the governor-general was so powerful that he was often likened to an independent monarch. He dominated the [Audiencia](https://www.britannica.com/topic/audiencia), or high court, was captain-general of the armed forces, and enjoyed the privilege of engaging in commerce for private profit.

Manila dominated the islands not only as the political capital. The [galleon](https://www.britannica.com/technology/galleon) trade with [Acapulco](https://www.britannica.com/place/Acapulco), Mex., assured Manila’s commercial primacy as well. The exchange of Chinese silks for Mexican silver not only kept in Manila those Spanish who were seeking quick profit, but it also attracted a large [Chinese](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chinese-people) [community](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/community). The Chinese, despite being the victims of periodic massacres at the hands of suspicious Spanish, persisted and soon established a dominance of commerce that survived through the centuries.

Manila was also the [ecclesiastical](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Roman-Catholicism) capital of the Philippines. The governor-general was civil head of the church in the islands, but the archbishop vied with him for political supremacy. In the late 17th and 18th centuries the archbishop, who also had the legal status of lieutenant governor, frequently won. Augmenting their political power, religious orders, Roman Catholic hospitals and schools, and bishops acquired great wealth, mostly in land. Royal grants and [devises](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/devises) formed the core of their holdings, but many arbitrary extensions were made beyond the boundaries of the original grants.

The power of the church derived not simply from wealth and official status. The priests and friars had a command of local languages rare among the lay Spanish, and in the provinces they outnumbered civil officials. Thus, they were an invaluable source of information to the colonial government. The cultural goal of the Spanish clergy was nothing less than the full Christianization and Hispanization of the Filipino. In the first decades of missionary work, local religions were vigorously suppressed; old practices were not tolerated. But as the Christian laity grew in number and the zeal of the clergy waned, it became increasingly difficult to prevent the preservation of ancient beliefs and customs under Roman Catholic garb. Thus, even in the area of religion, pre-Spanish Filipino [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture) was not entirely destroyed.

Economic and political institutions were also altered under Spanish impact but perhaps less thoroughly than in the religious realm. The priests tried to move all the people into pueblos, or villages, surrounding the great stone churches. But the dispersed [demographic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/demographic) patterns of the old _barangay_ s largely persisted. Nevertheless, the _[datu](https://www.britannica.com/topic/datu)_’s once hereditary position became subject to Spanish appointment.

[Agricultural technology](https://www.britannica.com/technology/agricultural-technology) changed very slowly until the late 18th century, as [shifting cultivation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/slash-and-burn-agriculture) gradually gave way to more intensive sedentary farming, partly under the guidance of the friars. The socioeconomic consequences of the Spanish policies that accompanied this shift reinforced class differences. The _datu_ s and other representatives of the old noble class took advantage of the introduction of the Western concept of absolute ownership of land to claim as their own fields [cultivated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultivated) by their various retainers, even though traditional land rights had been limited to usufruct. These heirs of pre-Spanish nobility were known as the _principalia_ and played an important role in the friar-dominated local government.

# The 19th century

By the late 18th century, political and economic changes in Europe were finally beginning to affect [Spain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain) and, thus, the Philippines. Important as a stimulus to [trade](https://www.britannica.com/money/international-trade) was the gradual elimination of the monopoly enjoyed by the galleon to [Acapulco](https://www.britannica.com/place/Acapulco). The last galleon arrived in [Manila](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manila) in 1815, and by the mid-1830s Manila was open to foreign merchants almost without restriction. The demand for Philippine sugar and abaca (hemp) grew apace, and the volume of exports to Europe expanded even further after the completion of the [Suez Canal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Suez-Canal) in 1869.

The growth of commercial agriculture resulted in the appearance of a new class. Alongside the landholdings of the church and the rice estates of the pre-Spanish nobility there arose haciendas of coffee, hemp, and sugar, often the property of enterprising Chinese-Filipino mestizos. Some of the families that gained prominence in the 19th century have continued to play an important role in Philippine economics and politics.

Not until 1863 was there public education in the Philippines, and even then the church controlled the curriculum. Less than one-fifth of those who went to school could read and write Spanish, and far fewer could speak it properly. The limited [higher education](https://www.britannica.com/topic/higher-education) in the colony was entirely under clerical direction, but by the 1880s many sons of the wealthy were sent to Europe to study. There, [nationalism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/nationalism) and a passion for reform blossomed in the liberal atmosphere. Out of this talented group of overseas Filipino students arose what came to be known as the [Propaganda Movement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Propaganda-Movement). Magazines, newspapers, poetry, and pamphleteering flourished, most notably the biweekly paper [_La Solidaridad_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/La-Solidaridad), which began publication in 1889. [José Rizal](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jose-Rizal), this movement’s most brilliant figure, produced two political novels— _Noli me tangere_ (1887; _Touch Me Not_) and _El filibusterismo_ (1891; _The Reign of Greed_)—which had a wide impact in the Philippines. In 1892 Rizal returned home and formed the [Liga Filipina](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liga-Filipina), a modest reform-minded society, loyal to Spain, that breathed no word of independence. But Rizal was quickly arrested by the overly fearful Spanish, [exiled](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/exiled) to a remote island in the south, and finally executed in 1896. Meanwhile, within the Philippines there had developed a firm commitment to independence among a somewhat less privileged class.

Shocked by the arrest of Rizal in 1892, these activists quickly formed the [Katipunan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Katipunan) under the leadership of [Andres Bonifacio](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Andres-Bonifacio), a self-educated warehouseman. The Katipunan was dedicated to the expulsion of the Spanish from the islands, and preparations were made for armed revolt. Filipino rebels had been numerous in the history of Spanish rule, but now for the first time they were inspired by nationalist ambitions and possessed the education needed to make success a real possibility.

## The [Philippine Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/Philippine-Revolution)

[![Portrait of Emilio Aguinaldo wearing a white suit and dark bow tie, looking directly at the camera against a plain background.](https://cdn.britannica.com/05/13605-050-FC419497/Emilio-Aguinaldo.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/05/13605-050-FC419497/Emilio-Aguinaldo.jpg)

[Emilio Aguinaldo](https://cdn.britannica.com/05/13605-050-FC419497/Emilio-Aguinaldo.jpg) As a key leader in the fight for Philippine independence, Emilio Aguinaldo declared the nation's sovereignty from Spain on June 12, 1898, in Kawit, Cavite, and later led armed resistance against U.S. rule.(more)

In August 1896, Spanish friars uncovered evidence of the Katipunan’s plans, and its leaders were forced into premature action. Revolts broke out in several provinces around Manila. After months of fighting, severe Spanish retaliation forced the revolutionary armies to retreat to the hills. In December 1897 a truce was concluded with the Spanish. [Emilio Aguinaldo](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Emilio-Aguinaldo), a municipal mayor and commander of the rebel forces, was paid a large sum and was allowed to go to [Hong Kong](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hong-Kong) with other leaders; the Spanish promised reforms as well. But reforms were slow in coming, and small bands of rebels, distrustful of Spanish promises, kept their arms; clashes grew more frequent.

Meanwhile, war had broken out between Spain and the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-United-States) (the [Spanish-American War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Spanish-American-War)). After the U.S. naval victory in the [Battle of Manila Bay](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Manila-Bay-1899) in May 1898, Aguinaldo and his entourage returned to the Philippines with the help of Adm. [George Dewey](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Dewey). Confident of U.S. support, Aguinaldo reorganized his forces and soon liberated several towns south of Manila. Independence was declared on June 12 (now celebrated as Independence Day). In September a [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) congress met in [Malolos](https://www.britannica.com/place/Malolos), north of Manila, which drew up a fundamental law derived from European and Latin American precedents. A government was formed on the basis of that constitution in January 1899, with Aguinaldo as president of the new [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state), popularly known as the “Malolos Republic.”

[![United States soldiers crouch and aim rifles from a trench lined with logs in a wooded area near Manila during the Spanish-American War in 1898.](https://cdn.britannica.com/20/62120-050-FBE6007A/soldiers-trench-Phil-Manila-Spanish-American-War-1898.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/20/62120-050-FBE6007A/soldiers-trench-Phil-Manila-Spanish-American-War-1898.jpg)

[Spanish-American War](https://cdn.britannica.com/20/62120-050-FBE6007A/soldiers-trench-Phil-Manila-Spanish-American-War-1898.jpg) U.S. soldiers in a trench near Manila during the Spanish-American War, 1898.(more)

Meanwhile, U.S. troops had landed in Manila and, with important Filipino help, forced the capitulation in August 1898 of the Spanish commander there. The Americans, however, would not let Filipino forces enter the city. It was soon apparent to Aguinaldo and his advisers that earlier expressions of sympathy for Filipino independence by Dewey and U.S. consular officials in Hong Kong had little significance. They felt betrayed.

U.S. commissioners to the peace negotiations in Paris had been instructed to demand from Spain the cession of the Philippines to the United States; such cession was confirmed with the signing of the [Treaty of Paris](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Paris-1898) on December 10, 1898. Ratification followed in the U.S. Senate in February 1899, but with only one vote more than the required two-thirds. Arguments of “ [manifest destiny](https://www.britannica.com/event/Manifest-Destiny)” could not overwhelm a determined anti-imperialist minority.

[![Philippine-American War: Manila](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/70929-050-5262D6CA/Portion-ruins-forces-Philippines-Manila-1899.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/70929-050-5262D6CA/Portion-ruins-forces-Philippines-Manila-1899.jpg)

[Philippine-American War: Manila](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/70929-050-5262D6CA/Portion-ruins-forces-Philippines-Manila-1899.jpg) Portion of the ruins of Manila after shelling by U.S. forces in 1899.(more)

By the time the treaty was ratified, [hostilities](https://www.britannica.com/event/Philippine-American-War) had already broken out between U.S. and Filipino forces. Since Filipino leaders did not recognize U.S. [sovereignty](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereignty) over the islands and U.S. commanders gave no weight to Filipino claims of independence, the conflict was inevitable. It took two years of counterinsurgency warfare and some wise conciliatory moves in the political arena to break the back of the nationalist resistance. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901 and shortly thereafter appealed to his countrymen to accept U.S. rule.

[![Philippine-American War](https://cdn.britannica.com/31/70931-050-F596C9D3/district-Manila-Phil-insurrection-1899.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/31/70931-050-F596C9D3/district-Manila-Phil-insurrection-1899.jpg)

[Philippine-American War](https://cdn.britannica.com/31/70931-050-F596C9D3/district-Manila-Phil-insurrection-1899.jpg) A district of Manila set afire during the Philippine-American War, 1899.(more)

The Filipino revolutionary movement had two goals, national and social. The first goal, independence, though realized briefly, was frustrated by the American decision to continue administering the islands. The goal of fundamental [social change](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-change), [manifest](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/manifest) in the nationalization of friar lands by the Malolos Republic, was ultimately frustrated by the power and [resilience](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/resilience) of entrenched institutions. Share tenants who had rallied to Aguinaldo’s cause, partly for economic reasons, merely exchanged one landlord for another. In any case, the proclamation of a republic in 1898 had marked the Filipinos as the first Asian people to try to throw off European colonial rule.
