# Industry News: COSC Excellence Chronometer

COSC unveils the “Excellence Chronometer” as the next benchmark for the watch industry.

[Articles](https://wristreview.com/category/articles/)

·February 16, 2026·[14 Comments](#disqus_thread)·3 min read

###### BY [HARLAN CHAPMAN-GREEN](https://www.wristreview.com/?s=Harlan+Chapman-Green)

A few days ago, an announcement was made that could impact the wider world of mechanical watchmaking. Since 1973, COSC, the Contrôle Officiel Suisse des Chronomètres (Official Swiss Chronometer Testing Institute), has been providing the watch industry with independent accuracy certification, giving consumers something tangible they can understand that speaks for the precision of their timepiece. On the 12th of February, COSC announced the launch of a new certification scheme, the “Excellence Chronometer”.

![](https://usercontent.one/wp/wristreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Equipment_mesure2-scaled.webp?media=1794109283)

COSC’s requirements for chronometers have historically been quite stringent. Although there are a few factors the watches need to meet, the main focus is a movement’s average daily rate – that is, how many seconds it gains or loses over 24 hours. For the standard COSC chronometer, the tolerance is **[-4s to +6s](https://wristreview.com/the-jargon-buster-watch-standards-agencies-part-1/)** per day. However, modern manufacturing techniques mean that many watches can meet that requirement quite easily. Should a brand want to, they can now submit their watch for the Excellence Chronometer certificate, which supplements the COSC chronometer certificate rather than replacing it.

![](https://usercontent.one/wp/wristreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/1770820756-arrivee-des-montres.avif?media=1794109283)

For the standard chronometer, COSC examines mechanical movements for their accuracy before they are cased by the manufacturer. Because the new Excellence Chronometer enhances the standard one, watches destined to be tested as Excellence Chronometers will still undergo the standard 15 days of testing uncased, where the average daily rate must stay within -4s to +6s per day. They will then be returned to the manufacturer for casing, and a robot simulating daily wear will be used for five days. The average daily rate during this period must fall within -2s to +4s per day.

![](https://usercontent.one/wp/wristreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Equipment_atomic_Clock-scaled.webp?media=1794109283)

The watches are then submitted to a magnetic field of 200 Gauss to ensure resistance to the low-powered magnetic fields we might encounter in our daily lives. For context, that’s equal to 20,000 microtesla (µT), the unit most commonly used when discussing everyday magnetic fields. By contrast, the **[Earth’s static magnetic field, as measured in the UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/electric-and-magnetic-fields-sources-and-exposure/electric-and-magnetic-fields-sources-and-exposure#what-are-the-main-sources-of-electric-and-magnetic-fields)**, measures around 50µT, or 0.5 Gauss. Even an induction hob – widely cited as a strong domestic magnetic field source – **[emits only a few microtesla in normal use](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9565691/)**. At the other extreme, **[a “typical” MRI machine’s magnet is rated at 3 tesla](https://www.siemens-healthineers.com/en-uk/news/mso-whats-that-knocking)**, which is 3,000,000µT, or 30,000 Gauss.

The final step in the Excellence Chronometer testing is to ensure the manufacturer’s claimed power reserve is accurate; if a watch doesn’t meet it, it won’t get the certificate.

![](https://usercontent.one/wp/wristreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Equipment_mesure3-scaled.webp?media=1794109283)

All of this sounds like good progress for the watch industry. There are clear intentions to further the reputation of the Swiss watch industry and the watch industry at large by making a new certification with tougher standards. Perhaps the biggest change of all of these is that the testing for the Excellence Chronometer occurs when the watch has been cased up, which is as close to simulating real-life wear as you can get. I expect it is also a pushback against in-house testing. Manufacturers like Rolex and Omega continue to offer their own certification, such as Rolex’s “Superlative Chronometer” and Omega’s METAS-certified “Master Chronometer”. Although the Excellence Chronometer doesn’t have the stringent requirements of +/-2 seconds deviation per day like the Superlative Chronometer or the Master Chronometer’s 15,000 Gauss resistance requirement, it does allow more manufacturers to prove their mettle in watchmaking. We should also remember that there are complex watches that are also COSC-certified chronometers, such as Chopard’s L.U.C Perpetual T tourbillon watch, which is more complex than most of Rolex’s and Omega’s offerings. I imagine then that the Excellence Chronometer is something Chopard and makers of complicated mechanical watches will be interested in.

![](https://usercontent.one/wp/wristreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Equipment_lot2-scaled.webp?media=1794109283)

Of course, the Excellence Chronometer is an optional extra, but I expect more manufacturers will take on the challenge. Will it impact in-house certification? I doubt it. The brands that do in-house certification will undoubtedly have spent hundreds of thousands at least, on restructuring their manufacturing processes to incorporate the testing phases, although we should bear in mind that we can only rely on the manufacturer itself to tell us that its watches are accurate. At least with COSC, it’s an independent testing body doing the examinations. The Excellence Chronometer could draw new brands that fancy a challenge. COSC says that it will continue ensuring it’s ready to deploy the new certification, and will officially unveil it at Watches & Wonders Geneva in April. Starting in October, brands will be able to send their watches for Excellence Chronometer testing, with the first Excellence Chronometer-certified watches coming as soon as possible after that.

Bryan Winton[9 months ago](#comment-6838468169 "Monday, February 16, 2026 7:35 AM")

While it’s a really interesting development, here's what's technically wrong with how this is being reported and it's bothering me more than it should. I keep seeing articles mess up the fundamentals.

First issue is

> "beat rate deviation tolerance of 10 seconds per day"

Which is imprecise language. COSC does not define its core requirement as beat rate deviation. The ISO 3159 standard specifies average daily rate, _marche moyenne journalière_ if we're going with the original French terminology, not beat error or rate deviation in the escapement sense. Beat rate refers to the frequency of the balance wheel oscillation, usually measured in beats per hour or vibrations per hour. Daily rate is how many seconds the watch actually gains or loses over a 24 hour period. These are related concepts but they are not interchangeable terms. Mixing them up muddies the water and makes it seem like the writer doesn't understand the difference between escapement regulation and practical timekeeping results.

Second, the tolerance is framed correctly in numbers but awkwardly in logic. Saying

> "10 seconds per day, specifically 4s to +6s per day"

is mathematically fine but COSC never presents it as a plus or minus 10 second band. It is always expressed directly as –4 to +6 seconds per day. That's the standard, not a derived total. When you frame it as "10 seconds" first it makes it sound symmetric like it's +/- 5 seconds, which completely misrepresents how the tolerance actually works. The range is intentionally asymmetric because mechanical watches behave asymmetrically. Mainspring torque decreases as it unwinds so watches typically run slower at lower power reserve. The wider positive tolerance accounts for the fact that watches tend to run faster when fully wound. That's not arbitrary, it's based on how mechanical movements actually function in the real world.

Third and this one really gets me, the minus sign keeps getting dropped. Writing "4s to +6s per day" without the –4s is a straight up technical error. Slow rates matter just as much as fast ones in COSC testing. My watch runs at –3 seconds per day and that's totally within spec, but you wouldn't know it from how some of these articles are written. The minus isn't decorative or optional, it conveys critical information about the direction of rate variance. You can't just drop the minus sign at your leisure in favor of a more "casual" "to".

All of this official terminology matters because the lack of it spreads misinformation. People read these articles, think they're learning the specs, and then repeat incorrect information on forums and in discussions. We end up with widespread confusion about what COSC actually tests and measures.
