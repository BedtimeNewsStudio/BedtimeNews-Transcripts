# BioShimazu Yoshihisa (1533-1611)

Shimazu Yoshihisa (島津義久, 1533–1611) was the sixteenth head of the [Shimazu clan](https://jref.com/articles/shimazu-clan.855/) and one of the most important regional warlords of late sixteenth-century Japan. Active from the [Sengoku period](https://jref.com/articles/sengoku-period.232/ "Sengoku Period") into the early [Edo era](https://jref.com/articles/edo-period-1600-1868.785/ "Edo Period"), he inherited a house with deep roots in southern [Kyūshū](https://jref.com/articles/kyushu-travel-guide.157/) and, with crucial help from his brothers and senior retainers, turned it into the strongest military power in much of the island. For a time, the Shimazu came close to controlling almost all of [Kyūshū](https://jref.com/articles/kyushu-travel-guide.157/ "Kyūshū"). That advance ended only when [Toyotomi Hideyoshi](https://jref.com/articles/toyotomi-hideyoshi-1537-1598.788/) intervened in force. Even after surrender, Yoshihisa remained central to the clan's politics and helped preserve its core domain through the transition from the age of civil war to Tokugawa rule.

[![Statue of Shimazu Yoshihisa](https://jref.com/data/attachments/127/127749-9c19748581a3ae3831abfc307ff4800b.jpg?hash=bfuawampK0)](https://jref.com/attachments/shimazu-yoshihisa-statue-webp.135856/)

Statue of Shimazu Yoshihisa from the collection of the University Art Museum, Tokyo University of the Arts (東京芸術大学大学美術館蔵).

The Shimazu were an old warrior house whose authority rested on long service as military governors in [Satsuma](https://jref.com/articles/satsuma-domain.71/), Ōsumi and Hyūga. By the [Muromachi period](https://jref.com/articles/muromachi-period.281/ "Muromachi Period"), the family had split into rival branches, and internal conflict weakened its hold over southern Kyūshū. That disorder was largely checked by Yoshihisa's father, Shimazu Takahisa (島津貴久, 1514–1571), who emerged from a cadet line and restored the authority of the main house. Yoshihisa, his eldest son, was born on 9 February 1533 at Izaku Castle in present-day Kagoshima Prefecture. His childhood name (幼名 _yōmyō_) was Torajumaru (虎寿丸). After coming of age, he first used the name Tadayoshi, then received the character yoshi from the [shōgun](https://jref.com/articles/shogun.29/ "Shōgun") [Ashikaga Yoshiteru](https://jref.com/articles/ashikaga-yoshiteru-1536-1565.898/) (足利義輝, 1536–1565), becoming Yoshitatsu and later Yoshihisa.

Later tradition presents him as quieter than his brothers, though his grandfather Shimazu Tadayoshi, better known as Jisshinsai (島津忠良, 1493–1568), is said to have recognised the distinct abilities of all four sons of the house. Yoshihisa was the commander, Yoshihiro the battlefield leader, Toshihisa the strategist, and Iehisa the tactician. Whether the saying is exact or not, it reflects the structure of Shimazu power. Their rise depended on coordinated family leadership rather than on the exploits of a single man.

Yoshihisa first saw war in 1554 during the attack on Iwatsurugi Castle, as part of the long campaign to subdue local warrior families in Satsuma and Ōsumi. These struggles were central to power in southern Kyūshū, where entrenched kokushū (国衆) still held local authority. In 1557, the Gamō submitted. In 1566, before Satsuma had been fully unified, Yoshihisa succeeded Takahisa as the sixteenth head of the clan. By 1569, the Shimazu had driven out rivals from Ōguchi, and in 1570, the Tōgō and Iriki'in also submitted, completing the unification of Satsuma.

Ōsumi followed soon after. In 1573, the Nejime submitted, and in the next year, the Kimotsuki and Ijichi were brought into line. The last major theatre was Hyūga, where the Shimazu had long faced the Itō. In May 1572, the Itō sent about 3,000 men into Shimazu territory. Yoshihisa's younger brother, [Shimazu Yoshihiro](https://jref.com/articles/shimazu-yoshihiro-1535-1619.856/) (島津義弘, 1535–1619), who held Iino Castle, marched out with only about 300 men and destroyed them at the Battle of Kizakihara. The victory became famous as an example of Shimazu's battlefield method: deception, ambush and close, disciplined attack. After the fall of Takabaru in 1576, Itō's authority collapsed further, and Itō Yoshisuke (伊東義祐, 1512–1585) fled to Bungo to seek help from [Ōtomo Sōrin](https://jref.com/articles/%C5%8Ctomo-s%C5%8Drin-1530-1587.860/) (大友宗麟, 1530–1587). By then, the Shimazu had effectively secured Satsuma, Ōsumi and Hyūga.

The decisive turning point came in 1578, when Ōtomo Sōrin invaded Hyūga with a large army. The Ōtomo laid siege to Takajō, defended by Yamada Arinobu (山田有信, 1531–1609) and reinforced by Shimazu Iehisa (島津家久, 1547–1587). Yoshihisa advanced with more than 20,000 men and met the invaders near the Mimi River. There, he used the tactic later closely associated with the Shimazu, tsuri-no-buse (釣り野伏戦), a feigned-retreat ambush tactic that drew the enemy forward before hidden troops struck. The Ōtomo army collapsed. Several leading commanders were killed, and the defeat broke Ōtomo's military strength in Kyūshū.

After Mimigawa, Yoshihisa's standing rose sharply. Local houses in Higo moved closer to the Shimazu, and in 1580 negotiations began with [Oda Nobunaga](https://jref.com/articles/oda-nobunaga-1534-1582.674/) (織田信長, 1534–1582), partly through Konoe Sakihisa (近衛前久, 1536–1612), to end the conflict with the Ōtomo on broader strategic terms. Yoshihisa seems to have accepted Nobunaga's supremacy in principle, and may have planned to support later operations against the Mōri. Nobunaga's death in 1582 ended that possibility.

Meanwhile, power in western Kyūshū shifted again as Ryūzōji Takanobu (龍造寺隆信, 1529–1584) expanded from Hizen. Arima Harunobu (有馬晴信, 1567–1612), unable to withstand the pressure, appealed to the Shimazu. In 1582, Shimazu forces attacked Chijiwa Castle, and Arima fell into their orbit. In 1584, Yoshihisa sent Iehisa to Shimabara while he himself advanced as far as Minamata in Higo. At Okitanawate, Iehisa destroyed the far larger Ryūzōji army on marshy ground. Ryūzōji Takanobu was killed together with many retainers. The battle removed the strongest rival in western Kyūshū and opened the way for rapid Shimazu expansion.

By 1585, the clan had reached its widest extent. Higo was effectively subdued after the defeat of Aso Koremitsu (阿蘇惟光, d. 1593), and Yoshihiro was installed as a governing official there. Chikuzen and Chikugo were invaded. In practical terms, the Shimazu controlled Satsuma, Ōsumi, Hyūga, Higo, Hizen, Chikuzen and Chikugo, with only parts of Bungo and a few other districts beyond their grasp. For a brief moment, the unification of Kyūshū seemed within reach.

That was also the point at which their expansion collided with the emerging national order. Other Kyūshū lords, above all the Ōtomo, appealed to Toyotomi Hideyoshi (豊臣秀吉, 1537–1598), who could not permit one regional house to dominate the whole island. He issued orders prohibiting further warfare under what would later be called the sōbuji policy. There was debate within the Shimazu camp, and some around Yoshihisa appeared to recognise the danger. Even so, he pressed on. In 1586, Shimazu's armies moved further into Chikuzen. Katsuo Castle fell. Iwaya Castle, defended by Takahashi Shōun (高橋紹運, 1548–1586), was taken after a fierce siege, though the cost to the attackers was high. Hōmanzan also fell, but Tachibana Castle held out, and the Shimazu turned towards Bungo.

The campaign briefly produced another victory. In December 1586, Iehisa defeated the first Toyotomi relief force at Hetsugigawa. Among the dead were Chōsokabe Nobuchika (長宗我部信親, 1565–1587) and Sogō Masayasu (十河存保, 1554–1587). Yet the result changed little. Hideyoshi had already committed himself to a full invasion.

The Kyūshū campaign of 1587 overwhelmed the Shimazu by scale alone. Toyotomi Hidenaga (豊臣秀長, 1540–1591) advanced from the northeast with a vast coalition force, and Hideyoshi followed with another huge army through northern Kyūshū. One lord after another changed sides. At Nejirozaka, the Shimazu attempted a night attack and failed, then fell back into Satsuma. As Toyotomi pressure reached their homeland, resistance began to break. Shimazu Tadatatsu of Izumi surrendered. Ijūin Tadamune, already engaged in negotiation, submitted as a hostage. Iehisa also opened his castle.

Yoshihisa returned to Kagoshima, shaved his head, took the Buddhist name Ryūhaku, and met Hideyoshi at Taiheiji together with Ijūin Tadamune. His surrender was formal, but the settlement was less destructive than it might have been. Hideyoshi stripped away most of the territory won during the great advance and redistributed it to generals such as [Katō Kiyomasa](https://jref.com/articles/kat%C5%8D-kiyomasa-1562%E2%80%931611.1109/) (加藤清正, 1562–1611), [Konishi Yukinaga](https://jref.com/articles/konishi-yukinaga-d-1600.1110/) (小西行長, 1555–1600) and [Kuroda Yoshitaka](https://jref.com/articles/kuroda-yoshitaka-1546%E2%80%931604.1024/) (黒田孝高, 1546–1604). The Shimazu, however, retained Satsuma, Ōsumi and the Morokata district of Hyūga. Their enlarged sphere was gone, but their southern heartland survived.

Yoshihisa did not disappear from politics after 1587. Public dealings with the Toyotomi regime increasingly passed through Yoshihiro, yet Yoshihisa retained the clan's ceremonial heirlooms, held important lands directly, and appears to have continued guiding internal government. Because he had no surviving son, succession became a pressing issue. He adopted Yoshihiro's son and arranged his marriage to his own daughter Kameju. After that, the heir died in Korea in 1593, and the arrangement shifted to a younger brother, the future Shimazu Tadatsune.

Under the Toyotomi rule, the Shimazu remained uneasy vassals. They were slow to comply with some central policies, struggled to meet military quotas, and drew suspicion in [Kyōto](https://jref.com/articles/kyoto-travel-guide.164/ "Kyōto"). During [the invasions of Korea](https://jref.com/articles/the-invasions-of-korea.789/), they fought with distinction in several engagements and were later rewarded with a 50,000- [koku](https://jref.com/articles/kokudaka.284/) increase, yet their service remained troubled. One retainer, Umekita Kunikane, even rebelled en route to service. Hideyoshi, reading this as continued defiance, demanded Shimazu Toshihisa's (島津歳久, 1537–1592) death, the most openly resistant of Yoshihisa's brothers. Yoshihisa, with little room left to manoeuvre, ordered him to commit suicide.

His later years were not spent solely on military or administrative matters. He had ties with courtly and literary figures, including [Hosokawa Fujitaka](https://jref.com/articles/hosokawa-fujitaka-1534-1610.810/) (細川藤孝, 1534–1610), who is said to have instructed him in classical literature, and Konoe Sakihisa, who seems to have frequented his household. He also initially received Christian missionaries such as Luís de Almeida with some favour. These connections suggest that the later Shimazu court was not merely a military command centre but part of the wider cultural world linking Kyūshū, Kyōto and overseas trade.

After [the Battle of Sekigahara](https://jref.com/articles/the-battle-of-sekigahara.987/) in 1600, Yoshihisa again showed political caution. Yoshihiro, who had been in Kyōto, joined the western army and survived the battle through the famous Shimazu breakout, but Yoshihisa remained in Satsuma. That distinction became the basis of subsequent diplomacy. The situation in Kyūshū was tense. Katō Kiyomasa attacked the former Konishi territory, and Shimazu forces fought in Higo in support of the former western allies. Plans were also discussed for a larger anti-Shimazu campaign involving Kyūshū lords, while [Tokugawa Ieyasu](https://jref.com/articles/tokugawa-ieyasu-1543-1616.790/) (徳川家康, 1543–1616) considered a major expedition under [Tokugawa Hidetada](https://jref.com/articles/tokugawa-hidetada-1579-1632.260/ "Tokugawa Hidetada"). Those plans, though serious, were suspended.

Yoshihisa argued that Yoshihiro's actions had been his brother's own and did not formally implicate the whole house. Ieyasu demanded that Yoshihisa come to the capital, but he repeatedly refused, citing illness, poverty, road repairs and other practical difficulties. Instead, he sent envoys, and eventually Tadatsune. Negotiations lasted two years. In the end, Ieyasu conceded enough to confirm the Shimazu lands. In December 1602, Tadatsune travelled to the capital as Yoshihisa's representative, and the clan escaped dispossession.

That same year, Yoshihisa formally transferred the clan heirlooms and headship to Tadatsune and entered retirement, though his influence remained considerable. He continued corresponding with the Tokugawa government and retained great authority within the domain. In 1604, he moved to Kokubu Castle in Ōsumi. Later tradition credits him with encouraging tobacco cultivation in the region, a plausible policy in a poor but commercially minded southern domain.

His final years were marked by growing internal strain. Sources suggest tensions between Tadatsune and Yoshihisa's daughter, Kameju, and among the senior lines of authority represented by Yoshihisa, Yoshihiro, and Tadatsune. Yoshihisa is also said to have opposed the invasion of the Ryukyus promoted by the younger leadership. By about 1610, rumours of division were already circulating within the domain.

He died of illness at Kokubu Castle on 21 January 1611, aged seventy-nine. His posthumous Buddhist title was Kankei Sonchū Anshu. He was buried at the former site of Fukushōji in Kagoshima, and memorials were later erected at Kokubu, Imakumano Kannonji in Kyōto, and Kōyasan. No secure contemporary portrait survives. A bronze statue at Taiheiji, depicting his surrender to Hideyoshi, was made only after his death.

![Grave of Shimazu Yoshihisa](https://jref.com/attachments/tomb-of-shimazu-yoshihisa-webp.135855/)

Grave of Shimazu Yoshihisa (site of Tokujian, Kokubu, Kirishima City)

A _hōkyōin tō_ (宝篋印塔) stone pagoda marking the grave of Shimazu Yoshihisa stands at the former site of Fukushōji (福昌寺) in Kagoshima City, a major Buddhist temple in Kagoshima closely associated with the Shimazu clan. It stood near Kagoshima Castle and served for centuries as the principal family temple (菩提寺 bodaiji) of the Satsuma rulers. The temple was founded in the medieval period and belonged to the Rinzai Zen (臨済宗) tradition. From the Muromachi era onward, it functioned as a central mortuary site for the Shimazu house. Many clan leaders were buried or commemorated there, and the grounds once contained numerous stone memorial monuments, including hōkyōintō (宝篋印塔) pagodas marking the graves of successive family heads. After the [Meiji Restoration](https://jref.com/articles/meiji-restoration.75/ "Meiji Restoration"), the temple suffered severe damage during the anti-Buddhist movement known as haibutsu kishaku (廃仏毀釈) in the early 1870s. Much of the complex was destroyed or dismantled. Only parts of the cemetery and scattered monuments survived.

Other memorials survive elsewhere. At the ruins of Kongōji in Kokubu, Kirishima City, a three-tiered stone pagoda enshrines what is said to be part of Yoshihisa's remains, traditionally described as a tooth. In Kyōto, at Imakumano Kannonji (今熊野観音寺), a memorial tower built in Keichō 5 (1600) stands alongside those of Hirata Masamune and Yamada Arihide. The structure was erected as a _gyakushū tō_ (逆修塔), a monument commissioned during a person's lifetime for their own posthumous rites. Another memorial pagoda dedicated to Yoshihisa stands on Mount Kōya.
