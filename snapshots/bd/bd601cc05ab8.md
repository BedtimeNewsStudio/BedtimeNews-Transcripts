## Measure design

Going into 2020, the existing charter required the city council to provide funding to the police department to provide for "a police force of at least 0.0017 employees per resident." This charter amendment would have removed this minimum funding requirement, giving the city council more authority over funding levels for the police department.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Text-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-Text)

A 51% vote at the ballot would have been required to pass this charter amendment.

## Path to the ballot

_See also: [Laws governing local ballot measures in Minnesota](https://ballotpedia.org/Laws_governing_local_ballot_measures_in_Minnesota "Laws governing local ballot measures in Minnesota")_

### Charter amendment process

Minnesota State Statute establishes several ways to amend a city charter. A charter amendment can originate in the Minneapolis Charter Commission and be referred by the commission for voter approval at the ballot. Such a charter amendment referral needs to go through the following process:[&#8202;](#ref-back-Amending-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-Amending)

- The Minneapolis Charter Commission votes to approve a proposed charter amendment for the ballot.
- The amendment is sent to the city council, where the appropriate committee prepares the ballot language for the amendment with assistance from the city attorney.
- The full city council then must approve the ballot language to send the measure to the voters.
  - The deadline for the council to approve the ballot language was 74 days before the election, which was August 21 for the November 2020 election.
- The mayor can veto the city council's official action on the ballot language.
- The city council can override the veto with a two-thirds supermajority vote.

There are also three other ways the charter can be amended.

- A charter amendment can originate in the city council and be proposed by the city council for voter approval at the ballot after review by the Minneapolis Charter Commission.
- A charter amendment can be put on the ballot by a citizen initiative petition signed by registered voters equal to 5 percent of the votes cast at the last statewide general election (10,356 for 2020).[&#8202;](#ref-back-Amending-1)[\[3\]](#refnote-Amending)
- The city council can pass certain charter amendments without sending them to the voters if city council members unanimously approve the amendment by ordinance. Such city council votes can be challenged by a signature petition containing 2,000 signatures.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Amending-2)[\[3\]](#refnote-Amending)

### Details for this charter amendment

- Minneapolis City Council members Jeremiah Ellison, Alondra Cano, Cam Gordon, Steve Fletcher, and President Lisa Bender sponsored a proposal to remove the police department from the city charter and replace it with a new charter department named the Department of Community Safety and Violence Prevention.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Text-3)[\[1\]](#refnote-Text)
- On June 26, 2020, the Minneapolis City Council voted unanimously to send the remove and replace charter amendment to the Minneapolis Charter Commission, a required step in the process for a charter amendment originating from the city council.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4)[&#8202;](#ref-back-CouncilLegislation-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-CouncilLegislation)
- The charter commission scheduled public hearings on the city council's proposal.
- The charter commission also introduced this alternative charter amendment to repeal the charter's minimum police funding requirement.[&#8202;](#ref-back-LegislationHistoryFunding-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-LegislationHistoryFunding)
- On July 20, the charter commission scheduled a public hearing for its alternative proposal for July 27.[&#8202;](#ref-back-LegislationHistoryFunding-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-LegislationHistoryFunding)
- On July 29, the charter commission voted 8-6 to reject the charter amendment and to not refer it to the ballot.[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-7)

### 2018 charter amendment on police oversight

On August 3, 2018, the Minneapolis City Council voted 7-5 to send a charter amendment proposal to the Minneapolis Charter Commission that would have repealed provisions in the charter giving the mayor complete control over the city's police department. The measure would have, instead, allowed rules and regulations for the police department to come from both the city council and the mayor. The charter commission did not make a recommendation to the city council in time for the city council to put the measure on the 2018 ballot. Below is the timeline of the 2018 charter amendment proposal:[&#8202;](#ref-back-CommissionTwoYears-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-CommissionTwoYears)[&#8202;](#ref-back-CouncilTwoYears-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-CouncilTwoYears)[&#8202;](#ref-back-CommissionTwoYearsTaskForce-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-CommissionTwoYearsTaskForce)

- **August 3, 2018:**The city council proposed sending the charter amendment to the November 2018 ballot for voter approval.

  - According to [the process for charter amendments in state law](#Path_to_the_ballot), the charter commission had to make a recommendation to the city council before the city council could vote to put the measure on the ballot.
- **August 8, 2018:** On the last charter commission meeting before the city council's deadline for adding measures to the November 2018 ballot, the charter commission voted to file the proposal and not make a recommendation to the city council. This prevented the city council from voting on the measure in time for inclusion on the 2018 ballot.
- **August 21, 2018:** The charter commission approved a task force to produce a report on the charter amendment proposal.
- **December 12, 2018:** The task force delivered the report to the charter commission. The report can be viewed by [clicking here](https://lims.minneapolismn.gov/Download/File/1969/Police%20Department%20Charter%20Amendment%20-%20Charter%20Commission%20Report.pdf).
- **January 2, 2019:** The charter commission rejected the city council's charter amendment proposal.
