# Moscow Journal; Father of K.G.B. Might Return to Headquarters

- Sept. 17, 2002

It was a defining symbol of the collapse of the Soviet Union: on the night of Aug. 22, 1991, five cranes pulled down the towering bronze statue of Feliks Dzerzhinsky, the founder of the Soviet secret police, as thousands of Muscovites cheered with the delirium of defiance and newly found freedoms.

Now, in what may become a defining symbol of today's Russia, a new campaign is under way to put Dzerzhinsky back on his central pedestal -- a traffic island in Lubyanka Square in front of the headquarters of the former K.G.B. and its successor, the Federal Security Services.

The idea of resurrecting Dzerzhinsky -- rehabilitating him in Soviet parlance -- has been nurtured for years by those still ambivalent about the Soviet Union's collapse. But an unexpected gift last week may ensure the statue's return from exile.

Moscow's mayor, Yuri M. Luzhkov, announced that he supported the return of the statue, calling it an ''excellent monument'' even as he denied that its restoration would represent ''a return to the past.''

The autocratic mayor's stark reversal from his former position shocked liberal politicians and human rights advocates who represent the victims of the police state Dzerzhinsky helped create. Today nearly 100 of them gathered in Lubyanka Square to begin a campaign to oppose the statue's restoration.

''Normal people understand what a monument to Dzerzhinsky represents: how many repressions, how many people died,'' said Nina N. Artemyeva, 77, a demonstrator.

Dzerzhinsky's statue, erected in 1958, was one of the most reviled symbols of Soviet rule. It came to symbolize the terror committed in the name of the revolutionary cause.

The controversy, in many ways, epitomizes a struggle here over the symbols of the new Russian state. After the Soviet Union collapsed, many Soviet monuments were removed, but plaques to Soviet heroes, not to mention the hammer and sickle, still adorn many buildings.

In Moscow, as in many Russian cities, there are central monuments to Lenin, the father of the revolution. Lenin himself, of course, remains in state in his tomb in Red Square.

Now, with the euphoria of that August night faded, the Soviet iconography is returning. Two years ago, President Vladimir V. Putin restored the music of the Soviet anthem, with new lyrics, and the Soviet Red Banner as the armed forces emblem.

Indeed, many of those who gathered on Lubyanka today saw Mr. Putin's pervasive influence in the mayor's sudden reversal. They warned of a resurgence of Russia's security services under Mr. Putin.

Mr. Putin is a former colonel of the K.G.B., who went on to serve briefly as the chief of the successor, Federal Security Services. It is a measure of Dzerzhinsky's status among the security services that their Web site, www.fsb.ru, includes a laudatory report on the 125th anniversary of Dzerzhinsky's birth: Sept. 11, 1877.

In an interview with the French newspaper Le Figaro this year, one of Mr. Putin's staunchest critics, Boris A. Berezovsky, recalled seeing a Dzerzhinsky bust on Mr. Putin's desk as recently as 1999.

''I think that Luzhkov thought for a long time about what to give Putin on his birthday on Oct. 7,'' Sergei N. Yushenkov, a member of Parliament and co-chairman of the Liberal Party, told the demonstrators today.

Whether Mr. Putin supports Dzerzhinsky's restoration remains to be seen. A spokeswoman for his office, Vera N. Barysheva, said today, ''No official statement has been made so far.''

But his support is unnecessary. Mr. Luzhkov appears to have the final say on the city's monuments. His office did not respond to requests for comment, as is its wont.

Dzerzhinsky, known as Iron Feliks because of his unbending persecutions of opponents of the new regime, was one of Lenin's most trusted lieutenants. After the revolution in 1917, he proposed creating the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, which later became the Soviet K.G.B.

He headed the secret police until his death by heart attack in 1926, before the worst of Stalin's purges, so some view him less harshly than other figures. An opinion poll conducted in 2000, after K.G.B veterans appealed in writing to Mr. Putin to restore Dzerzhinsky to Lubyanka Square, found that 60 percent of those who responded favored the idea, while 21 percent opposed it and 19 percent said it was difficult to say.

He also headed committees on children and railways, and it was on that work that Mr. Luzhkov focused.

''We should remember that he solved the problem of homeless children and bailed out the railroads in a period of devastation,'' the news agency Interfax quoted him as saying. ''There were excesses at the time, the Red Terror. But if all the useful things Dzerzhinsky did were taken into account, it would be worthy of making the decision to return the statue to Lubyanka.''

After it was toppled, Dzerzhinsky's statue was dumped in a park next to the Central House of Artists, along with Lenins, Stalins and Brezhnevs. The park has been spruced up and turned into a sculpture garden. Dzerzhinsky stands tall there, not far from a new memorial to the victims of Stalin's repression.

Four years ago, the lower house of Parliament, controlled by Communists, voted to restore the statue as ''a symbol of the fight against crime in Russia.'' The effort died, in large part because of Mr. Luzhkov's opposition. Today, the Communist leader, Gennadi A. Zyuganov, welcomed Mr. Luzhkov's reversal.

Yuri V. Samodurov, the director of the museum in Moscow devoted to Andrei D. Sakharov, the famed Soviet dissident, was there the night Dzerzhinsky's statue was pulled down. Today, at the protest rally, he drew a darker conclusion from the efforts to restore the statue.

''It is a sign,'' he said wistfully, ''that in all these years the democracy movement has lost a lot.''
