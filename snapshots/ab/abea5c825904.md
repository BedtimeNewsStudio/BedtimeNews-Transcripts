## Of Tomahawks and Terrorists

 By Lieutenant Commander Thomas J. Cutler, U.S. Navy (Retired) 

October 2026

Proceedings

Vol. 152/10/1,484

[Lest We Forget](https://www.usni.org/taxonomy/term/280)

[View Issue](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october)

[](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october/tomahawks-and-terrorists)[](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october/tomahawks-and-terrorists)[](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october/tomahawks-and-terrorists)[](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october/tomahawks-and-terrorists)[](https://www.addtoany.com/share#url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.usni.org%2Fmagazines%2Fproceedings%2F2026%2Foctober%2Ftomahawks-and-terrorists&title=Of%20Tomahawks%20and%20Terrorists)

[Comments](#block-disqus)

As Commodore of Destroyer Squadron 21, Captain James Stavridis had deployed to the Middle East in summer 1998 as a warfare commander within the USS _Abraham Lincoln_ Carrier Battle Group. The ships had come to the Middle East on a scheduled deployment, but on 20 August, routine operations had given way to preparations for combat.

![Image 8: The Pentagon after the 9/11 attacks and what remains of Admiral James Stavridis’s Naval Academy letter sweater (inset)  found in the ruins of his office. The admiral had been part of U.S. strikes on a bin Laden compound in Afghan­istan three years prior.](https://www.usni.org/sites/default/files/LWF-PRO-10-26-2.jpg)

The Pentagon after the 9/11 attacks and what remains of Admiral James Stavridis’s Naval Academy letter sweater (inset)found in the ruins of his office. The admiral had been part of U.S. strikes on a bin Laden compound in Afghan­istan three years prior. Courtesy of Admiral Stavridis

Thirteen days earlier, truck bombs had exploded outside the U.S. embassies in Nairobi, Kenya, and Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, killing 224 people, including 12 Americans, and wounding thousands more. Responsibility was traced to al Qaeda, an emerging terrorist organization still little known to most Americans.

After consulting his principal national security advisers, President Bill Clinton authorized cruise missile strikes against targets in Afghanistan and Sudan. The operation was code-named Infinite Reach. The _Abraham Lincoln_ Carrier Battle Group was assigned the Afghan portion of the attack, directed against the Zhawar Kili al-Badr training and support complex near Khost. Intelligence suggested Osama bin Laden, leader of al Qaeda, might be there.

In near total darkness, hatches on vertical launch cells on the surface ships opened. Beneath the surface, the USS _Columbia_ (SSN-771) readied her missiles as well. At the appointed moment, 73 Tomahawk land-attack missiles emerged and began their flights across Pakistani airspace toward Afghanistan.

Stavridis and the other commanders then waited for the battle-damage assessment. Initial imagery showed moderate-to-heavy damage throughout the Khost complex. But bin Laden reportedly had left the area only hours before the missiles arrived. To Stavridis, the news was an operational disappointment—not yet an omen.

Operation Infinite Reach had not produced the strategic effect its planners sought. The camps were repaired quickly, and surviving a U.S. missile barrage enhanced bin Laden’s stature in the jihadist world. Recruits and money flowed into Afghanistan, the Taliban solidified its relationship with al Qaeda, and in October 2000, a terrorist attack on the destroyer USS _Cole_ (DDG-67) went unanswered.

On the morning of 11 September 2001, now–Rear Admiral Stavridis sat in his fourth-deck office on the Pentagon’s outer E Ring, trying to make sense of reports emanating from New York. Hoping for clarification, he emerged from his office just in time to see a flash of light across the hall and was immediately slammed to the deck by the impact of American Airlines Flight 77 tearing into the Navy Command Center two decks below. Thousands of gallons of jet fuel ignited, creating a massive conflagration.

Stavridis and others who had survived the blast pulled themselves together and did what sailors are trained to do. They headed for the fire, hoping to fight it and rescue shipmates. But the inferno they encountered dashed all hopes of both. There was nothing they could do but evacuate. Stavridis made his way down the stairs and onto the lawn, where he helped the injured until civilian first responders arrived.

Late that day, when nothing more could be done, he began the 15-mile walk home, his wallet and keys still in his desk drawer. As he made his way along the highways crowded with vehicles, a passerby saw the smoke tarnished uniform and gave him a lift home, where his wife and children had been waiting in dread for news of his fate.

Only later did Stavridis fully comprehend the terrible symmetry of what had happened. In 1998, he had overseen a missile strike intended in part to kill bin Laden. Three years later, bin Laden had nearly killed him.

Stavridis would go on to a distinguished career that included heading “Deep Blue,” a Navy innovation cell charged with developing new strategies and operational concepts for a radically different kind of war.

When permitted to return to what remained of his office, Stavridis found his ruined Naval Academy sweater with his varsity letter still intact. The letter remains on display in his office, a fitting emblem not merely of competition, but of survival in a contest whose stakes had suddenly become terribly real.
