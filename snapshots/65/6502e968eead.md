Dream Chaser[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.7.jpg/250px-Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.7.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.7.jpg) Dream Chaser flight test vehicle in 2013

**Dream Chaser** is an American [reusable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_spacecraft "Reusable spacecraft") [lifting-body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifting-body "Lifting-body") [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane "Spaceplane") developed by [Sierra Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Space "Sierra Space"). Conceived as a [crewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight "Human spaceflight") vehicle, it is being developed in a cargo configuration known as the **Dream Chaser Cargo System** for missions to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") (ISS) under NASA's [Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") program.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-1)

Development of Dream Chaser began in 2004 as a project of [SpaceDev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceDev "SpaceDev"), which was acquired by [Sierra Nevada Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Corporation "Sierra Nevada Corporation") (SNC) in 2008.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AWST-19910715-26-2) In 2021, the program was transferred to Sierra Space, a subsidiary spun off from SNC as an independent company. The first Dream Chaser vehicle, _Tenacity_, was completed in November 2023.

Dream Chaser is designed for [vertical takeoff and horizontal landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTHL "VTHL"), launching atop a [Vulcan Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur "Vulcan Centaur") rocket and landing on conventional runways.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Vulcan-Selection-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-subNAS-4) It is capable of carrying both pressurized and unpressurized cargo. A proposed variant for the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency "European Space Agency") would launch aboard an [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6 "Ariane 6") rocket. The first flight of Dream Chaser was originally scheduled for the second flight of Vulcan Centaur but was not ready in time. As of April 2026, the spacecraft's propulsion system and software had not yet been certified by NASA,[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-5) and the spacecraft's initial mission, [SSC Demo-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSC_Demo-1 "SSC Demo-1") in late 2026, will no longer dock at the ISS as originally planned.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-09252025JonesNASA-6)

The Dream Chaser's design is derived from NASA's [HL-20 Personnel Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HL-20_Personnel_Launch_System "HL-20 Personnel Launch System"), a lifting-body concept studied in the 1980s and 1990s.

## Spacecraft

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Spacecraft")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/66/Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.5.jpg/250px-Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.5.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dream_Chaser_pre-drop_tests.5.jpg) Dream Chaser engineering test article, being driven along the runway by a pickup truck after an atmospheric test

The Dream Chaser [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane "Spaceplane") is designed to be launched on the top of a typical rocket and land like an airplane on a runway. The design has heritage going back decades. Currently, the Dream Chaser will [resupply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_spacecraft "Cargo spacecraft") the ISS with cargo. Per the company's website, a crew version is planned for a later date.

[Sierra Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Space "Sierra Space") was contracted under [CRS-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") to perform resupply missions to the [ISS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station"), but in a September 2025 contract change Sierra Space only needs to perform a free flight demonstration, with NASA optionally booking resupply missions.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-8)

### Propulsion

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Propulsion")\]

On-orbit propulsion of the Dream Chaser was originally proposed to be provided by twin [hybrid rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_rocket_engine "Hybrid rocket engine") capable of repeated starts and throttling. At the time, the SSC's predecessor, the SNC was also developing a [similar hybrid rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RocketMotorTwo "RocketMotorTwo") for Virgin Galactic's [SpaceShipTwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipTwo "SpaceShipTwo").[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-at20120906hybrid-9) In May 2014, SNC involvement in the SpaceShipTwo program ended.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-10)

After the acquisition of Orbitec LLC in July 2014, Sierra Nevada Corporation announced a major change to the propulsion system. The hybrid rocket engine design was dropped in favor of a cluster of Orbitec's Vortex engines. The new unit would be a pressure-fed three-mode engine. At low- and mid-power regimes it uses monopropellant fuel– hydrogen peroxide– and in high-power demand, the engine adds injection of RP-1 fuel. This increased thrust will be useful to shorten the de-orbit burn duration of the Dream Chaser.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-at20140819liquid-11)

### Thermal protection system

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Thermal protection system")\]

Its [thermal protection system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_protection_system "Thermal protection system") (TPS) is made up of silica-based tiles (for most of the belly and upper portion of the heat shield), and a new composite material called Toughened Unipiece Fibrous Reusable Oxidation Resistant Ceramic (TUFROC) to cover the nose and leading edges.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Tile-shop-2015-12)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nasa2018-13)

### Shooting Star module

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Shooting Star module")\]

In 2019, it was announced that an expendable Shooting Star cargo module would be part of the Dream Chaser cargo system for CRS-2 flights.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-spacecomshootingstar-14)[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-wrsshootingstar-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-sncannounceshootingstar-16) The module is a 15-foot-long (4.6m) attachment to Dream Chaser that will allow the spacecraft to carry an additional 10,000 pounds (4,500kg) of pressurized and unpressurized cargo to ISS. The module supports disposal of unwanted cargo by burning up upon re-entry.

In addition to carrying cargo, the Shooting Star module includes solar panels that supply up to 6kW of electrical power. It also supplies active and passive thermal management; provides Dream Chaser translation and rotation capability via six mounted thrusters; and supports berthing or docking (in different configurations) to the ISS. Access from ISS to Dream Chaser will involve crew passing through Shooting Star (which supports a shirt-sleeve environment) and through a hatch that separates Shooting Star from Dream Chaser. Sierra Nevada says the module is capable of additional types of missions in LEO or to cis-lunar destinations; they have developed a free-flying variant with additional capabilities.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-shootingstarbrochure-17)

In July 2020, Sierra Nevada announced a contract with the [Defense Innovation Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Innovation_Unit "Defense Innovation Unit") (DIU) to use its Shooting Star expendable cargo vehicle as a possible commercial solution for a high-powered uncrewed orbital outpost.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-americaspaceshootingstarcontract-18)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: History")\]

The Dream Chaser design is derived from [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA")'s [HL-20 Personnel Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HL-20_Personnel_Launch_System "HL-20 Personnel Launch System") [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane "Spaceplane") concept,[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AWST-19910715-26-2)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-22) which in turn is descended from a series of test vehicles, including the [Northrop M2-F2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_M2-F2 "Northrop M2-F2"), [Northrop M2-F3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_M2-F3 "Northrop M2-F3"), [Northrop HL-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_HL-10 "Northrop HL-10"), [Martin X-24A and X-24B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Marietta_X-24 "Martin Marietta X-24"),[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AWST-19910715-23)[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AWST-19900924-25) and [Martin X-23 PRIME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_X-23_PRIME "Martin X-23 PRIME").[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-WF-1997-26)

The name "Dream Chaser" had been previously used for two separate space vehicle concepts. The first was planned to be an orbital vehicle based on the [HL-20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HL-20_Personnel_Launch_System "HL-20 Personnel Launch System"), with the second suborbital vehicle proposed by the [Benson Space Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benson_Space_Company "Benson Space Company") for the purposes of space tourism.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-at20120906-27)

The Dream Chaser was publicly announced on September 20, 2004.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-28) In April 2007, [SpaceDev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceDev "SpaceDev") announced that it had partnered with the [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance") to pursue the possibility of using the Atlas V booster rocket as the Dream Chaser's launch vehicle.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-9-29) In June 2007, SpaceDev signed a Space Act agreement with NASA.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-10-30)

On October 21, 2008, SpaceDev with Dream Chaser was acquired by the [Sierra Nevada Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Corporation "Sierra Nevada Corporation") for US$38 million.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-SpaceDev_Aquired_by_SNC-31)

### CCDev phase 1

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: CCDev phase 1")\]

On February 1, 2010, [Sierra Nevada Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Corporation "Sierra Nevada Corporation") was awarded $20 million in seed money under NASA's [Commercial Crew Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Development "Commercial Crew Development") (CCDev) phase 1 program for the development of the Dream Chaser.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-12-32)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-13-33) SNC completed the four planned milestones on time, including [hybrid rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_rocket "Hybrid rocket") test fires and the preliminary structure design.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-19-34) Further initial Dream Chaser tests included the [drop test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drop_test "Drop test") of a 15% scaled version at the NASA [Dryden Flight Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryden_Flight_Research_Center "Dryden Flight Research Center").[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Dryden_test-35)

### CCDev phase 2

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: CCDev phase 2")\]

Sierra Nevada proposed Dream Chaser for the CCDev phase2 solicitation by NASA in October 2010, with an estimated project cost of less than $1 billion.[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-2-36)[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-15-37) On 18 April 2011, NASA awarded $80 million to Sierra Nevada Corporation for Dream Chaser.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-ft20110418-38) Since then, nearly a dozen further milestones have been completed under that Space Act Agreement. Some of these milestones included testing of an improved [airfoil fin shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin "Fin"), integrated [flight software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_simulator "Flight simulator") and hardware, [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricycle_landing_gear "Tricycle landing gear"), a full-scale captive carry flight test, and a Systems Requirement Review (SRR).[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-PDR-39)[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-20-40)

By February 2012, Sierra Nevada Corporation stated that it had completed the assembly and delivery of the primary structure of the first Dream Chaser flight test vehicle. With this, SNC completed all 11 of its CCDev milestones that were scheduled up to that point. SNC stated in a press release that it was "on time and on budget."[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-24-41)

On May 29, 2012, the Dream Chaser Engineering Test Article (ETA) was lifted by an [Erickson Skycrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikorsky_S-64_Skycrane#Erickson "Sikorsky S-64 Skycrane") helicopter in a captive carry test to better determine its aerodynamic properties.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-26-42)[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nsw20130514-43) In May 2013, the ETA was shipped to the [Dryden Flight Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryden_Flight_Research_Center "Dryden Flight Research Center") in California for a series of ground tests and aerodynamic [flight tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test "Flight test").[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nsf20130512-44) A second captive carry flight test was completed on August 22, 2013.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-2nd_Captive_Carry_Test-45)

On June 12, 2012, SNC announced the commemoration of its fifth year as a [NASA Langley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Research_Center "Langley Research Center") partner in the design and development of Dream Chaser.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-FiveYearLangely-46) The NASA/SNC team had worked on [aerodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics "Aerodynamics") and [aerothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_heating "Aerodynamic heating") analysis of Dream Chaser, as well as [guidance, navigation, and control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidance,_navigation,_and_control "Guidance, navigation, and control") systems. Together with [ULA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance"), the NASA/SNC team performed [buffet tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffet_(turbulence) "Buffet (turbulence)") on the Dream Chaser and Atlas V stack.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-FiveYearLangely-46)

On July 11, 2012, SNC announced that it successfully completed testing of the nose [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear "Landing gear") for Dream Chaser.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-NoseGear-47) This milestone evaluated the [impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_(mechanics) "Impact (mechanics)") to the landing gear during simulated [approach and landing tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approach_and_landing_tests "Approach and landing tests") as well as the impact of future [orbital flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_spaceflight "Orbital spaceflight"). The main landing gear was tested in a similar way in February 2012. The nose gear landing test was the last milestone to be completed before the free flight approach and landing tests scheduled for later in 2012.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-NoseGear-47) In August 2012, SNC completed CCiCap Milestone 1, or the 'Program Implementation Plan Review'. This included creating a plan for implementing design, development, testing, and evaluation activities through the duration of CCiCap funding.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-MilestoneOverview-48) By October 2012 the "Integrated System Baseline Review", or CCiCap Milestone 2, had been completed. This review demonstrated the maturity of the Dream Chaser Space System as well as the integration and support of the [Atlas V launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V"), mission systems, and ground systems.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-MilestoneOverview-48)

### CCiCap

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: CCiCap")\]

On August 3, 2012, NASA announced the award of $212.5 million to Sierra Nevada Corporation to continue work on the Dream Chaser under the [Commercial Crew Integrated Capability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Integrated_Capability "Commercial Crew Integrated Capability") (CCiCap) Program.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-17-49) On January 30, 2013, SNC announced a new partnership with [Lockheed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin "Lockheed Martin"). Under the agreement, SNC will pay Lockheed Martin $10 million to build the second airframe at its [Michoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michoud "Michoud") facility in [New Orleans, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans,_Louisiana "New Orleans, Louisiana"). This second airframe is slated to be the first orbital test vehicle, with orbital flight testing planned to begin within the next two years.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Flightglobal_LMC-20)

In January 2013, Sierra Nevada announced that the second captive carry and first [unpowered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliding_flight "Gliding flight") [drop test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drop_test "Drop test") of Dream Chaser would take place at [Edwards Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwards_Air_Force_Base "Edwards Air Force Base"), California in March 2013. The spaceplane release would occur at 12,000 feet (3,700m) altitude and would be followed by an [autonomous robotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncrewed_aerial_vehicle "Uncrewed aerial vehicle") [landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing "Landing").[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Flightglobal_LMC-20)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-FloridaTodayLMC-21)

On March 13, 2013, NASA announced that former [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle") [commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander "Commander") [Lee Archambault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Archambault "Lee Archambault") was leaving the agency in order to join SNC. Archambault, a former combat pilot and 15-year NASA veteran who flew on _[Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Atlantis "Space Shuttle Atlantis")_ and _[Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Discovery "Space Shuttle Discovery")_, will work on the Dream Chaser program as a systems engineer and test pilot.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-NASA_Archambault-50)[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-PA_Archambault-51)

On October 26, 2013, the first free-flight occurred. The test vehicle was released from the helicopter and flew the correct flightpath to touchdown less than a minute later. Just prior to landing, the left main landing gear failed to deploy resulting in a crash landing.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Landing_gear_failure_2013-52) The vehicle skidded off the runway in a cloud of dust, but was found upright with the crew compartment intact and all systems inside still in working order.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Press_Conference_2013-10-29-53)[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Video_of_free-flight-54)

In January 2014, SNC announced it had signed a launch contract to fly the first orbital test vehicle on a robotically controlled [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_spaceflight "Orbital spaceflight") [test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test "Flight test") in November 2016.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-bbc20140124-55)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/Dream-chaser-model.jpg/250px-Dream-chaser-model.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dream-chaser-model.jpg) Dream Chaser model being tested at NASA Langley, aboard an [Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V") mock-up

In early 2014, Sierra Nevada completed its wind tunnel testing as part of its CCiCAP Milestone 8. The wind tunnel testing involved analyzing the flight dynamics characteristics that the vehicle will experience during orbital ascent and re-entry. Wind tunnel testing was also completed for the Dream Chaser Atlas V integrated launch system. These tests were completed at NASA Ames Research Center at Moffett Field, California, CALSPAN Transonic Wind Tunnel in New York, and at NASA Langley Research Center Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel in Hampton, Virginia.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nsf20140519-56)

On August 1, 2014, the first completed piece of the orbital Flight Test Article (FTA) composite airframe was unveiled at a Lockheed Martin facility.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-57)

### CCtCap

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: CCtCap")\]

On September 16, 2014, NASA did not select the Dream Chaser for CCtCap, the next phase of the Commercial Crew Program. This occurred despite previous [Commercial Crew Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Development "Commercial Crew Development") awards in every phase since 2009,[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-58) due to lack of maturity.[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-59)

On September 26, Sierra Nevada filed a protest to the [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States "Federal government of the United States") [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office "Government Accountability Office") (GAO).[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-dp20141003-60) On October 22, 2014, a Federal Judge ruled the contract awards to Boeing and SpaceX valid, allowing NASA to proceed.[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-61)

On September 29, 2014, Sierra Nevada introduced the "Dream Chaser Global Project" which would provide customized access to low Earth orbit to global customers.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-62)

Despite not being selected to continue forward under NASA's Commercial Crew transportation Capability (CCtCap) phase of the effort to send crews to orbit via private companies, SNC completed the milestones assigned under earlier phases of the CCP.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-63) On December 2, 2014, SNC announced that it completed NASA's CCiCap Milestone 5a related to propulsion risk reduction for the Dream Chaser space system.[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-64)

By late December, details had emerged that "a high-ranking agency official"—" [William Gerstenmaier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Gerstenmaier "William H. Gerstenmaier"), the agency's top human exploration official and the one who made the final decision"—"opted to rank Boeing's proposal higher than a previous panel of agency procurement experts." More specifically, Sierra Nevada asserted in their filings with the GAO that Gerstenmaier may have "overstepped his authority by unilaterally changing the scoring criteria."[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-pa20141223-65)

On January 5, 2015, the GAO denied Sierra Nevada's CCtCap challenge, stating that NASA made the proper decision when it decided to award Boeing $4.2 billion and SpaceX $2.6 billion to develop their vehicles. Ralph White, the GAO's managing associate counsel, announced that NASA "recognized Boeing's higher price but also considered Boeing's proposal to be the strongest of all three proposals in terms of technical approach, management approach and past performance, and to offer the crew transportation system with most utility and highest value to the government."[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-b237-66) Furthermore, the agency found "several favorable features" in SNC's proposal "but ultimately concluded that SpaceX's lower price made it a better value."[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-b237-66)

### CRS-2 selection

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: CRS-2 selection")\]

Further information: [Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services")

In December 2014, Sierra Nevada proposed Dream Chaser for CRS-2 consideration.[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-67) In January 2016, NASA announced that Dream Chaser had been awarded one of the CRS-2 contracts and committed to purchasing a minimum of six resupply missions to the ISS.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-NASA_News-68) The cargo spacecraft will fly alongside spacecraft from the existing CRS-1 contract holders [SpaceX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX "SpaceX") and [Northrop Grumman Innovation Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman_Innovation_Systems "Northrop Grumman Innovation Systems").[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-69)

In October 2015, the thermal protection system was installed on the Engineering Test Article (ETA) for the next phase of atmospheric flight testing. The orbital cabin assembly of the Flight Test Article (FTA) was also completed by contractor Lockheed Martin.[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-SpaceDaily-2015-10-09-70)

In 2015, the ETA had reportedly been given the name **Eagle**,[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nsf20130512-44) while the FTA was originally named **Ascalon** before being changed to **Ascension**.[\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nasaspaceflight20151006-71)

On November 11, 2017, the Dream Chaser ETA was released from an altitude of 3,700 m and successfully landed at Edwards AFB.[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-72)[\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-73)

In March 2019, completion of NASA's Integrated Review Milestone 5 (IR5) confirmed that development was still on schedule.[\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-74)[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-75) In August 2019, SNC announced the first ISS flight of the Dream Chaser, known as [SNC Demo-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNC_Demo-1 "SNC Demo-1"), was planned for 2021.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Vulcan-Selection-3) However, on November 17, 2020, SNC announced it would be delayed until early 2022.[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-76)

In April 2021 SNC spun off its Dream Chaser division, creating the fully independent [Sierra Space Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Space_Corporation "Sierra Space Corporation"), which assumed responsibility for the further development of the Dream Chaser space vehicle system.

In May 2022, it was announced by the deputy manager of ISS, Dana Weigel, that the mission was scheduled for February 2023.[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-sheetz-20220517-77)

On November 2, 2023, SNC completed assembly of the first Dream Chaser vehicle, _Tenacity_.[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-78)

In May 2024, it was announced that Dream Chaser _Tenacity_ completed initial flight testing and was headed next to Kennedy Space Center to prepare for launch.[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-79) It arrived later that month for launch preparation.[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-80) The Vulcan Centaur to be used for the launch arrived to KSC in late June 2024. However, the _Orlando Sentinel_ reports, June 26, "Dream Chaser dropped from next Vulcan launch as ULA targets national security certification". The mission was subsequently delayed to no earlier than May 2025, and had still not been scheduled for a specific date as of September 2025.[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nasa-20241015-81)

In September 2025, NASA announced that Dream Chaser's demonstration flight, [SSC Demo-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSC_Demo-1 "SSC Demo-1"), will no longer visit the ISS and was delayed to late 2026.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-09252025JonesNASA-6) The spacecraft is also no longer contracted to complete seven resupply missions to the ISS.[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-82)

## Variants

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Variants")\]

### Crewed version

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Crewed version")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Dream_Chaser_Atlas_V_Integrated_Launch_Configuration.tif/lossy-page1-250px-Dream_Chaser_Atlas_V_Integrated_Launch_Configuration.tif.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dream_Chaser_Atlas_V_Integrated_Launch_Configuration.tif) Artist's conception of the Dream Chaser Space System in the launch configuration of the [Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V")

The originally planned Dream Chaser Space System is a [human-rated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human-rated "Human-rated") version designed to carry from three to seven people and cargo to orbital destinations such as the International Space Station.[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-vid-83) It was to have a built-in [launch escape system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_escape_system "Launch escape system")[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-at20120906hybrid-9) and could fly autonomously if needed.[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Mark-84) Although it could use any suitable launch vehicle, it was planned to be launched on a [human-rated Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V#Human-rating_certification "Atlas V") N12 rocket.[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Mark-84)[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-AutoIQ-6-85) The vehicle will be able to [return from space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_reentry "Atmospheric reentry") by [gliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliding_flight "Gliding flight") (typically experiencing less than 1.5 [g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force "G-force") on re-entry) and landing on any airport [runway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runway "Runway") that handles commercial air traffic.[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-David-86)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-aw20100219-19) Its [reaction control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_control_system "Reaction control system") thrusters burned ethanol-based fuel,[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Mark-84)[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-David-86) which is not an explosively volatile material, nor toxic like [hydrazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrazine#Rocket_fuel "Hydrazine"), allowing the Dream Chaser to be handled immediately after landing, unlike the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle").[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Mark-84)

As of 2020,[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit) the Sierra Nevada Corporation said it still planned to produce a crewed version of the spacecraft within the next 5 years. The company said it "never stopped working" on the crewed version and fully intended to launch it after the cargo version,[\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-87) and is still committed to the crewed version as of 2021.[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit)[\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-88)

In November 2021, Sierra Space Corporation reported that it received a $1.4 billion investment in Series A funding, which it would use to develop a crewed version of Dream Chaser and fly astronauts by 2025, although this ultimately did not occur.[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-89) On October 25, 2021, [Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Origin "Blue Origin") and [Sierra Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Space "Sierra Space"), released their plan for a commercial space station.[\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-90) The station, called [Orbital Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Reef "Orbital Reef"), is intended as a "mixed-use business park".[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-91) Sierra Space Corporation's Dream Chaser was chosen as one of the commercial spacecraft to transport commercial crew to and from the space station, along with [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing "Boeing")'s [Starliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Starliner "Boeing Starliner").[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-92)

### CRS-2 cargo version

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: CRS-2 cargo version")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Dream_Chaser_Docked_to_ISS.jpg/250px-Dream_Chaser_Docked_to_ISS.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dream_Chaser_Docked_to_ISS.jpg) Artist's conception of the crewed Dream Chaser docked to [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station")

The cargo version of the SSC Dream Chaser is called the Dream Chaser Cargo System (DCCS) and after development is completed, will fly resupply flights to the [ISS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS "ISS") under NASA's [Commercial Resupply Services-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_Transfer_Bag_Equivalent "Cargo Transfer Bag Equivalent") program. Featuring an expendable cargo module mounting [solar panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panels "Solar panels"), the spacecraft will be capable of returning 1,750kg (3,860lb) to Earth while undergoing maximum re-entry forces of 1.5G.[\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-DCCS-93)[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-SpaceNews-2015-03-13-94)

To meet CRS-2 guidelines, the cargo Dream Chaser will have folding wings and fit within a 5 m diameter [payload fairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload_fairing "Payload fairing"), in contrast to the Crewed Dream Chaser, which is intended to launch without a fairing. The ability to fit into a payload fairing allows the cargo version to launch on any sufficiently capable vehicle, such as the (retired) [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5 "Ariane 5") as well as the (soon to be retired) [Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V"). An expendable cargo module will launch attached to the back of the spacecraft, expanding the cargo uplift capacity and supporting the disposal of up to 3,250kg (7,170lb) of trash. Total uplift is planned for 5,000kg (11,000lb) pressurized and 500kg (1,100lb) unpressurized, with a downlift of 1,750kg (3,860lb) contained within the spaceplane.[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-SpaceNews-2015-03-17-95) The expendable cargo module is called "Shooting Star".[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-sn20200114-96)

On August 14, 2019, it was announced that all six Dream Chaser CRS-2 flights would be carried into orbit by ULA's [Vulcan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_(rocket) "Vulcan (rocket)") launch vehicle, with the first Dream Chaser flight being the second Vulcan flight in late 2021.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Vulcan-Selection-3)[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-97) However, on February 9, 2022, Ken Shields, Sierra Space's Director of Commercial Market Development, announced that the first flight would be pushed to January 2023.[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-spaceexplored1-98) The launch has been further delayed; as of October2024,[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit) it is scheduled for no earlier than May 2025,[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-nasa-20241015-81) and as of February 2026, the mission is targeted to launch in late 2026.[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-99)

## Dream Chaser Global Project

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Dream Chaser Global Project")\]

In December 2013, the [German Aerospace Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Aerospace_Center "German Aerospace Center") (DLR) announced a funded study to investigate ways in which Europe might take advantage of the Dream Chaser crewed spaceplane technology. Named the [DC4EU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC4EU "DC4EU") (Dream Chaser for European Utilization), the project will study using it for sending crews and cargo to the ISS and on missions not involving the ISS, particularly in orbits of substantially greater altitude than the ISS can reach.[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-pa20131216-101)

In January 2014, the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency "European Space Agency") (ESA) agreed to be a partner on the DC4EU project, and will also investigate whether the Dream Chaser can use ESA avionics and docking mechanisms. ESA will also study launching options for the "Europeanized" Dream Chaser, particularly whether it can be launched from the [Guiana Space Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Space_Centre "Guiana Space Centre"), within the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5 "Ariane 5")'s large aerodynamic cargo fairing – or, like the Atlas V, without it. In order to fit within the fairing, the Dream Chaser's wing length will have to be reduced slightly, which is thought to be easier than going through a full aerodynamic test program to evaluate and prove it along with the Ariane for flight without the fairing.[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-ESA_signs_agreement_2014-102) The Ariane 5 launch vehicle was designed from its inception to be crew rated, in order to launch the [Hermes Spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_(spacecraft) "Hermes (spacecraft)"), an ESA crewed vehicle which was proposed in the 1980s and 1990s, but was cancelled.

In late January 2014, it was announced that the Dream Chaser orbital test vehicle was under contract to be launched on an initial [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_spaceflight "Orbital spaceflight") [test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test "Flight test"), using an [Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V") rocket, from [Kennedy Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center "Kennedy Space Center") in November 2016. This was a [privately arranged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_spaceflight "Private spaceflight") commercial agreement, and was funded directly by Sierra Nevada and was not a part of any existing NASA contract.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-bbc20140124-55)

In September 2014, SNC announced that it would, with global partners, use the Dream Chaser as the baseline spacecraft for orbital access for a variety of programs, specializing the craft as needed.[\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-SNC-2014-09-29-103)

On November 5, 2014, SNC's Space Systems team publicly presented the challenges and opportunities related to landing the Dream Chaser spacecraft at public-use airports.[\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-104) Dream Chaser uses standard landing aids and non-toxic propellants that require no special handling.[\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-105)

### Dream Chaser for European Utilization

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Dream Chaser for European Utilization")\]

On February 3, 2015, the Sierra Nevada Corporation's (SNC) Space Systems and [OHB System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OHB_System "OHB System") AG (OHB) in Germany announced the completion of the initial Dream Chaser for European Utilization (DC4EU) study.[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-106) The study found that Dream Chaser is suitable for a broad range of space applications and could be used to advance European interests in space.[\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-deselding2015-107) The cooperation was renewed in April 2015 for an additional two years.[\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-deselding2015-107)\[ _[needs update](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_\]

### United Nations

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dream_Chaser&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: United Nations")\]

The [United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Office_for_Outer_Space_Affairs "United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs") (UNOOSA) selected the cargo Dream Chaser for its first space launch. This launch is intended to last for at least two weeks in [freeflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-g_environment "Micro-g environment") to provide space access to [United Nations member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_United_Nations "Member states of the United Nations") that have no space programs of their own, and carry up to 35 payloads.[\[108\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-UNOOSA-108) The [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") will pay for the mission and provide all support facilities.[\[109\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser#cite_note-Observer-2016-10-03-109)
