Top Questions

### When did Yoweri Museveni become president of Uganda?

Yoweri Museveni became president of [Uganda](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uganda) on January 26, 1986, after his resistance movement prevailed in a guerrilla war against the regime of [Milton Obote](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milton-Obote). Museveni declared himself president at this time and was later elected to the post on May 9, 1996. He has since been reelected multiple times, remaining the current president of Uganda.

### What have been Yoweri Museveni’s main focuses during his presidency?

Yoweri Museveni has focused on rebuilding the economy, providing political stability, and improving infrastructure. He has also implemented capitalist reforms and combated [AIDS](https://www.britannica.com/science/AIDS). Though he initially supported a free press, media freedom has diminished during his time as president.

### What controversies has Yoweri Museveni face during his presidency?

Yoweri Museveni has faced controversies over corruption and the failure to eliminate the [Lord’s Resistance Army](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lords-Resistance-Army) militant group. He has been criticized for his administration’s growing intolerance of dissenting views and for not relinquishing power. He has been accused of grooming his son, [Muhoozi Kainerugaba](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Muhoozi-Kainerugaba), as his successor.

**Yoweri Museveni** (born 1944, Mbarra district, Uganda) is a politician and former rebel leader who has served as [president](https://www.britannica.com/topic/president-government-official) of [Uganda](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uganda) since 1986. Though he has been praised for restoring political [stability](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/stability) and promoting [economic growth](https://www.britannica.com/money/economic-growth) during his presidency, he has also been criticized for government corruption, an increasing intolerance of dissent, and his unwillingness to relinquish power. He was most recently reelected in the election held on January 15, 2026.

## Early life and resistance activity

Museveni was born to cattle farmers and attended missionary schools. While studying [political science](https://www.britannica.com/topic/political-science) and [economics](https://www.britannica.com/money/economics) at the University of [Dar es Salaam](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dar-es-Salaam) (B.A., 1970) in [Tanzania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tanzania), he became chairman of a leftist student group allied with African liberation movements. When [Idi Amin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Idi-Amin) came to power in Uganda in 1971, Museveni returned to Tanzania in exile. There he founded the Front for National Salvation, which helped overthrow Amin in 1979.

 

Museveni held posts in transitional governments and in 1980 ran for president of Uganda. When the elections, widely believed to have been rigged, were won by [Milton Obote](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milton-Obote), Museveni and former president [Yusufu Lule](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yusufu-Lule) formed the [National Resistance Movement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Resistance-Movement) (NRM); Museveni led the NRM’s armed group, the National Resistance Army, which waged a guerrilla [war](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war) against Obote’s regime. The resistance eventually prevailed, and on January 26, 1986, Museveni declared himself president of Uganda.

## Presidency

Over the next several years, Museveni focused on rebuilding the economy and tamping down pockets of resistance in the [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state). A new constitution was [promulgated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/promulgated) in 1995, and elections were held in 1996 in which he was elected president in May and his backers won control of the [National Assembly](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Assembly-historical-French-parliament) in legislative elections held the following month. Museveni was reelected in 2001 and again in 2006 after a [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) [amendment](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/amendment) passed the previous year had eliminated established [term limits](https://www.britannica.com/topic/term-limits) for the presidency. He was reelected again in 2011 and 2016, although the opposition and international observers noted problems with the polling process in both elections. A constitutional amendment passed in 2017 removed the age limit for presidential candidates that had been set at age 75 while also reimposing a limit of two terms, clearing the way for an aging Museveni to stand for the next two presidential elections. He was reelected in 2021 and 2026, but the campaign period prior to the polls were criticized as being repressive.

 

As president, Museveni helped revitalize the country, providing political stability, a growing economy, and an improved [infrastructure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/infrastructure). He instituted a number of capitalist reforms and supported a free press. Although Museveni initially rejected multiparty [democracy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/democracy), arguing that it would degenerate into tribal politics in a poor African country, he accepted the results of a 2005 referendum that overwhelmingly supported a return to multiparty politics; the next year the country held its first multiparty elections since 1980. Museveni also [implemented](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implemented) measures to combat [AIDS](https://www.britannica.com/science/AIDS). Uganda, in fact, was one of the first African countries to have success battling the illness. In 2021 Museveni appointed [Robinah Nabbanja](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Robinah-Nabbanja) as prime minister; she was the first woman to hold the position in Uganda.

 

Corruption, however, was an issue in Uganda under Museveni. Over the years, foreign and domestic support for Museveni waned in some quarters, corruption being cited as one of the problems. Museveni’s growing intolerance with dissenting views was another commonly cited criticism: as he continued to cling to power, there were increasing reports of opposition figures—notably, Kizza Besigye and, later, [Bobi Wine](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bobi-Wine)—and their supporters being [harassed](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/harassed), detained, and even abducted. He also came under fire for his lack of success with eliminating the [Lord’s Resistance Army](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lords-Resistance-Army) (LRA), a militia led by [Joseph Kony](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Kony) that terrorized northern Uganda for decades. Although the LRA was largely forced out of the country by 2006, the group continued to commit atrocities in neighboring countries.

 

In his [foreign policy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/foreign-policy), Museveni often generated controversy by supporting rebels in other African countries. He backed [Laurent Kabila](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Laurent-Kabila), who deposed [Mobutu Sese Seko](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mobutu-Sese-Seko) in neighboring Zaire (now the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Democratic-Republic-of-the-Congo)) in 1997, the [Tutsi](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tutsi) exiles who were fighting against the government of [Rwanda](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rwanda), and a group, headed by one of his former schoolmates, battling the Islamic fundamentalist rulers of [Sudan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sudan). Museveni justified his support of rebels by stating that his goal was to achieve regional [integration](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integration) in both politics and economics and that the downfall of corrupt regimes was necessary to bring about such a union. He garnered praise for contributing troops to the [African Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/African-Union)’s force in [Somalia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Somalia) but elicited some [criticism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/criticism) for supporting the government of [South Sudan](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Sudan) in the civil war that erupted in that country in late 2013.

Museveni came under fire for not relinquishing power—which was [ironic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ironic), given that early in his political career he had complained about leaders who “overstayed” their time in power. Although he had won reelection multiple times, his victories were tainted by allegations of voting irregularities. Critics also took issue with the rising profile of his son, [Muhoozi Kainerugaba](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Muhoozi-Kainerugaba), and accused Museveni of grooming his son to be his successor. Kainerugaba, a military officer, had risen rapidly in the ranks and in 2024 was made chief of Uganda’s defense forces. He was known for making inflammatory statements on the [social media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-media) platform [X (formerly Twitter)](https://www.britannica.com/money/Twitter), which sometimes led to [diplomatic](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/diplomatic) controversies.
