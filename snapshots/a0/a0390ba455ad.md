# Baruch S. Blumberg

American physician

Also known as: Baruch Samuel Blumberg

[History](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Baruch-S-Blumberg/additional-info#history)

**Baruch S. Blumberg** (born July 28, 1925, [Brooklyn](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brooklyn-borough-New-York-City), [New York](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-York-state), U.S.—died April 5, 2011, Moffett Field, near Mountain View, California) was an American research physician whose discovery of an antigen that [provokes](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/provokes) antibody response against [hepatitis B](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis-B) led to the development by other researchers of a successful [vaccine](https://www.britannica.com/science/vaccine) against the [disease](https://www.britannica.com/science/disease). He shared the [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://www.britannica.com/science/Winners-of-the-Nobel-Prize-for-Physiology-or-Medicine-1856944) in 1976 with [D. Carleton Gajdusek](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Daniel-Carleton-Gajdusek) for their work on the origins and spread of infectious viral diseases.

Blumberg received an M.D. degree from [Columbia University](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Columbia-University)’s College of Physicians and Surgeons in 1951 and a Ph.D. degree in [biochemistry](https://www.britannica.com/science/biochemistry) from the [University of Oxford](https://www.britannica.com/topic/University-of-Oxford) in 1957. In 1960 he became chief of the Geographic Medicine and Genetics Section of the [U.S. National Institutes of Health](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Institutes-of-Health), Bethesda, Maryland. In 1964 he was appointed associate director for clinical research at the Institute for Cancer Research (later named the Fox Chase Cancer Center) in Philadelphia. He also served as professor of medicine, [human genetics](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-genetics), and anthropology at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://www.britannica.com/topic/University-of-Pennsylvania). In 1989 Blumberg became the first Fox Chase Distinguished Scientist, and he returned to Oxford to become master of Balliol College, a position that he held until 1994. Upon his return to the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), he continued to teach as professor of medicine and anthropology at the University of Pennsylvania. From 1999 to 2002 Blumberg served as director of the [National Aeronautics and Space Administration](https://www.britannica.com/topic/NASA) (NASA) Astrobiology Institute, where he embarked on investigations into the possibility of life on other planets. He held several different positions while at NASA, where he remained until 2004. The following year he was elected president of the [American Philosophical Society](https://www.britannica.com/topic/American-Philosophical-Society); he held the post until his death.

In the early 1960s Blumberg was examining blood samples from widely [diverse](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diverse) populations in an attempt to determine why the members of different ethnic and national groups vary widely in their responses and susceptibility to disease. In 1963 he discovered in the blood serum of an Australian Aboriginal person an [antigen](https://www.britannica.com/science/antigen) that he later (1967) determined to be part of a [virus](https://www.britannica.com/science/virus) that causes [hepatitis B](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis-B), the most severe form of [hepatitis](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis). The discovery of that so-called Australian antigen, which causes the body to produce antibody responses to the virus, made it possible to screen blood donors for possible hepatitis B transmission. Further research indicated that the body’s development of an antibody against the Australian antigen was protective against further infection with the virus itself. In 1982 a safe and effective vaccine [utilizing](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/utilizing) the Australian antigen was made commercially available in the United States. Blumberg’s book on his Nobel Prize-winning work, *Hepatitis B: The Hunt for a Killer Virus*, was published in 2002.

Top Questions

### How does a person become infected with hepatitis B?

Hepatitis B virus (HBV), the cause of hepatitis B, spreads through contact with infected [blood](https://www.britannica.com/science/blood-biochemistry), [semen](https://www.britannica.com/science/semen), or vaginal fluids. Worldwide, most infections occur through mother-to-child transmission at birth or as a result of early childhood exposure. HBV can also spread via sexual contact, shared needles, or occupational exposure. It is not transmitted through food, water, kissing, coughing, or shared utensils.

### What happens when a person tests positive for hepatitis B?

When a person tests positive for hepatitis B, follow up with doctor for a medical evaluation is essential to determine whether the infection is acute (short-term) or chronic (long-term). Hepatitis B progresses through acute and chronic phases. After incubation, acute infection often causes few symptoms, though some experience [fatigue](https://www.britannica.com/science/fatigue-physiology), [nausea](https://www.britannica.com/science/nausea-pathology), [jaundice](https://www.britannica.com/science/jaundice), or abdominal [pain](https://www.britannica.com/science/pain). Symptoms usually last one to four months; in rare instances, [liver](https://www.britannica.com/science/liver) failure occurs. In some cases, the virus persists, causing chronic infection; this is most common in those infected at birth and is rare in adults.

### Does hepatitis B go away completely?

For some individuals, hepatitis B resolves on its own, but the outcome largely depends on the age at which infection occurs. Adults typically develop an acute, short-term infection that the [immune system](https://www.britannica.com/science/immune-system) can clear within about six months. If the virus remains beyond that period, it becomes chronic, often persisting for life—especially in those infected during infancy or early childhood. The majority of persons with chronic hepatitis B may remain asymptomatic for 20 or 30 years.

### How long can a hepatitis B patient live?

While most adults recover fully from an acute hepatitis B infection, those with chronic disease can still live long, healthy lives with ongoing medical care and treatment. Although chronic infection raises the risk of serious liver complications such as [cirrhosis](https://www.britannica.com/science/cirrhosis) or [cancer](https://www.britannica.com/science/cancer-disease), consistent monitoring, therapy, and healthy habits greatly reduce those dangers. Treatments, such as oral [antiviral drugs](https://www.britannica.com/science/antiviral-drug), are highly effective in controlling the virus, slowing the progression of liver disease, and reducing the risk of [liver cancer](https://www.britannica.com/science/liver-cancer).

**hepatitis B**, [infectious disease](https://www.britannica.com/science/infectious-disease) of the [liver](https://www.britannica.com/science/liver), the causative agent of which is known as [hepatitis B virus](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis-B-virus) (HBV). The course and severity of [illness](https://www.britannica.com/science/disease) associated with HBV [infection](https://www.britannica.com/science/infection) varies widely. Some persons are asymptomatic, for example, whereas others experience [acute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/acute) illness and eliminate the virus from the body. Still others remain infected and develop chronic disease.

The global burden of disease associated with chronic HBV infection is high. In 2022, there were an estimated 1.2 million new cases, and worldwide, an estimated 254 million people are chronically infected with HBV. In about one-third of those individuals—many of whom live in eastern Asia or sub-Saharan Africa—chronic HBV infection leads to the development of complications such as liver failure or [liver cancer](https://www.britannica.com/science/liver-cancer). The [World Health Organization](https://www.britannica.com/topic/World-Health-Organization) (WHO) has estimated that worldwide, 1 in 3 individuals is infected with either HBV or [hepatitis C](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis-C) virus (HCV).

## Transmission of HBV

In infected individuals, HBV is present in [blood](https://www.britannica.com/science/blood-biochemistry), [semen](https://www.britannica.com/science/semen), and vaginal fluids, and the virus is [transmitted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/transmitted) through contact with those fluids. Globally, perinatal transmission (infection passed from mother to child at the time of birth) and early childhood transmission (via contact with an infected individual) are the major means of HBV infection. Occupational exposure (e.g., in [health](https://www.britannica.com/topic/health) care settings), sexual contact, and needle sharing (e.g., among intravenous drug users) are other ways in which HBV can be transmitted to uninfected individuals. HBV cannot be spread in food or water, and, although the virus may occur in saliva, it is not spread through kissing, coughing, sneezing, or contact with shared eating utensils.

## Symptoms of HBV infection

Hepatitis B has both acute and chronic phases. Acute illness [manifests](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/manifests) after an incubation period of one to several months. In most individuals, the acute phase is subclinical, with few or no apparent symptoms of illness. A minority of persons, however, develop indications of icteric [hepatitis](https://www.britannica.com/science/hepatitis), with a prodromal syndrome characterized by anorexia, [nausea](https://www.britannica.com/science/nausea-pathology), [fatigue](https://www.britannica.com/science/fatigue-physiology), and skin rash. Symptoms of liver disease emerge as the condition progresses and include dark [urine](https://www.britannica.com/science/urine), pale stools, [jaundice](https://www.britannica.com/science/jaundice) with icterus (jaundice of the sclera of the [eye](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-eye)), and [pain](https://www.britannica.com/science/pain) in the abdomen. Acute symptoms last anywhere from one to four months. Rarely, acute infection leads to fulminant liver failure, with widespread [necrosis](https://www.britannica.com/science/necrosis) of the liver and encephalopathy resulting in confusion or [coma](https://www.britannica.com/science/coma-pathology).

 

In some instances, following acute infection, HBV remains in the body, leading to [chronic](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/chronic) infection. Whether chronic infection will develop is determined primarily by the age at which a person becomes infected with HBV. Risk for chronic infection is highest among individuals who are infected perinatally and lowest for individuals who are infected in adulthood.

 

Chronic infection is defined by the onset of symptoms six months beyond the emergence of acute illness. However, similar to the acute phase, chronic infection may produce subclinical symptoms; the majority of persons with chronic hepatitis B may remain asymptomatic for 20 or 30 years. In roughly one-fifth of cases, chronic hepatitis B ultimately progresses to [cirrhosis](https://www.britannica.com/science/cirrhosis) (scarring of the liver). Chronic hepatitis B infection is a significant cause of liver cancer, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma.

## Diagnosis and treatment of hepatitis B

The presence of HBV in the body results in the production of [antibodies](https://www.britannica.com/science/antibody) against the virus. Examples of antibodies that target HBV include HBsAG (hepatitis B surface antigen), anti-HBc (total hepatitis B core antibody), and HBeAg (hepatitis B e antigen). Different blood tests have been designed to detect the different antibodies and thus are used to diagnose hepatitis B.

In acute HBV infection, [treatment](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/treatment) (when needed) typically is supportive, centerd primarily on adequate rest and fluid intake. Treatment for chronic HBV infection varies. Agents such as lamivudine and [interferon](https://www.britannica.com/science/interferon) alfa-2b disrupt viral reproduction, enabling the liver to recover some of its function. Some patients develop resistance to lamivudine, requiring the use of a different [antiviral drug](https://www.britannica.com/science/antiviral-drug), such as adefovir or entecavir, alone or in combination with lamivudine. Liver transplantation may be considered in cases of chronic liver failure.

## Prevention of hepatitis B

[![Gloved hands draw liquid from a vial into a syringe, overlaid on a digitally colored microscopic image of a virus with round particles in clusters.](https://cdn.britannica.com/78/267078-138-2BE6AC8F/how-are-vaccines-developed.jpg?w=800&h=450&c=crop)](/video/how-are-vaccines-developed/-327945)

Hepatitis B can be prevented through vaccination. The hepatitis B vaccine typically is given as a series of three or four injections, administered over the course of six months. The first dose of the vaccine is sometimes called the birth dose, since it frequently is given within the first 24 hours of birth. The vaccine is 90 to 95 percent effective in preventing hepatitis B.
