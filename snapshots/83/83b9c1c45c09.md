# Urban-rural Europe - income and living conditions

Data extracted: June 2026

Planned article update: September 2027

## Highlights

By global standards, most people living in the [European Union (EU)](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:European_Union_(EU) "Glossary:European Union (EU)") are prosperous: they have relatively high income/wealth and a positive sense of well-being, while there are established social protection systems that provide a safety net for people who are less well off.

Indeed, the European social model is based on offering protection to people who are most in need. Regardless of national differences, the implementation of social protection in EU countries is designed to provide people with some protection against, among other issues, the costs of bringing up a family or housing, the risks related to unemployment, poor health, poverty or social exclusion, and the consequences of old age. Nevertheless, 92.7 million people in the EU’s population, were [at risk of poverty or social exclusion](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:At_risk_of_poverty_or_social_exclusion_(AROPE) "Glossary:At risk of poverty or social exclusion (AROPE)") in 2025 (see Infographic 1); this equated to around 1 in 5 (20.9%) people.

The COVID-19 crisis underlined systemic inequalities in living conditions both between and within individual EU countries. While some people were fortunate enough to continue working full-time from home (and in some cases were even able to save more of their income than usual), many people in precarious employment or working in sectors/businesses particularly impacted by successive lockdowns faced reduced earnings, short-time work and unemployment. As the impact of the pandemic abated, concern shifted – from early 2022 onwards – to the cost-of-living crisis. This led several EU governments to introduce a range of support schemes, such as energy price caps, rebates for fuel prices or additional support for low income [households](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Household_-_social_statistics "Glossary:Household - social statistics") / pensioners (who typically spend a disproportionate amount of their income on essential purchases such as housing, food and energy).

This article forms part of [Eurostat](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Eurostat "Glossary:Eurostat")’s twin publications on *[Rural Europe](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Rural_Europe "Rural Europe")* and *[Urban Europe](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Urban_Europe "Urban Europe")*.

## People at risk of poverty or social exclusion

The number of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion is a headline indicator for social protection and inclusion within the [European pillar of social rights](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/european-pillar-of-social-rights). This indicator monitors the progress towards 1 of the EU’s headline 2030 targets, namely, to reduce the number of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion by at least 15 million (when compared with 2019).

**More about the data: people at risk of poverty or social exclusion**

The risk of poverty and social exclusion is not dependent solely on a household’s level of income, as it may also reflect joblessness, very low work intensity, working status, or a range of other socioeconomic characteristics. Across the EU, the share of people who were at risk of poverty or social exclusion declined for 4 consecutive years between 2015 (the first reference year for which data are available) and 2019. This pattern was largely repeated when analysed by [degree of urbanisation](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Degree_of_urbanisation "Glossary:Degree of urbanisation") (see Figure 1); the only exception was for people living in [cities](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:City "Glossary:City"), where there was no change in the share of people who were at risk of poverty or social exclusion between 2018 and 2019.

With the onset of the COVID-19 crisis, the share of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion in the EU increased in 2020 across all degrees of urbanisation: by 0.5 [percentage points](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Percentage_point "Glossary:Percentage point") among people living in cities, and by 0.4 points among people living in [towns and suburbs](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Town_or_suburb "Glossary:Town or suburb") and [rural areas](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Rural_area "Glossary:Rural area"). Developments in 2021 were more mixed. The share continued to increase among people living in towns and suburbs (up 1.3 points), remained unchanged among city dwellers and declined among those living in rural areas (down 0.8 points), for the latter falling below its pre-pandemic level.

In 2022, the share of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion in towns and suburbs continued to rise, although at a slower pace (up 0.2 points). This was followed by declines in 2023 and 2024, before a marginal increase in 2025. By contrast, the share fell consistently between 2022 and 2025 among people living in cities, and between 2021 and 2024 among those living in rural areas (before a modest increase in 2025).

Overall, the share of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion fell 0.1 percentage points in 2025. This reflected a decline of 0.3 points among people living in cities, while marginal increases of 0.1 points were recorded among those living in towns and suburbs and in rural areas. ### At-risk-of-poverty rate

The at-risk-of-poverty rate (after social transfers) is 1 of the 3 criteria used to identify people at risk of poverty or social exclusion. It identifies the proportion of the population which lives in a household with an annual equivalised disposable income that is below 60% of the national median (a threshold which is set separately for each EU country). As such, this indicator provides a measure of relatively low income compared with other residents of the same country; it does not necessarily imply a low overall standard of living.

In 2016, the EU’s at-risk-of-poverty rate by degree of urbanisation peaked at 20.4% among people living in rural areas; the lowest rate in that year was recorded among people living in towns and suburbs (15.9%), a gap of 4.5 percentage points; see Figure 3. During the 7 years that followed, there was initially a step-like decrease followed by a continuous fall in the risk of poverty for people living in rural areas, whereas for people living in cities there was almost no change. Recent developments for people living in towns and suburbs were different: a clear fall was observed between 2017 and 2019 and a notable increase in 2021. In 2025, the gap between the highest and lowest at-risk-of-poverty rates was 1.2 points, with a peak of 17.2% for people living in rural areas and lows of 16.0% among people living in towns and suburbs and people living in cities.

### Severe material and social deprivation rate

The severe material and social deprivation rate is also 1 of the 3 criteria used to identify people at risk of poverty or social exclusion. It provides information on the share of the population who could not afford (rather than did not want or did not need) at least 7 out of 13 surveyed items. In 2025, the EU’s severe material and social deprivation rate was 6.3%, which was 0.1 percentage points lower than the year before.

During the period 2019 to 2025, there was an initial period of convergence in the EU’s severe material and social deprivation rates when analysed by degree of urbanisation (see Figure 4) through to 2023, when there was 0.3 percentage points difference between the 3 rates. ### People living in households with very low work intensity

The final criteria used to identify people at risk of poverty or social exclusion is the share of people aged less than 65 years living in households where the adults (aged 18 to 64 years; excluding students aged 18 to 24 years and retired people) worked no more than 20% of their total potential during the previous 12 months. Within the EU, there were 26.1 million people who were in this situation in 2025, equivalent to 7.9% of all people aged less than 65 years. The share of people living in households with very low work intensity was higher for people living in cities (8.7%) than it was for people living in towns and suburbs (8.0%) or in rural areas (6.5%).

During the period 2015 to 2025 (see Figure 5), the share of people aged less than 65 living in EU households with very low work intensity generally followed a downward trend from 2015 to 2019 across all 3 degrees of urbanisation, but thereafter, developments diverged.

The difference in the share of people living in households with very low work intensity by degree of urbanisation widened to 3.8 percentage points in 2021, with the highest share recorded among people living in cities and the lowest share among people living in rural areas. The difference between these 2 shares narrowed in each of the 4 subsequent years, with a gap of 2.2 points by 2025.

## Housing

Demographic changes have led, in some cases, to a mismatch between the demand for and supply of housing. Some people face overcrowded conditions. A growing proportion of people in the EU are living alone, with some older people living in houses or flats that they previously occupied with a larger family.

In 2025, 33.4% of the EU’s population lived in an [under-occupied dwelling](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Under-occupied_dwelling "Glossary:Under-occupied dwelling"). This share was considerably higher among those living in rural areas, where living space tends to be more abundant. Indeed, 41.3% of the EU’s rural population lived in an under-occupied dwelling, compared with 35.6% for those living in towns and suburbs and 26.2% for people living in cities; see Figure 6.

Across most EU countries, it was also generally the case that the highest proportion of people living in under-occupied dwellings was recorded for those living in rural areas. In 2025, this was the situation in 26 out of the 27 EU countries; the only exception was Hungary, where a higher share of people living in cities were living in under-occupied dwellings.

In 2025, there were 12 EU countries where more than half of the rural population was living in an under-occupied dwelling. Cyprus, Ireland and Belgium had shares from 71.1% to 72.8%, while a peak of 73.3% was recorded in the Netherlands.

There was no distinct pattern concerning the change in the share of people living in under-occupied dwellings between 2024 and 2025, as 15 EU countries reported a mix of positive and negative rates of change for the 3 categories within the degree of urbanisation classification. In France, Italy, Hungary, Austria, Romania, Slovakia and Sweden, the share of people living in under-occupied dwellings increased for all 3 categories; in Belgium, Spain, Luxembourg and Portugal, it fell in all categories; in Germany, the share increased in 2 categories and was unchanged for people living in towns and suburbs.

## Income

The issue of inequality has gained increasing importance in political and socioeconomic discourse

[GDP](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Gross_domestic_product_(GDP) "Glossary:Gross domestic product (GDP)") per inhabitant has traditionally been used to assess divergence/convergence in mean living standards between countries. However, it does not capture the distribution of income within a population and thereby does little to reflect economic inequalities.

[Equivalised disposable income](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Equivalised_disposable_income "Glossary:Equivalised disposable income") is the total income of a household, after tax and other deductions, that is available for spending or saving, divided by the number of household members, where the number has been converted into equivalent adults; household members are made equivalent by weighting each according to their age, using the so-called modified [OECD](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development_(OECD) "Glossary:Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)") equivalence scale.

In 2025, [median](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:Median "Glossary:Median") equivalised net income in the EU was €22 939 per year. Figure 9 shows that people living in cities and in towns and suburbs generally had a higher level of income than those living in rural areas. Incomes grew at a relatively steady pace across the EU between 2013 and 2020. In 2021, there was a slowdown or a reversal of the development, which may be linked (at least in part) to the COVID-19 crisis. This was followed by renewed income growth across all 3 degrees of urbanisation from 2022 to 2025.

In 2025, median equivalised net income in the EU ranged from €20 994 per year among people living in rural areas up to €23 722 per year for people living in cities; the income for people living in towns and suburbs was almost as high, at €23 524 per year. As such, the median equivalised net income of people living in cities was 1.13 times as high as that for people living in rural areas.

In all EU countries, median equivalised net incomes for males were higher than those for females. This was also observed for nearly all EU countries in each of the 3 degrees of urbanisation. There were only 2 exceptions, both in Malta: the median equivalised net income was €593 higher for females than for males for people living in towns and suburbs; the medians were the same for both sexes in rural areas. In absolute terms, the largest gender gaps were recorded among people living in the rural areas of Luxembourg and the Netherlands, in the towns and suburbs of Denmark and Austria, as well as in the cities of Lithuania and Latvia. The unequal distribution of income can, among other consequences, lead to economic inequalities that manifest themselves in various forms of poverty or social exclusion. In 2025, almost 1 in 5 (18.9%) of the EU’s population had a level of income that was at least 150% of the median income. By this measure, the distribution of income was less equitable across those living in cities, where almost 1 in 4 (23.5%) of the population had a level of income that was at least 150% of the median income, with lower shares recorded among those living in towns and suburbs (17.5%) and, most notably, rural areas (13.6%).

Figure 13 shows that income inequalities (as measured by the share of people having an income at least 150% of the median) were generally concentrated in cities. In 2025, this was particularly the case in Bulgaria, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Hungary, Latvia and Romania where 30.0% or more of the population living in cities had an income that was at least 150% of the median. There were 2 EU countries where the highest share was not observed for cities: in Malta, a higher share of the people living in rural areas (32.1%) had a level of income that was at least 150% of the median income, while in Belgium the highest share was recorded among people living in towns and suburbs (14.4%).

## Context

On 4 March 2021, the [European Commission](/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Glossary:European_Commission_(EC) "Glossary:European Commission (EC)") set out its ambition for a stronger social EU to focus on education, skills and jobs, aiming for a fair, inclusive and resilient socioeconomic recovery from the COVID-19 crisis, while fighting discrimination, tackling poverty and alleviating the risk of exclusion for vulnerable groups. The [European pillar of social rights action plan](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=COM:2021:102:FIN) (COM(2021) 102 final) outlines a set of specific actions and headline targets for employment, skills and social protection in the EU. It includes a benchmark target for reducing the number of people at risk of poverty or social exclusion by at least 15 million people between 2019 and 2030 (of which, at least 5 million should be children).
