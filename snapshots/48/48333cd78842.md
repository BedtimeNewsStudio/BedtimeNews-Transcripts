# BACKGROUND NOTE 1:

# MONGOLIA CCDR MACROECONOMIC MODELLING BACKGROUND PAPER

*Xinru Lin, Ira Dorband, Hector Pollitt*

###### 1. Introduction

This note describes the macroeconomic modelling that was carried out for the Mongolian Country 
Climate Development Report (CCDR). The following section introduces the scenarios and discusses how 
the model inputs that were used to present them. Section 3 describes the MINDSET model that was 
used to carry out the analysis. Results are presented in Section 4 and key conclusions in Section 5.

###### 2. Scenarios

The scenarios can be categorized into three groups:

• Climate impacts

• Adaptation measures

• Mitigation actions

As well as estimates of mean climate impacts, there is a ‘shock’ scenario that could be described as a 
plausible worst outcome case (Box 1). The shock scenario uses the same modelling approach as the one 
that estimates mean outcomes. It is discussed in more detail in a separate background paper to the 
CCDR.

###### 2.1 Climate impacts and adaptation

There are two ways to estimate the impacts of climate change on an economy. The top-down approach 
uses historical data to identify an empirical relationship between temperature change and aggregate 
economic indicators (usually GDP). A range of global estimates is provided in Kahn et al (2019). The 
approach has been criticized for extrapolating the small temperature changes seen so far, and using 
spatial variation as a proxy for temporal variation (e.g. Keen, 2020).

In line with other CCDRs, the estimates of climate change on Mongolia’s economy are estimated using a 
bottom-up approach. In this approach, a series of impact channels is defined and the estimated effects 
through each channel are aggregated to provide a total economic impact. This approach is more 
appropriate for the CCDRs because a) it can give a more detailed description of impacts, for example 
across economic sectors; and b) it is easier to identify potential adaptation options and other policy 
responses when the impacts are properly defined.

In the Mongolia CCDR five impact channels are defined:

---

• Dzuds

• Floods

• Tourism

• Human health

• Labor productivity (heat stress)

The assessment approach for each channel is described further below. All quantification in this section 
assumes that no climate adaptation measures are taken. The quantification relates to direct production 
losses only; indirect effects are estimated by the MINDSET model. All estimates are for damages beyond 
those already experienced in 2023, meaning that results provide the *additional* costs of climate change.

###### 2.1.1 Dzuds

The effects of dzuds are felt primarily by the livestock sector. In 2009, 9.7m animals (22 percent of total 
herd size) perished in dzud conditions. Other dzuds since 2000 (when three occurred in succession) have 
led to smaller numbers of animal deaths, and there can be large numbers of deaths even in non-dzud 
years.

Although there is a high range of uncertainty, both the intensity and frequency of dzuds is expected to 
increase by mid-century. Under a high emission scenario (which lies between RCP6.0 and RCP8.5 and 
has expected average global warming of 3.5°C–4°C by 2100), dzuds could occur once every three years 
by 2050 (although counting non-dzud harsh winters, it could be every other year or even more often). 
The intensity of dzuds could double by 2100, potentially lasting for longer and covering a wider area of 
the country.

$$
3.5^{\circ}C-4^{\circ}C
$$

These findings are derived from MET (2018) but have been updated to reflect more recent data and 
hence provide different projections. Based on a herd size of 71m, average annual deaths could reach 
4.1m by 2050, which would make 2024 look like a normal year.

The effects of dzuds are modelled as losses of agricultural production and textile exports. The MINDSET 
model includes a detailed disaggregation of livestock, so separate values are entered for cattle, sheep 
and ‘other animals’ (including goats). The proportional loss of production is matched to the share of 
livestock perishing. The proportional loss of textiles imports is matched to the loss of goats, reflecting 
wool as well as meat that cannot be salvaged.

###### 2.1.2 Floods

The risk of economic loss from flooding is related mainly to UB. Potential economic damages are thus 
allocated to all sectors; even agriculture and mining operations have some presence in the city. Data are 
taken from the WRI Aqueduct model using the metric ‘affected GDP’, which also includes projections of 
how the severity of floods may worsen because of climate change (both in terms of increased severity 
and duration). The share of affected GDP is translated into direct production losses by sharing across the 
sectors proportionately.

---

Despite the growing intensity and frequency of floods, the expected macroeconomic costs remain small. 
By 2030, floods could subtract 0.3 percent of GDP. By 2050 the figure is 0.5 percent.

###### 2.1.3 Tourism

The effects of climate change on tourism in Mongolia are ambiguous. Tourism is not a major sector in 
Mongolia, with the harsh winter weather making the country an unsuitable destination for much of the 
year. Climate change could make the worst conditions even less welcoming, but could also extend 
warmer periods. Climate change may also make competing (hotter) tourism destinations less hospitable 
to visitors.

The estimates of the tourism impacts are derived from Roson and Sartori (2016), which applies an 
econometric assessment to link tourism to climate change. The usual caveats about using past 
relationships to estimate future trends under different conditions apply. Overall, under a scenario where 
global temperatures increase by 4°C, tourism revenues could increase by 4.8 percent in the projection 
period. This figure is scaled to match the warming expected in the climate impact scenario. Production 
in the hotels and catering sector is adjusted accordingly.

###### 2.1.4 Human health

The method for modelling health impacts is the same as that for tourism, and also takes results from 
Roson and Sartori (2016), scaled to match the temperature change in the modelled scenario. Health 
impacts are expected to cost 0.5 percent of GDP by 2030 and 1.0 percent by 2050. They are entered into 
the model as an increase in the cost of production to simulate loss of productivity.

The health impacts cover only include those directly related to climate change. They do not include 
health impacts resulting from poor air quality. As described in the CCDR, the impacts of poor air quality 
from burning coal in Mongolia are substantial. One of the key co-benefits of reducing coal consumption 
would be improvements to air quality and human health.

###### 2.1.5 Labor productivity (heat stress)

Health impacts affect labor productivity, but labor productivity is also impacted directly by higher 
temperatures. Outdoor labor may become difficult at certain times when there are high temperatures 
and high levels of humidity. Data on potential loss of labor productivity are taken from the NGFS 
scenarios, as developed by Climate Analytics.<sup>1</sup> Again, the values are scaled to be consistent with the 
climate scenario.

The net effects, once the damages already seen in 2023 are accounted for, are relatively small. 
Productivity is impacted by 0.2 percent in 2030 and 0.4 percent in 2050. These are entered into the 
model as an increase in the cost of production for all sectors.

###### Box 1: The Shock Scenario

The mean estimates of climate damages have persistently produced small costs to GDP, regardless of 
the type of modelling approach applied (World Bank, 2023a). Such results may lead to a false sense of 
security and give the impression that climate change is not a threat to social and economic 
development.

To counter this threat, and to acknowledge the underlying uncertainty about climate change, the 
question addressed in the modelling was changed from ‘what *will* happen’ to ‘what *might* happen’. 
The plausible worst-case scenario was developed to address this question. It draws on the approaches 
described in Trust and others (2024) and recognizes the ‘tail-risk’ that low-probability events may 
occur more often than expected in complex systems.

The approach was developed for CCDRs in the Cambodia CCDR (World Bank, 2023b) and applied for 
Mongolia. The Mongolian shock scenario includes:

• A complete loss of coal exports to China over three years

• A repeat of the dzuds that occurred in 2000-2002

• A large flood in UB like the one that occurred in 1966

The shock scenario is assessed using the MINDSET model. The coal export loss is modelled as an 
exogenous change in trade patterns. The dzuds and floods are modelled as described in this 
background paper. A separate background paper describes the scenario in more detail and presents 
results from the modelling exercise.

###### 2.2 Adaptation scenario

As described in the CCDR, the main benefits of implementing climate adaptation measures is to reduce 
Mongolia’s vulnerability to large economic shocks. However, adaptation measures will also reduce the 
mean expected impacts of climate change. Two adaptation measures are considered in the scenario:

• Irrigation to allow additional crops to be grown and stored, providing some protection from 
dzuds

• Flood prevention through regulation and new infrastructure

Although there is considerable uncertainty about how much of the impacts from future dzuds could be 
offset by better adaptation measures, a previous modelling exercise in GIZ (2023) suggests that up to 50 
percent of the negative impacts could be avoided. We use the same assumptions of costs and avoided 
damages in the scenario here.

The impacts of future floods could also be reduced through careful urban planning, enforcement of
building regulations, and climate-proofing of infrastructure. The risk of damages to new infrastructure 
could be reduced substantially at a relatively low cost with planning, but it is more difficult to climateproof existing infrastructure. To minimize costs, it is critical to identify which infrastructure would be 
vulnerable. Estimates of costs and avoided damages are taken from World Bank (2017). The figures used 
apply to new infrastructure only and so are weighted by the anticipated share of new infrastructure in 
the capital stock over the projection period. This estimate is based on expected future investment rates 
(derived from World Bank, 2019) and a depreciation rate of 2 percent. By 2030, around 70 percent of 
infrastructure could be climate-proofed. By 2050 around 94 percent could be. It is estimated that climate-proofed infrastructure could reduce damages by 62 percent. The public investment is financed 
by a small increase in income taxes.

###### 2.3. Mitigation scenario

The mitigation scenario aims to achieve a substantial reduction in greenhouse gas emissions over the 
period to 2050. It does not achieve net-zero emissions because of difficulties in fully decarbonizing heat 
and electricity supply (see main CCDR text), but it does put Mongolia well on the pathway to net-zero 
emissions.

The mitigation scenario incorporates the results from the energy system modelling, which is described in 
a separate background paper. The inputs to the macroeconomic model are the power mix, the 
investment requirements, and the impact on electricity prices. It is assumed that the power sector 
passes on all investment and operating costs to electricity prices.

Electricity and heat generation account for the largest share of emissions in Mongolia, but three other 
policies are added:

• Removal of energy subsidies by 2030

• Carbon pricing from 2030 to 2050

• Acceleration of electric vehicle (EV) uptake

The carbon prices are applied in the form of a tax, reaching $30/tCO2 by 2050. They are applied to all 
sectors except copper extraction. The reason for excluding copper extraction is that it would be subject 
to its own royalty payments, set at rates in line with global copper prices. An additional carbon tax risks 
making Mongolia’s extraction non-viable in a sector it could be aiming to expand (see CCDR main text). 
All revenues from the carbon tax are split three ways to maintain revenue neutrality: one-third to higher 
government current expenditure, one-third to reduce income tax rates, and one-third to reduce 
employers’ payroll taxes.

The uptake of EVs is based on two principles. First the large share of vehicles that is imported from 
Japan. Second the rate of technology diffusion within Japan. Figure 1 shows the pathways that are used. 
The starting point is Japan’s expected uptake of EVs under a global low-carbon scenario that meets the 
Paris targets. This pathway is derived from the FTT model of technology diffusion (Mercure, 2012; 
Mercure and others, 2018).

In the baseline case, it is assumed that these cars appear in Mongolia after 13 years (Mendjargal and 
others, 2022). In the mitigation scenario, the time lag is reduced to 7 years on the basis that incentives 
are made to encourage more rapid adoption. The difference between the black and green lines on the 
chart thus define the scenario inputs.

It is assumed that further measures to ensure EV viability, notably ability to handle extreme cold 
temperatures, are added, but these are not costed.

The main inputs to the macroeconomic model are the changes in energy use patterns and the 
investment costs. Public investment costs are funded by a small increase in income tax rates to maintain 
revenue neutrality.

###### 3. Modelling approach

MINDSET<sup>2</sup> is a macro-econometric input-output model that assesses the impacts of climate change, 
adaptation measures, and mitigation strategies with high sectoral and regional granularity. The model 
covers 120 economic sectors in 164 countries.

Quantifying the sectoral implications of induced structural change is essential for informing climate 
change adaptation and mitigation policies, and complementary social & labor policies, which are 
cognizant of both “just transition” and competitiveness considerations. The model supports multi- or 
single-country policy scenario analyses, including impact analyses from third-party policies.

The model informs a broad range of climate impacts as well as fiscal, price-based, and regulatory 
policies (see **Error! Reference source not found.).** It may be linked to the FTT<sup>3</sup> models of technology 
diffusion to capture within-sector transitions in the electricity and transport sectors. Sector- and 
country-specific, single or multi-country scenarios may be assessed. Key outputs from the model include 
standard macroeconomic indicators, plus sectoral emission levels, production, and employment. By 
linking to national labor force and household survey data, impacts on workers and consumers can be 
analyzed across income strata, skill levels, occupation types, and subnational provinces, providing 
relevant information for active labor market and reskilling interventions and sectoral or regional 
government support funds.

MINDSET is empirically grounded and combines the strengths of input-output analysis with those of a 
demand-led macro-econometric model. Based on the global GLORIA multi-regional input-output 
database<sup>4</sup> (Lenzen et al., 2017, 2021) and linked to IEA energy balances, the core element of the model 
is the detailed network of linkages between sectors and countries along global value chains.

Model results capture first-round direct, indirect, and induced supply-chain impacts that feed into fiscal 
multipliers. MINDSET incorporates sectoral and cross-country spillovers, including from climate change, 
from domestic policies affecting competitiveness like carbon pricing, and from trade-related measures
like Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanisms (CBAM). Unlike single-country and/or aggregate models it 
does not assume fixed linkages between countries or sectors. Also, the model assumes bounded 
rationality and knowledge limitations. The ability to inform policymakers about short-term distributional 
and sectoral frictions sets it apart from general equilibrium models and aggregate models.

In the input-output transition, MINDSET assumes that the economy operates below full capacity (i.e. not 
at the efficiency frontier in the baseline), where the derived demand will be met by additional supply.  In 
contrast to equilibrium assumptions, it assumes excess labor supply at existing wages (involuntary 
unemployment or underemployment) and no crowding out of investment (spare economic capacity<sup>5</sup>). 
unconstrained labor supply (involuntary unemployment) as opposed to crowding out. Scenarios where 
capacity constraints may impact transitions require off-model analysis. Introducing supply-side 
constraints, such as from immobility of labor and climate damages, is under development or at an early 
implementation phase.

###### 4. Simulation results

###### 4.1 Climate impacts and adaptation

The climate impact scenario shows minimal effects on total GDP and employment. Based on the 
estimates from the literature described in Section 2.1, Mongolia would be relatively unaffected by 
climate change in the medium and long term. Overall, GDP would be 0.3 percent and 0.7 percent lower 
in 2030 and 2050, respectively, compared to a ‘no climate change’ baseline scenario.

Similarly, employment effects would be small (Table 2). Partly, the small aggregate effects are due to 
large underlying heterogeneities between sectors: while agricultural sectors would be impacted 
negatively by 1.1 percent of sectoral GDP in 2030, the accommodation and food service sector would 
grow by almost 4 percent. This is driven by the assumption that, as temperatures in Mongolia increase, 
more tourists would visit the country year-round (see Section 2.1).

Despite the small climate impacts, the adaptation investments and expected reductions in impacts do 
not fully compensate for climate losses. GDP would still be 0.1 and 0.4 percent smaller than without 
climate change in 2030 and 2050, respectively.

Small aggregate effects mask larger differences between sectors, however (

Figure 2). The negative effects of climate change in terms of sectoral GDP losses are largest in the 
agriculture and advanced manufacturing sectors: GDP would be 1.1 percent and 0.4 percent lower 
compared to the reference case in 2030. The adaptation measures considered (see Section 2.2) benefit 
mostly the agricultural sector, avoiding over 90 percent of the losses, such that climate impacts are 
reduced to 0.1 percent of agricultural GDP. Losses in other sectors are reduced slightly less in relative 
terms. Adaptation investments, such as in irrigation infrastructure, give a positive boost to the 
construction sector.

###### 5. Conclusions

**This background paper describes the macroeconomic modelling that was carried out for the CCDR.** The

modelling covers climate impacts, climate adaptation measures, and climate mitigation policies. In each 
case the modelling estimates the impacts on macroeconomic indicators like GDP, sectoral impacts, and 
labor market outcomes.

**The core tool that was used for the analysis is the MINDSET macroeconomic model.** MINDSET is an 
input-output model with a high level of sectoral disaggregation. It is a non-equilibrium model, meaning 
that it does not assume full use of available resources. The model thus does not rule out by assumption 
the possibility of double dividend effects, in which there are improvements in both economic and 
environmental outcomes, compared to the baseline case.

**The mean estimates of expected damages from climate change on Mongolia are modest, highlighting 
the priority to focus on risk reduction.** Overall, the mean expected economic costs of climate change in 
Mongolia are expected to be less than 1 percent of GDP. Some of the higher costs of more frequent 
dzuds could be outweighed by higher revenues from tourism. However, there is considerable 
uncertainty about the scale of all impacts and Mongolia remains highly vulnerable to climate-related 
shocks. These shocks are also modelled using MINDSET, and described in a separate background paper.

**Adaptation measures could further reduce climate damages and, importantly, reduce risk exposure.**
For a relatively modest cost, adaptation measures could reduce Mongolia’s exposure to the threats from 
more intense dzuds and more frequent floods. Irrigation could boost the production of animal feed, 
which would give some security in winter months for herders. Active enforcement of 
building/development regulations could reduce exposure to future floods, particularly in the ger areas.

---

**Measures to reduce emissions would have a small economic cost.** The modelling found that measures 
to reduce GHG emissions in Mongolia would lead to slightly lower GDP than there is in the baseline case. 
However, the difference between scenario and baseline is less than 1 percent. Key sensitivities include 
how copper production might respond to greater environmental regulation or carbon pricing. As 
described in the background paper on key risks, Mongolia’s coal exports (more than 10 percent of GDP) 
depend on future climate policy in China.

**Overall, the macroeconomic modelling highlights the importance of reducing exposure to climaterelated risks.** Most of the impacts found in this background paper are modest in size, although there 
may be some larger localized impacts. Losses of GDP that are less than 1 percent by 2050 pale into 
insignificance compared to the potential GDP losses of more than 20 percent in the 2030s in the 
plausible worst-case outcome (see separate background paper). The main conclusion from the 
modelling exercise is thus that the focus of climate policy should be in reducing exposure to climaterelated risks. Climate adaptation measures and diversification of economic production will play a key 
role in reducing these risks.

# BACKGROUND NOTE 2:

#### HOW BAD COULD IT GET? ASSESSING MONGOLIA’S ‘WORST CASE PLAUSIBLE’ SCENARIO

###### Migle Petrauskaite, Hector Pollitt

###### 1. Introduction

**Mongolia is grappling with numerous challenges, including escalating climate change impacts, macro 
financial vulnerabilities, and under-diversified exports, leaving it susceptible to simultaneous shocks.**
This note explores a ‘worst-case plausible’ scenario where a series of catastrophic events unfolds in 
rapid succession. It is informed by the principles outlined in Trust and others (2024) (see Box 1. Climate 
Scorpion) and acknowledges the rising likelihood of more frequent and severe climate-related shocks, to 
the extent that their convergence becomes a foreseeable threat. This scenario is not intended as a 
forecast but rather to illustrate what could happen without adequate preparation to mitigate future 
losses.

**The planet is witnessing severe climate-related impacts that are manifesting at lower temperature 
increases than previously expected** (Ripple and others 2023). Historical ‘tail’ risks–unlikely events 
occurring, generally in the ‘tails’ of a probability distribution–are hence more likely to materialize. Given 
the intricate and interconnected nature of these risks, they pose a direct threat to the stability of our 
social and economic systems. Adopting a systems approach is essential to assess how these 
interconnected risks may amplify societal impacts (Trust and others 2024).

**Complicating the global response to climate change is the likely surpassing of the 1.5°C temperature 
threshold.** Observations in 2023 suggest that we are rapidly approaching, if not already reaching, this 
critical limit (Armstrong McKay and others 2022). Adding to the concern is emerging evidence suggesting 
that the Earth’s climate sensitivity may be greater than anticipated (Trust and others 2024). This implies 
that for a given concentration of greenhouse gases, the resultant warming could be more severe. The 
precise degree of warming we are committed to post-2030 remains uncertain, influenced by many 
factors, such as the potential crossing of climate tipping points and the pace at which the energy 
transition progresses.

**The World Bank’s Country Climate and Development Reports (CCDRs) consider economic development 
in the context of a changing climate and growing imperatives to curb greenhouse gas emissions.** The 
reports published so far suggest that by 2050, the impact of climate change on global GDP could range 
from minimal to a decrease of up to 12 percent. If we set aside the ‘pessimistic’ projections, the 
expectation is that no country will experience a GDP reduction of more than 7 percent by that time. The 
mean estimates of climate damages in Mongolia, for example, which exclude the possibility of large 
climate shocks, suggest only small negative average impacts of up to 1 percent of GDP. Considering the anticipated overall economic growth by 2050, one might conclude that climate change and related 
economic repercussions are of minor concern. However, such conclusions do not reflect Mongolia’s 
exposure to large, and potentially compounding, economic shocks, which could severely impact the 
country’s development trajectory. Such conclusions are also at odds with the sense of urgency conveyed 
by the scientific community, as well as with public sentiment—climate surveys in Mongolia show that an 
overwhelming majority of the population, 92.7 percent, express concern about climate issues (GGGI 
2023).

**As we near the 1.5°C increase, the risk of concurrent climate shocks intensifies.** This note thus explores 
the potential impact of possible negative ‘tail’ outcomes. In complex systems, including both our natural 
environment and economies, such tail outcomes have become much more likely than are suggested by 
the normal distributions typically used in economic modelling exercises (see e.g., Beinhocker 2007). 
Models are representations of reality, meaning that their results reflect the model’s assumptions and 
structure rather than reality itself. Consequently, interpreting model results requires bridging the gap 
between the model and the real world; otherwise, the gap could potentially lead to inaccurate 
probability estimates of adverse events (Trust and others, 2024). When our goal is to manage risk, 
underestimating these probabilities poses a greater issue than overestimating them.

**This note highlights a series of events that have become increasingly probable over time.** According to 
the 2023 State of the Climate Report, “We are entering uncharted territory. Extreme events will increase 
in frequency and magnitude as the planet warms further” (Ripple and others, 2023). Extreme events 
occurring simultaneously could lead to severe outcomes. Our focus in this note is on a dzud (a harsh 
winter disaster), a severe urban flood, and China halting its imports of Mongolian coal—all occurring 
over a hypothetical three-year period in the near future. While not all three events are equally likely, the 
probability of their occurrence increases due to the higher likelihood of ‘tail’ events in a rapidly changing 
climate. The probability of the entire scenario occurring may be higher than the simple multiplication of 
each individual event’s probability. An unstable climate amplifies the likelihood and severity of all three 
events.

**Although this note includes some quantitative analysis, its purpose extends beyond merely projecting 
greater GDP impacts from climate change on the CCDR for Mongolia.** It seeks to identify critical 
vulnerabilities within the country that could be exacerbated by climate change. Effective climate policy 
should focus on the ‘tail’ of the probability distribution to limit the likelihood of severe outcomes. The 
models predicting (usually low) climate impacts on GDP may thus not be asking the right questions. The 
primary question should be, “How bad could it get?” By pinpointing these vulnerabilities, we can assess 
proactive measures to mitigate and prepare for future risks. Given Mongolia’s limited influence on the 
pace of global climate change, these measures are vital for the country’s long-term strategy to develop a 
resilient economy.

**This note considers the combined effect of three crises, and how the worst-case outcomes could be 
avoided.** In Section 2, we analyze each of the three adverse events, while Section 3 presents a shock 
scenario where each of these events contributes to a bigger crisis: a dzud leading to the death of 
millions of cattle; a severe flooding event debilitating Ulaanbaatar; and a sudden drop in the demand for 
Mongolian coal exports to China. Section 4 then discusses how to build a strategy to prepare for and 
protect against the risk of such disasters occurring in Mongolia. The final section draws broader 
conclusions from the exercise.

###### 2. Disaster scenarios

###### 1.1 Extremely harsh winter kills millions of cattle

**Mongolia suffers from extremely harsh winter conditions that kill large numbers of livestock.** Locally

called a ‘dzud’, an extremely harsh winter is a compound hazard that occurs in a cold, dry climate. It 
involves a mix of drought, extreme cold, windstorms, and unusually heavy snowfall, making it difficult for livestock to access enough grass and other plants to eat. It can last throughout the year and lead to 
widespread death of livestock during the winter and spring. The most severe dzuds, where large 
numbers of animals die, often happen after a dry growing season that has already weakened the 
available pastures, combined with particularly severe winter weather (Natsagdorj and Dulamsuren 2001; 
Nandintsetseg, Shinoda, and Erdenetsetseg 2018). Since 2000, dzuds have occurred in one out of five 
years. Figure 5 shows the share of livestock lost each winter over the past three decades, as well as 
future projected losses.

**Dzuds have become more frequent and severe over time, with mounting livestock losses putting 
pressure on the country’s agricultural sector.** Animal husbandry accounts for over 80 percent of 
Mongolia’s agricultural output and animal products contribute around 10 percent to national exports 
(FAO 2018). The economic impact of dzuds can thus been profound: in the harsh winter of 2000-2001, 
which affected over 90 percent of Mongolia’s territory, the damage reached 7.5–11.5 percent of the 
country’s GDP (Myagmarsuren and Galtbayar, 2021). There was a substantial drop in agricultural 
productivity—the sector’s contribution to the economy fell from 37 percent in 1999 to 20.7 percent in 
2002, a 44 percent decrease. Similarly, following the 2009-2010 dzud, when 80 percent of the territory 
was affected and almost 23 percent of the total livestock was lost, the agricultural sector’s contribution 
fell by 35 percent while the growth rate turned negative, contributing to a downturn in overall GDP 
growth. The damage amounted to 6.2 percent of national GDP (Ministry of Environment and Tourism 
(MET) 2018), with six provinces reportedly losing over 30 percent of their livestock (Rao, Mukund Palat 
and others, 2015). In the most recent dzud of 2023-2024, more than 6.6 million livestock perished.

**Future projections are alarming, suggesting that by the end of this century, under the present planet 
warming rate, the frequency of dzuds is expected to rise by up to 40 percent** (MET 2018). As a result, livestock losses are anticipated to increase by around 50 percent by 2050 and could potentially double 
by 2100. This means that by mid-century, Mongolia could be losing up to 4.1 million livestock *per year,* 
and this figure could grow to over 6.8 million per year by 2100 (amounting to 9.6 percent of total 
livestock). The loss of such a vast number of livestock would substantially reduce Mongolia’s agricultural 
output, export revenues, and incomes for herders. This would mean that the estimated 6.6 million 
animals (9.7 percent of total livestock) lost in the most recent dzud of 2023-2024 could become the 
expected average in the coming decades while extreme cases like the 2010 dzud that killed 22 percent 
of stock of animals could become even worse (World Bank 2024).

###### A repeat of the 1966 urban flood

**Flooding in Mongolia, which historically occurred once every three years, has increased in frequency 
over the past decade.** The unpredictability of rainfall patterns continues to exacerbate the risk of 
flooding. A 2015 study estimated that over 200,000 residents of Ulaanbaatar—15 percent of the 
capital’s population—live in areas classified as medium to high flood risk (GFDRR 2015). Many of those 
exposed are considered poor and have limited capacity to cope with floods. Many schools, hospitals, 
and other pieces of key urban infrastructure are in flood-prone areas, increasing the vulnerability of 
urban residents to the impacts of flooding (World Bank, 2022).

**One of the biggest rainfall floods in the modern era occurred in 1966 in the Tuul River basin.** On July 
10-11, 1966, Ulaanbaatar recorded a daily rainfall of 103.5 mm—nearly half of what the city usually gets 
in an entire year. Flood water velocity reached 4-5 meters per second, with a discharge of 1,700 cubic 
meters per second—enough water to fill an Olympic-sized swimming pool in less than a minute. The 
water level rose by 151 cm within a single day. This caused considerable loss of life and property, 
severely disrupting socio-economic functions and necessitating international assistance.

**Since then, changing rainfall patterns around Ulaanbaatar, combined with rapid urbanization, have 
led to an increase in flooding within the city.** The duration of rainfall floods has decreased by 2-3 days, 
but their intensity has gone up, meaning that there is more intense rainfall happening in a shorter 
amount of time. The percentage of heavy rainfall during the warm period has also notably increased, by 
at least 18 percent (Natsagdorj, Gomboluudev, and Batima 2005). While the level of annual precipitation 
has not changed dramatically in the past 40 years, deluges are replacing the light rain that previously 
would fall for more than a week (Vandandorj and others 2017). In 2004, for example, only nine incidents 
of heavy rains and floods were recorded by Mongolia’s National Emergency Management Agency 
(NEMA). In the 20 years since, that number has risen, with official data recording 72 disasters in 2021 
alone (PreventionWeb 2022).

###### As erratic rainfall events become more intense in a changing climate, a repeat of a 1966 flood

**becomes more likely.** If no preemptive measures are taken, Ulaanbaatar could experience a rapid rise in 
water levels, surpassing hazardous thresholds swiftly. The potential human impact might exceed that of 
1966, with considerable loss of life in the increasingly densely populated capital. Ulaanbaatar’s 
population has grown from 200,000 in 1996 to 1.67 million in 2023. Of those residing in high-risk flood zones, 64 percent now live in ger<sup>8</sup> districts, which are especially susceptible to flooding. Since 2004, over 
3500 gers have been destroyed by floods and heavy rain in Mongolia, with more than half of this 
damage occurring in the last four years (PreventionWeb 2022).

###### Coal exports to China drop

**Mining is a cornerstone of Mongolia’s economy, with its importance growing steadily since the mid-**
**2000s.** Over the last decade, the mining sector, especially in copper and coal, has constituted nearly a 
quarter of the economy, with coal alone making up a third of that share. The sector has attracted over 
70 percent of foreign direct investment (FDI), accounted for more than 90 percent of export revenues, 
and contributed 30 percent of government revenues. Recent increases in FDI inflows have focused on 
developing infrastructure and production capacities, supporting projects like the underground mine 
stage of Oyu Tolgoi (OT), one of the world’s largest copper mines, and the construction of railway lines 
to enhance coal export logistics.

**However, Mongolia’s heavy reliance on mining has led to an undiversified economy that is vulnerable 
to macroeconomic instability.** Large investments in the mining sector have hindered the growth of nonmining sectors, limiting the creation of high-quality jobs and increasing susceptibility to external shocks 
(World Bank 2020a). Fluctuations in mining production and mineral prices have exacerbated 
macroeconomic volatility, which has been further intensified by pro-cyclical macroeconomic policies. 
Historically, fiscal expenditures have tended to rise during economic expansions, worsening economic 
cycles and limiting the accumulation of fiscal buffers needed during downturns.

**Over 90 percent of Mongolia’s coal exports go to China, making its economy vulnerable to China’s 
climate change mitigation efforts.** China takes almost all of Mongolia’s coal exports. However, it has 
pledged in its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) to reduce the share of fossil fuels in primary 
energy consumption to 75 percent by 2030, down from 81 percent in 2023 (Energy Institute, 2024). Coal 
consumption is projected to decline by 0.7 percent annually until 2050 (Figure 6A). Decarbonization 
could reduce coal use in Chinese steel production by 45 percent by 2050, with a more pronounced 
decline afterward (Figure 6B). Additionally, Mongolia’s coal exports face challenges from reduced 
Chinese construction activity, shifts in trade policy, and payment timing issues under coal offtake 
agreements. Thus, Mongolia’s economy could experience large negative spillovers from unexpectedly 
slow growth in the Chinese economy.

**Although China is Mongolia’s key market for coal exports, Mongolian coal only accounts for a small 
share of China’s coal imports.** While the recent boom in mining exports is supporting macroeconomic 
conditions in Mongolia, without reforms to diversify the Mongolian economy and solidify economic 
resilience to commodity cycles, these positive developments can be short-lived. Mongolia thus needs a 
new growth model for long-term sustainable development (World Bank 2023).

###### 3. The shock scenario – or <em>A Tail of Three Events</em>

**A plausible worst-case scenario could see a convergence of these low-probability but high-impact 
events, resulting in a 20 percent shock to Mongolia’s annual GDP over a three-year period** (Figure 7).
In this scenario all three events occur in quick succession: a series of consecutive dzuds killing millions of 
cattle; a severe flood, like that in 1966, sweeping Ulaanbaatar; and coal exports to China suddenly 
dropping. The loss of coal exports alone would have substantial economic impact but, if it coincides with 
a major natural disaster, it would stretch Mongolia’s coping mechanisms. Each of these events would 
have profound implications for Mongolia’s economic stability, and their simultaneous occurrence would 
compound the impacts across multiple sectors. The drop in output could reach 21 percent and 
unemployment could rise to 14 percent.

**A severe dzud could result in a 26.4 percent reduction in livestock.** This loss would be catastrophic for 
Mongolia’s agricultural sector, which relies heavily on livestock. But the economic fallout from extensive 
dzud-induced livestock losses extends far beyond the immediate impacts on agriculture, rippling 
through the entire Mongolian economy and society. The dzud would severely impact the livelihoods of 
herders, reduce meat and dairy production, and consequently lead to a substantial decrease in 
agricultural exports. The sector could see a 31.2 percent decrease in output and a 20 percent reduction 
in useful employment. As cattle are an important export commodity, the dzuds would severely hit 
Mongolia’s trade balance and reduce foreign exchange earnings.

**The resultant income loss for herders, a critical segment of the rural economy, could lead to 
heightened poverty levels in these communities.** About 35 percent of Mongolia’s workforce relies on 
herding, with 63 percent of rural household assets being livestock. Herding accounts for a third of 
Mongolia’s employment, yet 30 percent of herders live in poverty, worsening the impact of dzuds on 
livelihoods, health, and urban migration. After the early 2000s dzuds, national unemployment rose to 
4.6 percent in 2001, and rural poverty increased from 32.6 percent in 1998 to 43.4 percent in 2003 
(Myagmarsuren and Galtbayar 2021). The herder population declined due to livestock loss, driving 
migration to urban areas, straining urban infrastructure and services, which remain vulnerable to severe 
flooding (MET 2018).

**The modeled urban flood scenario estimates a 1.8 percent loss of national GDP, primarily 
concentrated in Ulaanbaatar, which accounts for 63 percent of Mongolia’s GDP.** Municipal authorities 
suggest that a repeat of the 1966 flood would cost the economy US$ 80 million. This value seems too 
small. A flood disrupting the city for a week, followed by a 35-day recovery period (Tanoue and others, 
2020), would not only halt economic activities but also incur substantial recovery costs. With 25 percent 
of capital impacted (World Bank 2022), urban flooding would disrupt businesses, damage infrastructure, 
and displace residents, leading to huge economic losses. The concentration of economic activities in 
Ulaanbaatar means that urban flooding would have outsized impacts on national economic 
performance—the immediate impact on Ulaanbaatar’s GDP could reach 2.88 percent. Should a flood of 
a similar magnitude to that of 1966 occur, the direct GDP impacts would likely come closer to $309 
million—almost four times the figure put forward by the municipal authorities.

**The loss of coal exports to China, Mongolia’s largest trading partner, would severely impact the 
mining sector, resulting in a GDP loss of up to 30 percent.** Without adequate labor market adjustments, such as retraining displaced workers, total employment might decline by 2.3 percent. A rapid decrease 
in Chinese demand for coal would likely trigger a severe recession.

**The simultaneous occurrence of these three shocks could severely undermine financial stability in** 
**Mongolia.** Although some of the effects of the shocks would be temporary, the loss of export earnings 
would eradicate a major source of hard currency, exacerbating balance of payments (BOP) issues and 
putting pressure on the Mongolian *tugrug* at a time when food imports would become necessary. 
Similarly, revenue losses from mining activities would put considerable pressure on public balances, as 
would public support for herders. While post-flood reconstruction activities could provide a temporary 
boost to GDP by increasing government spending and investment in infrastructure, this would not 
translate into increased consumption (Hallegatte 2014). The recovery from an urban flood would strain 
public finances further, diverting funds from other critical areas and limiting the government’s ability to 
stimulate consumer spending or address other economic challenges. The decline in employment could 
translate to higher social security burdens and dampen consumer spending further, weakening domestic 
demand. These factors together could lead to a devaluation of the local currency, increased inflation, 
and a potential liquidity crisis as the government struggles to meet its financial obligations. It could undo 
years of Mongolia’s economic transformation (See Box 2).

###### Box 2. A triple shock hits Mongolia: breaking down the potential impacts

**_The mining sector, crucial to Mongolia’s economy, accounts for most of the output, GDP, and 
employment losses._** The mining sector accounts for nearly 30 percent of Mongolia’s GDP, reflecting 
recent mining-driven economic growth (World Bank 2020a). However, as a capital-intensive industry, 
mining accounts for only 12 percent of all employment. Simulation results for the plausible worst-case 
scenario, with all expected shocks combined, indicate that the mining sector bears a disproportionate 
burden of the losses. Specifically, it accounts for 67 percent of all output losses, 52 percent of the GDP 
loss, and 45 percent of employment losses. The disaggregation of the impacts reveals that trade shocks 
are the primary cause, contributing to 76 percent of employment losses and 82 percent of GDP loss.

**_Large losses in the services sector underscore the vulnerability of both high and low-income workers 
to the shocks._** Services constitute almost half of GDP and employ over half of the workforce. The sector 
employs both wealthier workers (World Bank 2020b) and women who often work in low-paying subsectors (World Bank 2020a). A loss of service sector jobs thus adversely affects both more affluent 
workers and low-paid women.

**_These projected losses could undo years of economic development in Mongolia._** The services sector, 
already impacted by the pandemic, would face further setbacks, exacerbating existing output and 
employment losses. Mongolia already struggles with the benefits of structural transformation and 
poverty reduction being offset by labor movements into lower-productivity, non-tradable services 
(World Bank 2020a). Ideally, workers could instead shift to higher-productivity (and traded) sectors. 
However, with the shock impacts primarily transmitted through trade channels, these difficulties could 
worsen in the decades ahead.

###### 4. Preparation, adaptation and coping mechanisms

**To strengthen Mongolia’s resilience to natural disasters, it will be critical to adopt a comprehensive 
and proactive approach to disaster risk management, financing, and insurance, including a wider 
range of risk management instruments.** Given the increasing frequency and magnitude of natural 
disasters, there is a need to better integrate disaster risk reduction measures into development planning 
and investment, as well as the budget process (including fiscal planning, the public investment 
management framework, and fiscal risk analysis). Enhancing Mongolia’s resilience to more frequent and 
severe natural disasters requires a comprehensive approach to disaster risk reduction and management. 
Current laws and policies predominantly focus on disaster preparedness and emergency response but 
do not adequately cover disaster risk reduction, financing, or insurance, and where measures do exist, 
implementation is generally weak. Therefore, there is also a need to collect better data on vulnerability 
and to enhance preparedness by considering different risk retention instruments, based on a 
comprehensive assessment of the physical risks to the country’s public finances, private sector, and 
households.

###### 4.1 Early intervention and coordination to mitigate dzud impacts

**To help herders prepare for increasingly severe winters, facilitating access to adaptive livestock 
industry inputs through robust market channels will be key.** These inputs, particularly veterinary 
services, feed, and fodder are crucial for managing drought, livestock diseases, and mitigating rangeland 
degradation (See Box 3). Veterinary services protect livestock from pest and disease outbreaks, ensuring 
animals can survive harsh winters and meet export standards. A strong fodder market could help 
maintain livestock productivity in extreme weather while reducing the strain on natural resources. 
Supplementing forage with feed and fodder could provide flexibility in slaughter seasons and support 
continuous production of livestock products, contributing to consistent quality and improved market 
opportunities (Mercy Corps 2017).

**To achieve this, the government should create a favorable environment for small and medium 
businesses to invest in veterinary and fodder services, and educate entrepreneurs on these 
opportunities.** Facilitating market linkages may help businesses to understand herders’ needs and 
develop innovative models that serve mobile populations effectively. By enhancing production knowledge and promoting the use of higher-yield inputs, herders could increase and stabilize incomes, 
supporting future investments and protecting existing herds against risks (Mercy Corps 2017).

###### In addition, access to savings, loans, and insurance products could help to strengthen herders’

**resilience to natural disasters.** Favorable loan products may facilitate investments in adaptive 
livelihoods, while insurance could prove crucial for mitigating the risk of herd loss during harsh winters 
and droughts. However, ensuring diversified and accessible financial services requires a transformation 
in governance, social attitudes, and economic systems. New measures may include formal registration 
to qualify for loans, designing loan products that meet the specific needs of the livestock economy, and 
allowing livestock to serve as collateral. Additionally, the financial regulatory environment should 
balance risk aversion with support for Savings and Credit Unions to fill critical financing gaps. 
Understanding client risks and providing solutions like alternative savings products and business 
recovery loans is also vital.

###### Adaptive Social Protection (ASP) could play a key role in enhancing resilience and preventing or

**mitigating the adverse effects of climate-driven covariate shocks.** While Mongolia has a foundation for 
ASP in place, as revealed by its strong COVID-19 response, integration of ASP principles into existing laws 
(including the draft on disaster risk management strategy), and disaster risk financing could be 
improved. Data and systems essential for identifying potential ASP beneficiaries are generally well 
developed, but linkages between databases maintained by different agencies could be strengthened.

###### Box 3. Lessons in dzud resilience

**_During the dzud of 2016–2017, livestock mortality rates saw significant variation between two soums 
of Khuvsgul aimag, despite similar climates and geographical features._** Chadraabal and others (2020) 
conducted a field study to identify critical climatic and socioeconomic factors driving the different dzud 
impacts in the two soums. The authors attribute the higher mortality rate in Tse soum to inadequate 
local government preparations, such as failing to meet the necessary hay and fodder quotas and 
mismanaging dzud response actions, which exacerbated livestock vulnerability and mortality. 
Conversely, Tsagaan-Uul soum’s success in minimizing livestock losses is attributed to proactive 
measures by herders and supportive local government actions. Herders in Tsagaan-Uul soum prepared 
adequately for winter by storing hay and fodder, setting up winter camps, and practicing otor (seasonal 
movements).

**_These self-reliant strategies, combined with effective local government support, such as maintaining 
a well-managed fodder fund and ensuring timely resource distribution, were crucial in reducing the 
dzud’s impact._** Chadraabal and colleagues emphasize the need for coordinated preventive strategies 
involving herders, local governments, and aid providers. Improving hay and fodder storage, enhancing 
winter shelter preparations, and increasing the frequency and distance of seasonal movements could 
go a long way in reducing dzud-related mortality. Additionally, developing a robust social support 
network and a nationwide integrated system for humanitarian assistance could ensure timely support 
for vulnerable herder households, reducing the overall impact of dzuds.

###### 4.2 Preparation for and prevention of urban floods in Ulaanbaatar

**With over half of the recorded 34 floods in Ulaanbaatar between 1915 and 2013 occurring from 2000 
to 2009, the urgency to address flood risks has never been greater.** The expansion of residential areas, 
particularly the ger districts, into flood-prone zones highlights the need for better land-use policies. 
Current laws allowing citizens to claim land for residential use have led to settlements being constructed 
on hazardous mountain slopes and flood plains. These areas, often occupied by lower-income migrants, 
lack basic services and infrastructure, increasing their susceptibility to flooding. Furthermore, poor solid 
waste management has clogged the city’s natural and artificial drainage systems, compounding the 
flood risk. Many levees and drainage structures have been demolished or are inadequately maintained, 
reducing the city’s capacity to manage floodwater effectively.

**Given the complexity of flood risks, developing a comprehensive flood risk management strategy is 
key.** The municipal authorities have developed a strategy based on extensive consultations with 
stakeholders, which outlines five key directions for mitigating flood risks. Firstly, protecting river basins 
through rehabilitation and conservation efforts is essential. This includes forest protection to reduce soil 
erosion and increase water retention capacity, ultimately reducing runoff. Secondly, resilient urban
development aims to reduce flood exposure by ensuring that land use, sediment, and solid waste 
management are informed by risk assessments, and by upgrading urban infrastructure. Enhancing flood 
infrastructure involves improving flood protection measures and drainage networks, especially in the 
ger areas. Additionally, fostering safe and resilient communities through improved social and emergency 
services, early warning systems, and community-based disaster risk management is crucial. Finally, good 
governance and effective flood risk management require institutional strengthening and clear 
delineation of roles and responsibilities among agencies involved in flood management.

**To further strengthen flood resilience, authorities should leverage existing flood hazard information 
to understand risks in project areas.** This involves verifying project locations against detailed flood 
hazard maps for accurate assessment and appropriate mitigation planning. Engaging technical expertise 
is also vital; consulting professionals with specialized knowledge in flood risk assessment ensures 
effective mitigation strategies tailored to the project’s hazard level and vulnerability. Considering project 
relocation is another critical step for high-risk zones, reducing the vulnerability of infrastructure and 
communities to flooding. Implementing early warning systems provides advanced alerts of impending 
flood events, enabling timely deployment of defenses or evacuation protocols. Conducting thorough 
location assessments before resource commitment ensures informed decisions regarding urban flood 
hazards and necessary interventions. Adopting comprehensive flood hazard management measures, 
such as catchment management and flood defenses, protects infrastructure and communities from 
potential damage. Finally, ensuring that new infrastructure does not exacerbate flood risks requires 
careful planning and implementation to avoid altering local hydrology negatively.

###### 4.3 Diversification from coal

**The potential loss of coal exports presents an important challenge to Mongolia’s trade balance and 
economic stability.** Mongolia heavily relies on coal exports as a major source of revenue, with most of 
these exports going to China. In 2022, coal accounted for almost half of Mongolia’s total export revenue. 
If coal exports were to decline, either due to decreased global demand or a shift towards greener energy 
sources, Mongolia’s trade balance would be severely affected.

**Investing in human and institutional capital is a crucial step for Mongolia to diversify its economy.** 
Despite large investments in education, there is a mismatch between the skills of the workforce and 
available job opportunities, leading to high youth unemployment and underemployment of skilled 
workers. By designing a comprehensive human resources development plan focused on improving 
education quality, particularly in STEM areas, and establishing strict quality control systems for 
education and training programs, Mongolia can better utilize its human capital. This alignment of 
educational programs with market demands will attract and retain talent, essential for economic 
diversification.

**Improving public investment efficiency should be a priority.** The government must enhance the 
management of public investment projects to ensure high returns and effective resource utilization. 
Prioritizing projects with clear economic benefits and ensuring their timely completion will support the 
development of diverse economic sectors. This, in turn, could reduce Mongolia’s dependency on coal by 
fostering growth in other industries.

###### 5. Conclusion

**This background note shifts the focus of the analysis in the CCDR from prediction to risk management.** 
It changes the question from ‘what do we think will happen?’ to ‘how bad could it get?’. In doing so, the 
note develops a plausible worst-case scenario of three disaster events occurring in quick succession: a 
series of consecutive dzuds kills millions of cattle; a severe flood, akin to that of 1966, sweeps 
Ulaanbaatar; and coal exports to China suddenly drop. Each of these individual events has happened in 
Mongolia before, and an unstable climate is making them more severe and more likely in the future. 
Hence the potential overlap of these three crises in the next 20 years is a possibility.

###### Mean estimates of climate damages overlook Mongolia’s exposure to large, and potentially

**compounding, economic shocks that could severely impact the country’s development trajectory.** In 
contrast, this note acknowledges the rising likelihood of more frequent and severe climate-related 
shocks, to the extent that their convergence becomes a foreseeable threat. The analysis in this report 
has found that a convergence of the three low-probability but high-impact events could result in a 20 
percent negative shock to Mongolia’s annual GDP over a three-year period, severely undermining 
output and employment and hitting vulnerable groups the hardest. The loss of coal exports alone would 
have substantial economic impact but, if it coincides with a major natural disaster, it would strain 
Mongolia’s coping capabilities.

---

**The message of this exercise is therefore clear: it is crucial to formulate policy based on the best 
possible estimate of _worst_**-**case scenarios.** The triple-shock scenario is not intended as a forecast but 
rather to illustrate what could happen without adequate preparation to mitigate future losses. This note 
has therefore sought to identify critical vulnerabilities within the country that could be exacerbated by 
climate change in the coming decades. Effective climate policy should focus on the ‘tail’ of the 
probability distribution to limit the likelihood of severe outcomes. By pinpointing these vulnerabilities, 
we can assess proactive measures to mitigate and prepare for future risks.

**It is therefore necessary to consider adaptation measures that would reduce the potential impacts of 
severe shocks.** These measures are briefly considered in Section 4 and explored further in the CCDR’s 
background notes. Transitioning from a reactive crisis mentality to one that prioritizes preparedness, 
adaptation, and multi-sectoral collaboration from a systems perspective will be critical for Mongolia’s 
long-term resilience.

# BACKGROUND NOTE 3: CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES IN THE MINING SECTOR

*Andrew Womer, Hector Pollitt*

###### 1. Introduction

In the coming decades, global decarbonization will dramatically shift how energy is produced and 
stored. Energy production in the modern era has primarily come from the combustion of fossil fuels—
namely oil, coal, and natural gas. Moving to low carbon energy production will weaken demand for fossil 
fuels and increase the demand for a new suite of minerals that are required for green energy production 
and electricity storage. The new technologies required for the low-carbon future will be much more 
minerally intensive and diverse than those of the fossil fuel era.

This changing energy landscape presents a mixed picture for the Mongolian economy. Its future growth 
is tied to its export-driven mining industry, which is currently dominated by coal and copper shipped to 
China. Chinese decarbonization plans will reduce the demand for Mongolian coal. To adapt, and sustain 
the sectoral performance, Mongolia’s mining sector will need to increase copper resource inventory,
extraction and explore options for extracting other minerals critical to the energy transition. This will 
require an improved investment climate with responsible mining companies, foreign direct investment,
and new and upgraded infrastructure to support the growth and functioning of the existing and new 
mineral industries.

###### 2. Mining and the Economy

Mining is a critical part of the Mongolian economy and has been central to its growth over the past two 
decades. Following the discovery of major coal and gold-copper ore deposits in the early 2000s, largescale extraction took off in 2004 in partnership with foreign and local mining companies, state owned 
mining entities. Although mining currently employs only 3.6 percent of Mongolia’s workforce, it makes 
up 26 percent of fiscal revenue, 94 percent of exports, and 25 percent of GDP (up from just 10 percent
in 2000). Production in other sectors, including construction and transport, and small-medium sized 
enterprises are also dependent on the mining sector.

The two main commodities that Mongolia produces are coal and copper. They have driven mining 
expansion since 2004, being responsible for 82 percent of the growth in mining exports. As shown in 
Figure 1A, coal and copper have traded places as Mongolia’s leading export. However, since 2021 coal 
exports have far surpassed copper exports because of Chinese steel production increases and demand 
for metallurgical coal. New infrastructure projects connecting critical coal deposits to Chinese land ports
have also allowed increases in production.

The mining sector overall is highly dependent on China. More than 90 percent of Mongolia’s thermal 
and metallurgical coal and copper exports are purchased by China (Figure 1b). A combination of 
geographical proximity and the land-locked geography of the country, plus the high cost of transporting 
bulky goods through land, has meant that China has historically been the main destination for 
Mongolia’s mining exports. Rapidly growing demand from China in concert with growing domestic 
capacity has therefore driven growth in Mongolian extraction. Cooperation since 2014 under the Belt 
and Road initiative has further promoted trade with China. An increasingly large share of Mongolia’s 
GDP is now dependent on sustained mineral export demand from China. Russia, for its part has 
historically bought very little extraction from Mongolia despite sharing a border. In 2021, Russia 
accounted for just 0.7 percent of Mongolian exports, although over half of these exports were in 
precious metal ores, which could be a growing industry in Mongolia.

Although high transportation costs make China the only realistic destination for Mongolian coal,
Mongolian copper may be competitive in the global market if a viable route to international ports was 
established. However, China’s copper demand is by far the largest in the world (over double the 
combined demand of the European Union, UK, North America and India) and China is investing heavily in 
the adoption of green energy technology, for which copper is essential.

Beyond coal and copper, Mongolia has mining operations in gold, iron, zinc, crude petroleum, 
molybdenum and fluorspar. Sales of gold to the Mongol Bank were valued at USD 1.1bn in 2022 and are 
expected to increase in the next decade (Figure 2).

However, the minerals that offer the greatest opportunity for Mongolia may be the ones for which
mining operations do not yet exist: uranium, lithium and rare earth elements (REEs). Mongolia has rich 
reserves of uranium, and historical mining sites of Soviet times are present. After several decades of 
development and social licensing challenges, uranium production at the Zuuvch-Ovoo deposit is set to 
begin through a cooperation agreement between state-backed mining companies in Mongolia and 
France. Lithium and REEs are both in the exploration stage of development and their potential will be 
understood in the coming years as assessments take place.

Continuing to improve border capacity and customs procedures is also a crucial element to bringing

costs weigh most heavily on exports of coal, which has the highest transport costs per unit of any of 
Mongolia’s major minerals. In the past five years, the Mongolian government has been proactive in 
bringing down these transport costs and customs procedures. It has increased capacity at existing 
border crossings and has added additional border crossings to China to share the load. The Mongolian 
government has worked with the Chinese government to simplify the process for exporting goods across 
the border into China and built railroads connecting coal mines to the Chinese border. The goal is to 
expand this network further to coal and copper production facilities.

The large role of mining in Mongolia has meant that the economy tends to move with commodity prices. 
This has led to economic instability, for example with large variation in growth of real wages and 
consumption. The poverty headcount has increased in recessions and declined in boom periods that 
have been defined by movements in commodity markets. Financial instability resulting from swings in 
commodity prices is also a deterrent to investment in all sectors.

###### 3. Metallurgical Coal

Mongolia has mining operations in both thermal and metallurgical coal, primarily used in energy 
production and as an input in steel production, respectively. Also, thermal coal remains the primary source of domestic energy consumption, accounting for over 70 percent of the total energy supply. 
Metallurgical, also known as *coking* coal is entirely exported as an input for Chinese steel production.

Decarbonization will likely mean a shorter lifespan for thermal coal as an energy source in Mongolia, 
dependent on domestic policy choices. This is discussed in more detail in the background note to the 
CCDR on energy.

The future for coking coal is uncertain. Production of coking coal in Mongolia is currently growing 
because of investments in transport infrastructure and sustained Chinese demand. The key asset for 
coking coal is the Tavan Tolgoi mining complex, home to one of the world’s largest coal deposits in the 
South Gobi, about 250 km from the Chinese border. Since 2020, the large transport investments made 
by the Mongolian government have reduced costs and increased volumes of coal exports to a record
high of 8.3 billion tons. In the next five years, coking coal exports are expected to keep growing, albeit at 
a slower pace. A study by the CRU conducted in 2021 predicted that coking coal production will continue 
to grow until at least 2030.

However, three factors could reduce Chinese (and hence total) demand for Mongolian coking coal. The 
first factor is the slowing Chinese construction sector following from the property crisis, which is 
dampening steel demand. The second factor is China’s efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, 
including from the steel sector. Finally, technological advances may eventually reduce the use of coal in 
steelmaking.

The long-term outlook for coking coal depends on a combination of technological readiness and the 
level of ambition in China to decarbonize production. Coal is used in current blast furnace-basic oxygen 
furnaces (BF–BOFs), with the current electric arc alternatives requiring enough scrap steel for 
production. Eventually, hydrogen could be used to produce zero-carbon steel that does not require 
recycled inputs. Converting China’s entire steel production will be an enormous task<sup>9</sup>. However, China 
has communicated its goal of carbon neutrality, and many of its largest steel producers have now made 
explicit pledges to reduce and eventually eliminate CO2 emissions from steel production.

Nevertheless, even if the climate targets set in the Paris Agreement are missed and a path consistent 
with 2.4 degrees temperature change is followed, it is possible that many of the minerals and metals 
necessary to produce low-carbon technologies will face shortages by 2030. Supply shortages and the 
resulting higher prices could further slow the low-carbon transition.

The extraction, and often processing, of critical minerals is also highly concentrated in certain countries
(much more so than for fossil fuels). For lithium, cobalt and rare earths, the world’s top three producers 
control well over three quarters of global output. China dominates both extraction (60 percent of global

Resource security is increasingly becoming a concern for major trading nations and is featuring in 
current industrial strategies.

There is therefore a substantial opportunity for Mongolia to fill the gap in global demand by being a 
reliable supplier of critical minerals, because Mongolia is endowed with copper and other energy 
transition minerals. As with any transition there are also risks, notably in predicting the technologies 
that will drive the low-carbon transition, how dependent they will remain on specific minerals, and how 
Mongolia can compete against other countries that will similarly be looking to increase supply.

###### 5. Could Mongolia Increase Mineral Production?

This section considers the minerals where there may be potential for Mongolia to increase production. 
Mongolia already extracts some of these minerals, so would need to scale up production. For other 
minerals, resource inventories need to be increased and new capacity would need to be developed so 
that the mining sector could seize the opportunity.

###### 5.1 Copper

The economic case for a large expansion of production is the most compelling for copper. Copper is the 
most prevalent mineral in a broad range of different low-carbon energy technologies and there are no 
indications of likely substitutes arising. Copper’s unparalleled thermal and electrical conductivity makes 
it a necessary component for batteries, electricity transmission, EVs, solar photovoltaics, and wind 
turbines. As a result, the global market for copper is secure in the next several decades. According to the 
International Energy Agency, under a scenario with net-zero emissions by 2050, copper demand will 
triple in the next decade.

Copper is already Mongolia’s second biggest mineral industry. The underground mining development of 
the Oyu Tolgoi mining complex in the South Gobi aimag is underway, which is projected to begin 
production in 2025. The Oyu Tolgoi mine is one of the largest copper-gold mines in the world. After a 
decade of open pit mining, plans were agreed to develop underground mining operations to untap the 
high-grade copper at 2 km depth at the site, which began in March 2023. When completed, this 
expansion will greatly increase production at the mine, bringing full capacity production to 500,000 tons per year for at least 2028-2036<sup>11</sup> (an increase by a factor of 3). Annual production value could increase 
by $2.6 billion at forecasted real price levels, equivalent to 40 percent of the total value of coal 
production in 2023 (Mongolian coal’s best year on record). The current mining operation at Oyu Tolgoi is 
planned until 2055 and will likely extend further<sup>12</sup>.

In the near term, the destination of Mongolia’s copper exports will remain almost entirely China. Copper 
has a higher value-to-weight ratio than coal and so there may be some flexibility in trade destinations, 
both within China and to other countries. However, any exports of copper would need to pass through 
either China or Russia, so additional leverage in trade partners would be limited.

The central risk to the copper industry in Mongolia is water scarcity. Mineral processing for copper uses 
much more water than any other of Mongolia’s existing mining operations—in 2020 water used for coal 
extraction and washing for thermal and coking coal was under 10 percent of that used by copper. The 
availability of freshwater supplies varies across Mongolia, but the country has high water-stress levels in 
several regions, including those with mining operations. Mining already accounts for 22 percent of total 
water consumption nationally, and copper is by far the largest consumer; it also is a much larger 
problem when considered regionally (Figure 7).

###### 5.2 Uranium

Mongolia is rich in uranium resources. The Nuclear Energy Agency and IAEA estimated in 2020 that 
Mongolia has around 60,500 tU in reasonably assured resources priced at $130/kgU—a value of over 
$7.8 trillion. The Mongolian government has put the resource figure even higher, at 166,657 tU and 
geological surveys suggest that total resources could be up to 1.32 million tU. Mongolia’s uranium is 
highly concentrated, with over 99 percent of the reasonably assured resources located in the Zoovch-
Ovoo deposit that is invested by French state mining company, Orano.

The uranium industry in Mongolia began in the 1970s following the discovery of large deposits in the 
Dornod aimag. Uranium production first took place in 1988-95 as a joint venture between Mongolia’s 
SOE MonAtom and the Russian mining company Priargunsky, with only modest production levels. Post 
Soviet complexities resulted in sudden closure of the mining operation in 1995; since then there has 
been no uranium mining activity.

However, as pressure increases globally to reduce emissions, the ability of nuclear power to provide a 
reliable source of low-carbon electricity has become more prominent. The outlook for uranium prices 
and demand (especially from China) has improved considerably. Water scarcity means that Mongolia is 
unlikely to be able to produce its own nuclear power, but the opportunity to extract and export uranium 
to other countries is now larger than it has been previously. The World Nuclear Association (WNA) has 
estimated that uranium demand will increase by 28 percent by 2030 and double by 2040 (2023 Nuclear 
Fuel Report).

Current efforts to develop uranium extraction must learn from the shortcomings of previous efforts. In 
the 1990s, the Mongolian government worked with private companies to assess the commercial viability 
of deposits in Dornod aimag and awarded licenses to undertake exploration and feasibility studies. 
However, the passing of the Nuclear Energy Law (NEL) in 2009, which increased the state participation 
and equity share in radioactive materials mining projects, ended prospects for development and led to a 
USD 70m legal settlement fee for the Mongolian government. Reputational damage has been a factor in 
the failure of more recent efforts to develop the sector.

Geopolitical uncertainty has driven major economies to diversify the composition of their energy 
suppliers, particularly for sensitive products like uranium. For example, France, which has a heavy 
reliance on nuclear power, has previously imported uranium from Niger. Political instability in the Sahel 
region has called into question the reliability of Niger as a supplier of uranium, prompting the French 
government to seek alternative sources.

The Zuuvch Ovoo deposit is currently under development by the French state-backed Orano group 
through its Mongolian subsidiary. The initial discovery of the deposit was made in 2010 and resources 
and reserves were estimated in preparation for production. In 2023, a Memorandum of Understanding 
(MOU) was signed by the Mongolian and French governments on production at Zuuvch Ovoo. The 
detailed investment agreement is underway between the working groups of the two governments. The 
project is expected to bring in $1.6 billion in investment and $1 billion in tax revenue.

###### 5.4 Rare Earth Elements (REEs)

REE deposits contain any combination of the total 17 separate metallic elements that share similar 
chemical properties. They have long been an important component in various high-end technologies 
such as smartphones, lasers, light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and batteries for EVs because of their 
capabilities as heat-resilient, permanent magnets. After stable demand throughout the 2010s, REE 
production grew by over 160 percent in between 2017 and 2023, largely because of their use in solar 
cells, EVs, wind turbines and other low-carbon technologies. Although efficiency improvements will 
reduce REE inputs per unit of production, there are limited alternative technology options to at least 
some REEs. Demand is expected to increase until at least the mid-2030s (Figure 6). It will be difficult for 
supply to expand at the same pace, so higher prices are also predicted. There is therefore an 
opportunity for new players such as Mongolia to enter the global market.

REE mining, processing, and elements extraction is challenging because of environmental concerns and 
high processing costs. Currently, REE mining and element extraction is concentrated in China, although 
the US and Russia also have large reserves<sup>19</sup>. There is currently a shortage of REEs in the US, Japan, 
Korea and Europe because of know-how of REE processing and extraction and trade restrictions set by 
China. A search for alternative production sources is thus ongoing. Mining of REE in Mongolia has 
garnered interest from foreign governments. The development of high technology minerals, including 
REEs are part of the government’s “Long Vision 2050” strategic plan, and continues to be ranked as a 
high priority in the government’s implementation plan. In 2023, the US Department of State signed a 
Memorandum of Understanding on cooperation with Mongolia on REE commercialization, development 
of the mineral sector and governance. Mongolia has also become a member of the Minerals Security 
Partnership. The regional development plan of Western Mongolia listed the development and value 
addition of the Khalzan Buregtei REE deposit as a development anchor for the extremely rural region.

The commercial viability of the Mongolia’s REE is being estimated. There are seven deposits with 
resources approved by the Minerals Authority of Mongolia. Two of these seven deposits host almost 
90% of the mineral resource, one located in Southern Mongolia, a Khotgor deposit, and the other in 
Western Mongolia, a Khalzan Buregtei deposit. Both of these deposits are currently upgrading the 
mineable reserve following the international resource statements and pre-feasibility studies that are 
underway. Based on the survey work that has already been done, the quality of the Khotdor deposit is 
comparable to the global average, but the Khalzan Buregtei deposit is less than the global average.

<u><sup>18</sup>https://www.ionenergy.ca/urgakh-naran2/</u>

<u><sup>19</sup>https://go.nature.com/3h1aeji</u>

---

However, the content of the highly sought out Heavy Rare Earth elements in Khalzan Buregtei is high, 
which would help with the bankability of the project.

Due to the sizeable nature of the end product from REE deposits, transportation is commonly not a 
constraint, and even air-freight option may be viable. The main challenge of the Khalzan Buregtei 
deposit is its location, far from current infrastructure and a high-capacity electricity grid. Nevertheless, 
with the government’s plan of expanding energy capacity in the Western Regions, establishing a 
competitive advantage in REE extraction could become viable.

There are also environmental and social risks to mining REEs in Mongolia. Mongolia has little experience 
in mining and processing commodities with radioactive elements. The waste management, 
environmental social impact, and mine closure policies need to be strengthened in preparation for REE 
mining. Managing the radioactive waste from REE mining can be costly. China’s dominance in REE 
extraction reflects in part its investment in safety measures and crackdown on illegal mining, as well as 
other countries’ reservations about allowing operations that create radioactive waste. For example, 
even the highly regulated Mountain Pass mining operation in the US had to close temporarily in 1998 
because of leaking radioactive wastewater<sup>20</sup>. Therefore, the environmental and radioactive 
management laws and regulations need to be revised in preparation for REE mining in Mongolia.

Several hurdles would therefore need to be overcome to establish large-scale REE extraction in 
Mongolia. First, further exploration is required to upbring the existing deposits to bankable level. 
Second, substantial investment would be required to develop mining operations and other 
infrastructure (e.g. energy) necessary to allow operation. Increased international collaboration would be 
required, alongside an investment-friendly administrative and legal framework.

###### 5.5 Other minerals

Mongolia also has mining operations in zinc and molybdenum, although they make up less than 2 
percent of total mineral exports. Demand for both products is likely to increase in a low-carbon future. 
However, in both cases the opportunity to expand production looks limited. Zinc deposits have been 
thoroughly surveyed and the surveys suggest that a large-scale expansion of extraction is impossible 
(mining of polymetallic deposits are limited to around a decade). Molybdenum is a by-product of copper

###### 5.7 Governance Issues

Expanding existing mineral extraction and starting new operations will require major investment 
cooperation with foreign companies, both on making initial investments and on running ongoing 
operations. Consistency and strong long-term relationships with foreign companies will be important 
because long project development lead times require a degree of trust between project partners. It
takes on average 16 years to advance mining projects from discovery to first production, with more time 
needed to generate profits (IEA, 2021). Mongolia’s government is a major commercial participant in the 
mining sector and needs to be more predictable, more transparent and increase its management 
capacity within mining SOEs over the entire project lifetime. In dealings with foreign companies, 
Mongolia needs to guarantee fair and consistent licensing, stability, ownership, and investment rules. 
Many mining projects in the past have failed because of legislative or administrative changes that 
undermined the participating foreign company’s stake in the project.

The government must also ensure that these projects benefit the public through the collection of taxes, 
royalties, and fees, as well as community engagement. Domestically, mining projects have often faced 
opposition because of benefit sharing and environmental costs.

The mining industry in Mongolia is governed by the Minerals Law of 2006 and its subsequent 
amendments, which set out the licensing, environmental protection, and government ownership 
regulations. The state’s participation has changed through various amendments that make it stronger, 
and the Mongolian government has typically played a large role in the mining industry—it owns and 
operates five mines, including the Tavan Tolgoi coking coal deposit. It also holds significant minority 
ownership in many other mines, such as Oyu Tolgoi, where it holds 34 percent of equity shares in the 
joint company licensed to mine its deposits. Initially, there were no requirements for government 
ownership in the Minerals Law, but an amendment in 2015 dictated that the government would own 
between 34 and 100 percent of the “strategic” deposits. In April 2024 the parliament passed an 
additional amendment that limited the share that foreign companies without an Investment Agreement 
with the Mongolian government could hold in these strategic deposit licensed companies to 34 percent.

For radioactive minerals, the Nuclear Energy Law (NEL) has separate rules. The NEL mandates a 51 
percent government stake in radioactive minerals. This share requires a higher level of government 
involvement than in many other countries but is not unique. Notably, Kazakhstan, the world’s largest 
producer of uranium is dominated by the SOE Kazatomprom and, in joint ventures, foreign companies 
are limited to minority stakes in uranium projects.

However, many other producers call for far less or even no government ownership. For example, in 
Namibia, the second largest producer of uranium, and Niger, foreign companies can hold a stake of up 
to 90 percent in a mining project. In Niger, the government is also required to make direct financial 
contributions to capital and operating costs<sup>22</sup>. Further afield, Canada and Australia, the third and fourth 
largest producers respectively, do not have government ownership requirements, but have often taken 
stakes in projects. Notably, Canada limits ownership of non-Canadian companies to 49 percent, although 
there is not a limit on private Canadian companies.

State owned mining can be an effective way to correct for market failures and provide investment in 
cases where private investors would perceive the investment as being too risky. However, state owned 
mining companies can also crowd out private investment if it is perceived that state owned companies 
will receive preferential treatment over private enterprises.

Transparency in these companies is critical. In Mongolia there have been issues with governance in 
state-backed participants in mining operations, for example in procurement. The result is lower 
institutional capacity and ability to deal consistently with foreign companies.

In a report on a joint institutional development program at Erdenes Mongol (a large SOE involved in 
mining in Mongolia), the Asia Development Bank highlighted the lack of in-house financial and project 
management capacity as being a major impediment to the implementation of long-term projects for 
Mongolia. Erdenes Mongol’s legal status was also in question because of changing legislation and 
political uncertainty which makes international cooperation challenging. There was ambiguity as to 
whether the Ministry of Finance of Mongolia would guarantee contingent liabilities for Erdenes Mongol, 
which led to uncertainty around cooperating with the SOE. The ADB suggested that there continued to 
be lack of mechanisms to channel funds raised from natural resources to strategic investments in 
infrastructure, something that the Mongolian government has also recognized in its National 
Development Strategy in the past.

The lack of transparency has also been a key issue in mining that has held back cooperation with foreign 
companies and allowed corruption to take place. The Mongolian government has been making strides in 
addressing the problem—it is a signatory of the Extractive Industry Transparency Initiative (EITI) as of 
2007 and has launched several e-governance initiatives including the passing of the Glass Accounts Law 
in 2015, which requires financial transactions above 5 million tugrugs (approximately USD 1,750) for 
SOEs to be made publicly available online<sup>23</sup>. Compliance with the Glass Accounts Law has been uneven, 
with many major SOEs taking several years to report their data. For example, Erdenet Oyu Tolgoi, the 
minority shareholder in the Oyu Tolgoi copper mine, still had not reported any data publicly by May 
2022.

Reforming the state-owned mining companies in Mongolia could include requiring professional external 
cost auditing of all mining projects, implementing international accounting standards, and setting 
objective criteria for hiring and firing corporate managers and independent assessments of manager 
performance (World Bank, 2021).

**Mongolia has experienced a robust but uneven economic growth.** Mongolia is the 18th largest country

energy-related CO2 emissions were estimated at 23 mt CO2, accounting for about 0.07 percent of global

**The electricity sector of Mongolia relies on coal-powered CHP plants for domestic power generation 
and imports from Russia and China to meet its electricity needs.** Domestic power generation accounts 
for 80 percent of total electricity supply, while imports supply the remaining 20 percent. The large Oyu 
Tolgoi (OT) copper and gold mines as well as the Nariin Sukhait coal mine in the South Gobi region are 
not connected to the Mongolia power grid but rely on power imports from China. New domestic 
generation capacity is urgently needed to meet growing demand and improve the quality and resilience 
of the electricity system.

**Although Mongolia has abundant solar and wind resource potential, an insubstantial proportion of 
these resources has been developed due to a weak enabling environment and inadequate grid 
capacity.** A previous World Bank study estimated the exploitable renewable energy potential in 
Mongolia to be at least 400 GW for wind and 700 GW for solar. Most of the solar and wind energy 
resources are in the southern part of Mongolia, far south from UB where demand is concentrated. 
However, there is a lack of high-voltage (HV) transmission lines to evacuate power from the south to UB, 
preventing the development of renewable resources in the area. Transmission line infrastructures from 
UB to the South will be critical for the development of renewable energy resources necessary for closing 
the supply-demand gap while enhancing energy security in Mongolia.

**The Mongolian power sector has been undergoing market-oriented reforms over the last few 
decades.** In 2001, the power sector was unbundled, and the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC) was 
established to regulate generation, transmission, distribution, dispatch, and supply of energy. ERC 
oversees a single-buyer model in which the National Dispatching Center, which is also the System 
Operator, buys electricity at a regulated price from generators and sells to distribution companies. 
Commercialized state-owned enterprises (SOEs) owns sector utilities but Independent Power Producers 
(IPPs) exist in the sector. The National Power Transmission Grid (NPTG) is the single national 
transmission company operating the High Voltage (HV) transmission lines in the CES and SES. WES, AUES 
and EES own and operate their own transmission lines independent from the NPTG.

###### 2.2 Heating sector

**Space heating is a critical energy service in Mongolia, essential for livelihoods and economic activities.** 
Mongolia is one of the coldest countries in the world, with extreme winter conditions. It has an average 
ambient temperature that reaches minus 20-30<sup>o</sup>C during the year and a heating season that lasts 8 
months in most parts of the country. As a result, heating services is one of the biggest uses of energy in 
Mongolia, comprising about 35 percent of the final energy balance. Three main modes of heating exist 
in Mongolia. In large urban centers such as the capital – UB, large DH systems supplied by coal-fired CHP 
plants and HOBs serve thousands of buildings. In small aimag and soum centers, small central heating 
systems powered by HOBs are common, typically supplying one or several buildings. In the gers or 
detached houses, heating stoves fueled by coal or wood are used in individual houses. Thus, the heating 
sector is dependent on coal even in households without access to DH services. Only about 25 percent of 
the population have access to DH services, which is about a half of the urban population and about twofifths of the population in UB. Over 70 percent of the population use individual stoves for heating 
purposes, while the remaining 5 percent benefit from small central heating systems.

**Lack of investment has led to deteriorated heating networks.** Primary and secondary heating networks 
are in dire need of rehabilitation and upgrades, and this has led to high technical losses in the system. 
About 50 percent of transmission pipelines are in poor technical condition, urgently requiring 
replacement while distribution networks require major rehabilitation. Currently, the heat transmission 
network is reaching its capacity limit, constraining the ability to connect new customers.

**The heating sector is the source of major air pollution, especially in the capital UB.** Space heating is a 
major source of winter air pollution in urban areas, posing serious health risks for households relying on 
coal-fired or wood-fired stoves. In 2018, the Government banned the use of raw coal in UB and 
introduced periods of free night-time electricity to incentivize the use of electric heaters. In 2019, it 
further introduced a subsidy scheme for washed coal for heating purposes. These interventions led to a 
significant reduction in air pollution in UB during the winter months but by themselves, have not been 
able to reduce particle emissions enough to eliminate the health hazards in urban areas.

###### 3. Challenges of the Energy Transition (Electricity and Heating Sector)

**Major challenges in the electricity and heating sectors undermine the energy transition in Mongolia.**
These include geographic factors such as the remote location of renewable energy resources against
load centers, technical constraints in the generation portfolio and grid that constrains the integration 
of variable renewable energy (VRE), regulatory and policy arrangements that disincentivizes private 
sector investments especially in renewable energy, high subsidies, amongst others.

**The integration of VRE is hindered by the lack of a strong transmission backbone across the regions 
and a modern grid control system.** Most of the renewable energy resources in Mongolia are 
concentrated in remote locations in the southern part of the country, far from UB in the north where 
electricity demand is concentrated. However, capacity limitations of inter-regional transmission lines, 
the development of RE power plant has been hampered despite its enormous potential.

**The dominance of CHP in the Mongolian electricity sector and weaknesses in its grid network makes 
the electricity system inflexible to the integration of VRE.** CHP plants are particularly inflexible in its 
operations, but this is worse in the Mongolian case due to the old age of plants and the weakness of 
its grid network which makes substantial VRE integration even more challenging. Subsequently, the development of new VRE capacity has been placed on hold tentatively to maintain grid stability until 
all necessary upgrades and rehabilitation are complete. Furthermore, obsolete SCADA to control the 
grid has been another obstacle for system operators to handle the intermittency of the VREs properly.

**Investments in the energy sector are lagging demand growth.** Domestic electricity demand has 
doubled over the last decade but installed capacity has increased by less than 10 percent during the 
same period. This has led to a high dependance on expensive imported power from Russia and China 
to meet electricity needs. It is expected that demand growth will increase at the same pace over the 
next decade, and with import capacity reached, Mongolia will struggle to meet its electricity demand 
unless new generation capacity is added.

**Electricity and heating prices are below the cost of service, creating financial challenges for sector 
utilities.** Electricity and district heating tariffs in Mongolia, as per the current methodology, does not 
make a provision for profit allowance based on the asset base. Moreover, the existing asset base 
remains unevaluated, and the depreciation allowance does not accurately represent real depreciation 
cost. Tariffs only cover fuel costs and operation and maintenance costs, with no allowance for capital 
investments. As a result, sector companies and utilities are unable to undertake the necessary capital 
investments. Sector financial deficit is projected to reach 36 percent of costs by 2025, underscoring the 
urgent need for tariff reforms in both the district heating and electricity sectors.

**In addition to the underpricing of electricity and heating services, there are other explicit and implicit 
subsidies in the Mongolian energy sector that disincentivizes investments in renewable energy and 
clean heating technologies.** In 2023, electricity tariffs covered 77% of the total cost of electricity supply. 
By 2030, household tariffs will need to triple to achieve cost recovery, while non-household tariffs will 
need to increase by approximately 65%. In the district heating sector, except for UB District Heating 
Network, which achieves approximately 98% cost recovery based on 2022 data, all other companies fall 
within the range of 40-60% cost recovery. These subsidies exert significant pressure on the national 
budget, amounting to 29 billion Mongolian tugrik (MNT) in 2022 at the expense of critical social services 
such as health, education, and social protection. In addition, these subsidies incentivize inefficient 
consumption and depress private sector investments especially in renewable energy technologies.

**In UB the current DH networks are insufficient, unreliable, and deteriorating.** Only about 30 percent 
of the population have access to central heating modes, making the heating sector highly 
decentralized and challenging to manage. However, connecting new customers to the central heating 
networks is constrained by the low capacity of the heat transmission network which has reached its
capacity limit. It is estimated that 50 percent of the transmission pipelines are in poor technical 
condition, urgently requiring replacement while the secondary (distribution) network requires major 
rehabilitation and upgrades.

###### 4.2.2. Modeling Results

**The deployment of about 5.6 GW of advanced renewable and clean-heating technologies is required 
to fully decarbonize the heating sector of Mongolia.** These include electric HOBs (2.8 GW), Air-water 
Heat Pumps (1.8 GW), waste-to-energy HOB (0.6 GW), solar thermal (0.3 GW), and geothermal (0.05

from  1.3 GW and coal HOBs providing support during peak hours. With the complete decarbonization 
of the heating sector in this scenario, there is a steady reduction in emissions from the heating sector which is expected to reach zero by 2050. This compares to an increase in emissions  from 2.3 MT in 2023

###### 4.3. Implications of Energy Transition from the modelling results

**The successful decarbonization of Mongolia’s energy sector will be tied to the phasing out of CHP 
plants, and the deployment of RE and clean heating technologies in the electricity and heating sectors.**
In the modelled decarbonization scenario, CHP plants are phased out and no new coal plants are 
developed till 2050. This has major implications for the continuous supply of energy while achieving the 
pathway towards net zero in future. To deploy renewable energy and clean heating technologies at scale 
in place of coal-fired CHP, the electricity and heating sectors need a clear roadmap for developing new 
capacity.

**Mongolia can leverage its massive renewable energy resources to accelerate its energy transition.** For

the power sector, the NZ_80 scenario indicates up to 11.1 GW of Wind, 5.2 GW of Solar PV, and 3.2 GW 
of hydropower, backed by 2.7 GW of energy storage systems, will be required to support 
decarbonization efforts by 2050. For the heating sector, existing CHP and HOB plants are gradually 
decommissioned and replaced by a combination of advanced renewable and clean-heating technologies by 2050. To provide electricity for the electrified clean-heating technologies such as Electric HOB and 
Air-water Heat Pump, additional renewables including 980 MW of Solar PV and 1,760 MW of Wind 
energy plants will also be required by 2050.

**Renewable energy integration will be catalyzed through investments in grid capacity and flexibility.** 
Most of the renewable energy resources in Mongolia are concentrated in remote regions, especially in 
the southern part of the country, which is far from UB in the north where electricity demand is 
concentrated. The completion of new transmission backbones with sufficient capacity is a critical 
condition for large scale renewable energy integration. In addition, the installation of energy storage 
systems will help balance supply with demand while securing system flexibility given the intermittency 
of VREs. In addition, improvements in grid control measures by smart grid technologies including up-todate SCADA enables remote control of the grid facilities and automized operation of generation plants, 
which in turn maximizes the utilization of generation from VREs.

**Despite the increasing competitiveness of renewable energy technologies, the cost of renewable 
energy deployment remains significant.** These include the cost of system integration, backup systems, 
ancillary services, and system-balancing equipment. Decarbonizing the Mongolian energy sector will 
require an additional $11.4 billion cumulatively from 2023 to 2050 relative to the Reference Scenario, 
reflecting a 53% increase in cost. This corresponds to an additional $946 million in annual costs over
those in the Reference Scenario by 2050, translating into a 20% annual cost premium. These costs are 
largely due to the CAPEX-intensive cost structure of deploying the range of renewable power generation 
and heating technologies. This is expected to increase the levelized cost of electricity as well as end user 
tariffs, which need to be managed strategically and effectively.

###### Executive Summary

**Climate change could lead to a slight increase in grazing productivity up to 2030 under average future 
conditions but will likely lead to declines in grazing productivity by 2050.** Mongolia has a cold 
continental climate. Some modest warming would tend to boost productivity. However, warming also 
leads to greater evaporation and could exacerbate water shortages. Model results show slight increases 
in grassland productivity under climate projections to 2030 relative to current outcomes for a range of 
future climate scenarios. By 2050, however, model results show a decline in grassland productivity, with 
larger declines in climate scenarios associated with greater warming. Further warming beyond 2050 
would likely cause further declines in grassland productivity.

**Climate change may cause an increase in the probabilities of extreme events with negative impacts on 
grazing.** In addition to changing average conditions, climate change may increase the probability of 
extreme events including the occurrence and severity of dzud. Dzud increase livestock mortality and 
have a negative impact on net returns from grazing. Including estimates of the impact of climate change 
both on average conditions and the increased likelihood of dzud generates estimates of expected losses 
of livestock productivity between 2 - 11 percent by 2050 relative to no climate change.

**It is possible to increase both carbon storage and economic returns from grazing, forestry, and crop 
production through changes in land use.** Using optimization methods to derive efficiency frontiers 
showing the maximum weighted combinations of carbon storage and net economic returns for a range 
of weights, we find substantial potential increases in carbon storage and net economic returns relative 
to current outcomes. Shifts in land use among grazing land, forestry, cropland, and natural lands can 
generate increases in carbon storage on the landscape of approximately 6% without loss in net 
economic returns. Mongolia is a largely arid country, which limits the potential for increasing carbon 
storage in terrestrial ecosystems. On the other hand, net economic returns from land can be increased 
by 115% without loss of carbon storage under current climate conditions. Increases in forestry and, to a 
much lesser extent, increases in intensively managed cropland, make it possible to increase the net 
value of economic activity from land and to do so without loss of carbon storage. These changes occur 
on a relatively small portion of the land, with grazing lands continuing to dominate land use.

**Large potential gains in net economic returns with no loss in carbon storage are generated  by 
improved forestry management.** Expanding forestry on lands where forests can grow in north central 
Mongolia, allows for large increases in net economic returns. The share of net economic returns from 
forestry as a share of total crop, livestock, and forestry production, rises from less than 20% in the 
current land use, to around 50% in the case where the landscape is chosen to maximize net economic returns without loss of carbon storage. The expansion in net economic returns can be accomplished 
without loss of carbon storage by reforesting some land.

**Increases in carbon storage and net economic returns from land are a function of climate change but 
large potential gains are still possible with climate change.** As climate change reduces grassland 
productivity, the efficiency frontier contracts inward meaning that smaller potential efficiency gains 
relative to current outcome are possible. However, under all climate scenarios analyzed out to 2050, 
large potential gains remain with improved land use and land management.

###### 1. Introduction

Agriculture and forestry are important for the economy in Mongolia. Approximately one-third of 
Mongolia’s labor force is employed in agriculture (FAO 2024a) and the agriculture, forestry, and fishing 
sector makes up 13% of GDP (World Bank 2024). As fishing makes up a negligible share of economic 
activity in Mongolia, for the rest of this report we will focus on agriculture (crops and livestock) and 
forestry. Within agriculture, livestock makes up the largest share of economic activity, accounting for 
approximately 80% of gross agricultural output and one-quarter of all jobs in Mongolia (FAO 2024b). 
Crop production and livestock are also among the largest contributors to greenhouse gas emissions in 
Mongolia, accounting for ~40% of total greenhouse gas emissions in 2020 
(https://www.climatewatchdata.org).

The net economic value derived from agricultural crops, livestock, and forest products depends on landuse and land-management decisions, and on climate. In this report, we analyze the impact of alternative 
land use and land management as well as climate on net economic returns and land-based carbon 
storage in Mongolia. We use models that predict net economic returns to crops, livestock, and forest 
product production, and terrestrial carbon storage, as a function of land use and land management 
using methods developed by the Natural Capital Project and applied in the *Nature’s Frontiers* Report 
(Damania et al. 2023). We use optimization methods to find land-use and land-management choices 
that simultaneously increase the value of economic output from agricultural crops, livestock and forest 
products and the amount of carbon storage in terrestrial ecosystems, as compared to the current 
outcome. We trace out a landscape efficiency frontier that shows the maximum possible carbon storage 
value for each level of net economic returns (Polasky et al. 2008, 2023, Damania et al. 2023). We also 
show what land-use and land-management changes are undertaken in moving from the current 
situation to the efficiency frontier. This analysis is done initially under current climate conditions.

###### 2. Methods

We model the impacts of 13 different land-use and land-management options on land-based carbon 
storage and net value of production using the InVEST model from the Natural Capital Project (Natural 
Capital Project 2024) as described in Damania et al. (2023) and Polasky et al. (2023). The 13-alternative land-use and land-management options are natural habitat, forestry, grazing, and ten options for crop 
agriculture. The ten land-management options for crop agriculture are current management on existing 
land, current management with expanded area, intensified rainfed and irrigated agriculture with and 
without best management practices on existing land (4 options), and intensified rainfed and irrigated 
agriculture with and without best management practices on expanded area (4 options). Best 
management practices include buffers along streams and natural set asides. We assume that best 
management practices result in retention of 10% natural habitat of the area designated as cropland. 
Within the natural habitat option, pixels can be various types of coniferous and broad-leaved forests, 
grasslands, shrublands, wetlands, or barren areas. The classification into these habitat types is based on 
potential vegetation as defined by European Space Agency land cover data set (ESA 2019).

We assembled biophysical and economic data sets at 10 arc sec (~ 300m<sup>2</sup> at the equator). We use this 
data to model outcomes for land-based carbon storage and net monetary returns from agricultural crop 
production, livestock grazing, and timber production, under the 13-alternative land-use and landmanagement options. We also account for transition costs of switching from land use alternative to 
another (see Polasky et al. 2023 for details). We constrain land-use and land-management options to 
not allow agricultural crop production, forestry, and grazing in designated protected areas (IUCN 
Categories 1-IV; UNEP-WCMC and IUCN 2021). We also do not allow expansion of rainfed crop 
agriculture in arid regions without sufficient rainfall, expansion or intensification of crop agriculture on 
slopes greater than 10%, or irrigation in areas where irrigation relies on unsustainable mining of 
groundwater aquifers (Rosa 2019). For forestry we apply a sustainability constraint that limits 
production to a level that can be sustained: timber harvests cannot exceed growth of forests so that the 
standing biomass of timber remains undiminished. To make fair comparisons, we also evaluate the 
current landscape with the same sustainability constraint used to calculate the frontier.

The component models and land-use and land-management alternatives are incorporated into an 
optimization approach to find a set of Pareto efficient solutions (Polasky et al. 2008, 2023). In a Pareto 
efficient solution, it is not possible to change the land-use and land-management alternative on any set 
of pixels to increase the score for any one outcome without also reducing the score for some other 
outcome. We group pixels into hexagonal parcels (8000 hectares at the equator) for optimization. The 
optimization algorithm chooses one of the 13 land-use and land-management alternatives for each land 
parcel so that the resulting land-use and land-management pattern for the country is Pareto optimal.

###### 2.1 Carbon storage

We used the InVEST Carbon Storage and Sequestration model to evaluate each land-use and landmanagement alternative for the amount of carbon stored in different carbon pools (Natural Capital 
Project 2024). For this analysis, we include the cumulative amount of carbon stored in above-ground 
and below-ground biomass, but not in soil, leaf litter, or wood products. The amount of carbon stored in 
forest, herbaceous vegetation (grass and shrub), and agricultural land cover classes, are taken from 
Spawn et al. (2020). To represent changes in carbon storage for adoption of best management practices 
in agricultural crop production, a weighted average is taken for the value in that carbon zone for the 
dominant natural vegetation type (proportional to 10% of the area) and agricultural crop production 
(proportional to 90% of the area). Agricultural crop intensification is not assumed to change carbon 
storage. We assume that the amount of carbon stored on a forest pixel that is managed for timber 
production is 50% of the carbon stored on that pixel when it is natural forest. This assumption reflects 
the fact that standing carbon stock is carbon stock is low shortly after timber harvest and grows back 
trees mature. For grazing lands, we assign the same carbon storage value as for natural grasslands.

###### 2.2 Crop production

Agricultural crop production provides crucial food, feed, fiber, and fuels that support human well-being, 
provide employment, and generate income. We used information on ten major crops and biophysical 
and socio-economic factors affecting their value to generate crop yield maps that vary as a function of 
location (including soil and climate variables), fertilizer application, irrigation infrastructure and 
sustainability of water supply, and calculate gross production value and net production value for each 
crop at each location. In the optimization we keep the crop mix constant (i.e., we do not optimize over 
crop selection). Crop yield maps for the ten major global crops (barley, cassava, maize, oil palm, 
rapeseed, rice, sorghum, soybean, sugarcane, wheat) were based on yield and harvested area data for 
each crop across ~20,000 political units from 1974-2012 (Ray et al. 2019). These data were normalized 
so that yield and harvested area matched with national-scale FAOSTAT production data. An average data 
layer was constructed for 2010 based on an area-weighted average of yields over the period 2008-2012. 
Potential attainable yields under agricultural intensification were modeled using a yield ceiling as a 
function of biophysical data and whether crops were grown under rainfed or irrigated conditions. 
Attainable yield is based on a 95<sup>th</sup> percentile quantile regression model of reported regional yields World 
Bank (2021a). Adoption of best management practices reduce the area of cropland by 10% (10% natural 
habitat) leading to a proportional reduction in output. For current sustainable management, crop 
irrigation was not permitted in those parcels where irrigation is unsustainable based on surface and 
groundwater supply (Rosa et al. 2019). For details of the crop production model see Polasky et al. 
(2023).

Gross income from crop production was estimated for each crop and parcel as total production (tons) 
multiplied by producer price. Crop production for each of the 10 crops in each pixel was calculated by 
multiplying yield by harvested area. We used the average producer price for each crop and country 
based (2006-2015) as reported by FAO (2024c). To convert gross returns to net returns for agricultural 
crops, we used the Global Trade Analysis Program GTAP 10 Data Base, which provides information on 25 
factors of production, for 65 economic sectors, in 141 regions (Aguiar et al. 2019). We used the same 
procedure to convert gross returns to net returns for livestock grazing and forestry and so we report on 
this procedure for all three here. The factors of production in the GTAP 10 Data Base include 18 agroecological zones (AEZs), 5 types of labor (clerks, agricultural and unskilled labor, etc.) and two types of 
capital (produced capital and natural capital). Land in Mongolia falls almost exclusively into two AEZs. 
We multiply the factor share for land in each of the AEZs by the gross returns to generate an estimate of 
net returns attributable to nature (returns to land), which nets out costs for labor, produced capital and 
other human inputs. For Mongolia, the percentage of gross value attributable to nature was 6% for 
agricultural crops, 30% for grazing, and 27% for forestry.

We calculated transport costs of moving crop production from agricultural field to consumers based on 
travel time to the nearest city (Weiss et al. 2018), minimum wage for truck drivers (ILO 2021), a fuel 
efficiency of 0.4 liters/km, the cost of diesel fuel (World Bank 2020), and assuming that each truck 
carries 15 tons of crop.

production. We subtracted transport costs (USD/ha/kg beef) to production in each pixel based on travel 
time to the nearest city from the pixel (Weiss et al. 2018), minimum wage for truck drivers (ILO 2021), a 
fuel efficiency of 0.4 liters/km (Delgado et al. 2016), the cost of diesel fuel (World Bank 2020), and 
assuming that each truck carries 15 tons (Delgado et al. 2016).

To account for the impact of climate change on livestock productivity, we compared the ratio of 
grassland productivity under alternative climate scenarios to current grassland productivity using the 
Rangeland Production Model developed by Kowal et al (2021). The Rangeland Production Model (RPM) 
was developed specifically for Mongolia and does a good job of predicting biomass production under the 
climate conditions in Mongolia. We used RPM for the impact of climate for this reason. We did not use 
RPM for meat production as the model was unable to match published statistics for meat production. 
The projected grazing value under climate change is calculated as:

$$
projected graining value=\frac{projected gassland productivity}{current gassland productivity}*estimated current graining value
$$

where projected grassland productivity and current grassland productivity are from RPM and estimating 
current grazing value is calculated using the ORCHIDEE-GM model.

For the climate analysis, we take 2015 as the base year and use three scenarios, SSP126, SSP245, and 
SSP370, for two periods, 2030 (average of 2021-2040) and 2050 (average of 2041-2060). The monthly 
maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and precipitation for current and future scenarios are 
from WorldClim’s CRU-TS-4.06 and CMIP6 downscaled data (https://www.worldclim.org/). Data are 
grided at 2.5 minutes (~21 km2 at the equator) resolution. Other variables used in RPM are 
characteristics and soil depth. Soil data including bulk density, clay, silt and clay ratio, and pH value are 
from ISRIC – World Soil Information (https://www.isric.org/explore). The mean value at the soil depth of 
15 to 30 cm is used. Livestock numbers at each district level from 2010 to 2019 are obtained from the 
database of the National Statistics Office of Mongolia (https://1212.mn/mn).

###### 2.5 Methane

Livestock production contributes to methane emissions, another greenhouse gas. We calculated 
methane emissions from each pixel of grazing land as a function of grazed biomass and a methane 
emissions conversion factor (kg CO2e/ha; based on Herrero et al. 2013), manure as a function of grazed 
biomass and a manure emissions factor (kg CO2 eq/ha; also based on Herrero et al. 2013). We report 
methane emissions separately from land-based CO2 storage for the following reasons. Methane 
emissions are a flow each year while CO2 storage is a stock. In addition, reducing meat production from 
grazing would reduce meat supply, which would tend to raise meat prices and lead to increased 
production from other sources (e.g., feedlots, imports). Because we do not model the methane 
emissions from these other sources, giving full credit to reducing methane emissions from reducing 
meat from grazing is likely to be an overestimate of actual methane reductions due to changes in land

###### 2.6 Transition costs

Transitions between land uses typically require an upfront investment cost. For example, converting 
land into agricultural crop fields from natural vegetation entails clearing costs and crop establishment costs. Restoring agricultural cropland to natural habitat typically requires replanting and other 
restoration costs. In areas without established irrigation, transitioning to irrigated agriculture requires 
investment to install irrigation infrastructure. The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (TEEB) 
provides the cost of restoration to natural habitats by habitat type (TEEB 2009). TEEB reports on 
“typical” costs per hectare, broken down by habitat type: inland wetlands $33,000/ha, tropical forests 
$3,450/ha, other forests $2,390/ha, woodlands/shrublands $990/ha, grasslands $260/ha. To estimate 
land clearing costs, we assumed land clearing would be done by machine mulching of existing 
vegetation. Three field operations are required to convert pastureland to agricultural crop production: 
tillage, mowing, and soil finish. These costs are reported for the US (Schnitkey and Lattz 2019) and were 
adjusted for country specific fuel costs (World Bank 2021b). The International Water Management 
Institute provides irrigation setup costs for different sub-regions (Inocencio et al. 2007). We use value 
for successful projects that are defined as having projects with a greater than 10% economic internal 
rate of return at project completion. For Europe and North America we used irrigation cost data from 
Garrido and Calatrava (2010) and Amosson et al (2011). For East Asia, irrigation costs are $5,048 per 
hectare. For the optimization, all transition costs are annualized assuming a 20-year amortization with a 
3% discount rate. To calculate the net present value of the transition cost, we assumed that the 
transition from the current landscape to the optimized landscape would take place over 30 years and 
calculated NPV assuming a 3% discount rate. For details on transition costs see Polasky et al. (2023).

###### 2.7 Accounting for the impact of dzud

Weather shocks impose risks on livestock owners. In Mongolia, cold winters with heavy snow or ice 
cover, especially when this follows a dry summer, can lead to excess livestock mortality. We calculated 
the excess mortality associated with dzud by comparing livestock mortality in years with dzud 
occurrence versus years without dzud occurrence for the period 1970 to 2023. Within this period 54-
year period, 21 years are classified as dzud years, which translates to a probability of 39% for the 
occurrence of a dzud in a given year. Dzud probability has been increasing over this period. For the 
period from 1990 to 2023, 50% of years have been classified as dzud years. From 2010 to 2023, 57% of 
years have been classified as dzud years (Table 2).

To estimate the impact of future climate change on livestock mortality, we base our approach on 
estimates of an increase in dzud frequency of 5-20% under representative concentration pathway 4.5 
(RCP4.5) and a 5-40% under RCP8.5. reported by the Mongolian Ministry of Environment and Tourism 
(Ministry of Environment and Tourism of Mongolia 2018). We used a 5-20% range for SSP245, which

We assumed that SSP126 will fall toward the lower end of the 5-20% range.

**_Climate change impact on mortality = excess mortality rate (0.0317) * annual probability of dzud

###### 3. Results

Results of the optimization show there are large potential efficiency gains that can be made in Mongolia 
under current climate conditions (Figure 2). In Figure 2, the horizontal axis measures the net economic 
returns to land from crop, livestock, and forestry production and the vertical axis measures the carbon 
stored in terrestrial ecosystems. The blue dot shows outcomes under the current landscape and the blue 
curve shows the landscape efficiency frontier. The efficiency frontier relative the outcomes under the 
current landscape shows that carbon storage can be increased by ~6% (from 354 to 365 million tons) 
without loss of net economic value. As much of Mongolia is arid, there is not a large potential for 
increasing carbon storage in terrestrial ecosystems, but some increased carbon storage can still be 
achieved by better land use. On the other hand, we find that net economic returns can be more than 
doubled without loss of carbon storage (~144% increase, from $327 to $797 million 2015 USD). These 
large gains occur primarily through an increase in forestry (Figure 3). The share of net economic returns from forestry as a share of total crop, livestock, and forestry production, rises from less than 20% 
currently, to around 50% in the case where the landscape is chosen to maximize net economic returns 
without loss of carbon (“Pareto max econ,” Figure 3). Forestry generates high net economic returns on 
lands where forests can grow. Forestry expansion, however, is quite limited as it can only occur on lands 
with enough precipitation for tree survival, which occurs primarily in north central Mongolia. There is
little change in the value of grazing in moving from the current outcome to the outcomes along the 
efficiency frontier. There is some increase in net economic returns from a shift of some agricultural crop 
land to more intensive production methods resulting in increased yields (Figure 3). As shown in Figure 2, 
there are many possible combinations of potential gains in carbon storage and net economic value 
(target points 1 – 5 and points in between).

The large gain in the share of net economic value from forestry occurs despite the fact that the vast

forest land (forest land that is not harvested for timber) declines in moving from the current landscape 
to the frontier, but the overall area of forest, natural forest plus forestry land, increases. This overall 
increase in forests and the shift to forestry is the main reason that economic net returns can increase 
while still allowing an increase in carbon storage. The shift from natural forest to forestry explains some 
of the decline in natural land in moving from the current landscape to the frontier. There is also some 
shift of natural land into grazing. In this analysis, grazing land has the carbon storage value as natural 
grasslands. If land can generate positive net economic returns from grazing, the optimization approach 
assigns it to grazing land. The shift out of natural land to economically productive land is more 
pronounced in the movement towards maximizing economic returns (target point 5). Overall, however, 
almost 90% of land does not change land use categories between current land use and land use for 
points on the efficiency frontier.

Our reported numbers for the net economic value for the contribution of nature to crop, grazing, and 
forestry production for the current landscape measured in the base year 2015, was $327 million 
(2015$). This figure is not directly comparable to value added for agriculture, forestry and fishing as 
reported by the World Bank, which was $1,636 million (2015$; World Bank 2024). However, after 
accounting for differences between the net economic value for the contribution of nature and value 
added in these sectors, along with accounting for additional value from livestock beyond meat 
production, our approaches yield values of similar magnitude. Our value for grazing only includes meat 
production and excludes dairy products, fiber, and hides. Using information on quantities and prices, we 
calculated the value of meat production to be 42.6% of the total value of livestock production. In our net 
economic value for the contribution of nature, we only include the share of returns attributable to land 
and other inputs of nature, which was 6% for crop production, 30% for grazing, 27% for forestry. If 
instead, we assess the total (gross) value for crop, livestock, and forestry production (i.e., not multiply by 
the fractions attributable to land), we get a value of $2,494 million. Further adjustment to subtract the 
value of purchased inputs would reduce this figure, making it more similar to the value-added figure 
reported by the World Bank. In our results, we focus on percentage changes in economic returns 
between current and the efficiency frontier, and below, the comparison of economic returns under 
climate change scenarios, rather than the absolute magnitude of the monetary returns. While we do not 
recommend using our monetary values directly, rough magnitudes can be estimated by multiplying by 
the ratio of the World Bank reported value ($1,636/$327 million = 5.0).

Next, we analyze how climate change is likely to affect productivity of grasslands in Mongolia, and how 
this will affect economic returns to grazing and the efficiency frontier. We use three climate scenarios, 
SSP 126, 245, and 370 and calculate impacts in the period 2021 – 2040 (reported as 2030) and 2041-
2060 (reported as 2050). Climate change appears to have a slight positive effect between the baseline year (2015) and 2030 (Figure 5). These value calculations assume that grazing extent remains identical to 
baseline (2015) extent, which covers 77% of Mongolia’s non-urban land area. Doing so allows us to focus 
on how climate affects productivity and economic returns directly, without changing the extensive 
margin of grazing.

All three climate scenarios lead to increases in value from grazing between 2015 and 2030 (Figure 5). 
The gains in grazing value range from 4.7 to 8.3%, with the largest gains coming from the scenario with 
the greatest climate change (SSP370; Table 3). By 2050, however, all three climate scenarios show 
decreases in grazing value relative to current conditions (Figure 5). The declines relative to current range 
from -2.2 to -10.5% (Table 3). The decreases in grazing value are largest with the greatest climate
change: losses under SSP370 are larger than losses with SSP245, which in turn are larger than under 
SSP126. Further climate change beyond 2050 would likely show even larger losses. Mongolia has a cold 
continental climate. Some modest warming would tend to boost productivity. However, larger changes 
in temperature that are projected to occur in 2050 result in declining grassland productivity.

The regional pattern of climate change impacts on grassland productivity under the three climate 
scenarios in 2030 and 2050 is shown in Figure 6. Much of Mongolia shows declining grassland 
productivity in both 2030 and 2050, with the exception of northeastern Mongolia. The gains in 
productivity in northeastern Mongolia largely dissipate by 2050, except under SSP126, which has the 
least climate change of the three scenarios. There are fairly large declines in productivity in much of 
central and western Mongolia, particularly in 2050. By 2050, there is expected to be significant increases 
in temperatures across Mongolia (Figures 7 and 8). There is also predicted to be some loss of 
precipitation in the central and western Mongolia, which is also likely to depress grassland productivity 
(Figure 9). Eastern Mongolia is predicted to have increased precipitation, which will partially offset 
negative effects elsewhere in the country.

Reflecting the loss in grassland productivity by 2050, the efficiency frontiers under each of the future 
climate scenarios are shifted inward relative to the frontier based on current conditions reflecting loss of 
grazing value under climate change (Figure 10). Potential gains in carbon relative to the current 
landscape remain almost unchanged showing ~6% potential gain in carbon storage without loss of net 
economic value. The potential gain in net economic value without loss of carbon storage shrinks by 2050 
but still remains large. Under SSP245 this value is closer doubling rather than increasing by 115% in the 
case without climate change. This result shows that even though climate change will likely have a 
negative effect on grazing, which is the dominant economic use of land in Mongolia, better 
management of the land and allocation of land among sectors (crops, livestock, and timber) offer 
significant gains in output that more than offset the negative impacts of climate.

###### 4. Discussion

The results show that it is possible to improve on performance in terms of climate mitigation and 
economic returns in Mongolia both under current climate conditions and under future climate 
conditions. The size of these potential improvements more than offsets the potential negative impacts 
of climate change in Mongolia. In the nearer term (out to 2030), climate change may have net positive 
effects. By 2050, however, climate change is expected to have negative effects on grazing returns in 
Mongolia.

There are several important limitations of the current approach. One limitation of the current modeling 
approach is that we were not able to analyze the impact of overgrazing. The grazing models we 
considered did not capture rangeland degradation from overgrazing. One of the major concerns with 
overgrazing is that too high of stocking density will cause loss of vegetation cover leading to soil erosion 
and loss of grassland productivity. This type of mechanism was not adequately captured in any of the 
models we used to analyze grazing. It also turned out not to be feasible to capture the negative impact 
of excess grazing leading to declining forage availability for livestock. We attempted to parameterize the 
models for the amount consumed per animal needed to just maintain weight versus consumption that 
allows weight gain and found the models were extremely sensitive to assumptions that did not have adequate empirical grounding to define with confidence.  Other limitations were that we did not analyze 
the impact of climate change on forestry and crop production. There is not much existing data in 
Mongolia on which to base such an analysis, though such an analysis should be possible using methods 
developed for other countries and altered to fit the circumstances of Mongolia. Forestry expansion of 
the magnitude described in moving from the current landscape to the efficiency frontier, may require 
investments in infrastructure (e.g., roads, processing facilities), the cost of which are not included in this 
analysis. Finally, many of the impacts of climate change are likely to have local or regional effects (e.g., 
effects of dzud). There is a lack of detailed downscale climate results for Mongolia on which to base 
finer-scale resolution modeling.

# BACKGROUND NOTE 6:

##### TECHNICAL NOTES ON CCDR CHAPTER 4.2. BEYOND DISASTER RISKS

Coal mines are often located in remote areas, away from established settlements. The opening and 
closing of mining sites are, therefore, likely to significantly impact local labor markets. To estimate 
localized effects, employment needs to be measured at the sub-national level. Mongolia is divided into 
21 aimags (provinces) plus the capital city. Aimags are further divided into 331 soums (districts). The 
results discussed in this chapter are obtained from linear regression models that control for fixed effects 
at the sub-national level.

###### Correlation between coal production and coal mining employment:

Estimates of the effect of coal production on employment in coal mining are obtained from annual firmlevel data on coal production and employment obtained from the Mineral Resources and Petroleum 
Authority of Mongolia, covering the years 2016-2020. To measure the local employment effects of coal 
production, we use an econometric model that allows for multiple fixed effects, controlling for location 
of the mining site at the soum level and potential aggregate shocks captured by annual control variables.

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{c o a l\:m i n i n g\:e m p l o y m e n t=\:\beta_{1}(c o a l\:p r o d u c t i o n)+\beta_{2}(r e g i o n a l\:f i x e d\:e f f e c t s)+}\\ &{\beta_{3}(a n n u a l\:f i x e d\:e f f e c t s)+\epsilon}\end{array}
$$

Results suggest a positive, statistically significant correlation between coal production and total coal 
mining employment, including full-time and part-time workers (Table 1).

###### Employment spillover effects:

How does employment in other sectors respond to changes in coal mining employment? The analysis of 
employment spillovers uses individual-level data from the 2012-20 rounds of the Mongolian Labor Force 
Survey (LFS), with employment in coal mines identified according to ISIC division 05 (mining of coal and 
lignite).<sup>28</sup> Given data limitations (mainly due to incomplete industry classification in earlier rounds of the 
LFS, which does not allow us to identify employment in coal mines), spillovers are estimated as two-year 
changes between 2012 and 2020, controlling for regional fixed effects.

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{c h a n g e\;i n\;e m p l o y m e n t\;i n\;s e c t o r_{i}=\;\beta_{1}(c h a n g e\;i n\;c o a l\;m i n i n g\;e m p l o y m e n t)+}\\ &{\beta_{2}(r e g i o n a l\;f i x e d\;e f f e c t s)+\epsilon_{i}}\end{array}
$$

Results suggest that for every 10 jobs created in coal mining, between three and four jobs are lost in 
agriculture, depending on the model specification. Job creation in services is of a similar magnitude to 
that in coal mining: for every 10 additional jobs in coal mining, nine jobs are created in services (Table 2).

Sensitivity analyses allowing for shorter time periods and varying the size of the geographic fixed effects 
(soum or aimag-level) largely confirm these findings. In particular, the positive employment effect for 
jobs in the service sector is consistent.

<sup>28</sup>The data refer only to individuals directly employed in the coal mining industry and do not necessarily include, for example, people employed 
in the transport of coal.
