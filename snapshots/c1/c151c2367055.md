**Livingstone Falls**, series of 32 rapids and cataracts on the [Congo River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-River), extending for about 220 miles (354 km) between [Kinshasa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kinshasa) and [Matadi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Matadi) in Congo (Kinshasa) and partially along the border with Congo (Brazzaville). The total drop of the falls is about 850 feet (260 m), despite only minor rapids over an 87-mile (140-kilometre) stretch to Isangila. The falls, beginning 100 miles (160 km) inland from the coast, prevent navigation from the mouth of the river to the interior but provide, in return, a tremendous potential for hydropower, as [manifested](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/manifested) in the giant Inga hydroelectric scheme just above Matadi. Other dams have been built (mainly on Congo tributaries). The falls, named for the Scottish explorer-missionary [David Livingstone](https://www.britannica.com/biography/David-Livingstone), were crossed in 1877 by Henry (later Sir Henry) Morton Stanley, who charted the course of the Congo River.

### What is the Congo River?

The [Congo River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-River), located in west-central [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa), is the continent’s second longest river after the [Nile](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nile-River). The Congo River has a length of 2,900 miles (4,700 km) and its drainage basin covers an area of 1,335,000 square miles (3,457,000 square km). The river rises in the highlands of northeastern [Zambia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Zambia) as the Chambeshi River, flowing in a giant counterclockwise arc to the northwest, west, and southwest before draining into the [Atlantic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean). With its many tributaries, the Congo forms the continent’s largest network of navigable waterways, and though its navigability is limited by 32 cataracts in the river’s lower course, the river is an important navigational system in Africa. The Congo basin is home to the world’s second largest [rainforest](https://www.britannica.com/science/rainforest).

### Where does the Congo River originate?

The Congo River originates in the highlands of northeastern [Zambia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Zambia) between Lakes [Tanganyika](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Tanganyika) and [Nyasa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Nyasa) as the [Chambeshi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chambeshi-River).

### What are the main obstacles to navigation on the Congo River?

Navigation on the Congo River is limited by a series of 32 cataracts over the river’s lower course, including the famous [Inga Falls](https://www.britannica.com/place/Inga-Falls).

### What is the Congo River’s drainage basin size?

The drainage basin of the Congo River covers an area of 1,335,000 square miles (3,457,000 square km).

### What is the significance of Malebo Pool?

[Malebo Pool](https://www.britannica.com/place/Malebo-Pool) is a lakelike expansion of the lower [Congo River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-River) and marks the point of departure for inland navigation. It covers an area of 174 square miles (450 square km) and is divided into deep navigable channels by Bamu Island (70 square miles [181 square km]) in its center. The capital cities of [Kinshasa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kinshasa) (Democratic Republic of the Congo) and [Brazzaville](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brazzaville) (Republic of the Congo) are located on opposite banks of Malebo Pool.

**Congo River**, [river](https://www.britannica.com/science/river) in west-central [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa). With a length of 2,900 miles (4,700 km), it is the continent’s second longest river, after the [Nile](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nile-River). It rises in the highlands of northeastern [Zambia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Zambia) between Lakes [Tanganyika](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Tanganyika) and [Nyasa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Nyasa) (Malawi) as the [Chambeshi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chambeshi-River) at an elevation of 5,760 feet (1,760 meters) above [sea level](https://www.britannica.com/science/sea-level) and at a distance of about 430 miles (700 km) from the [Indian Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indian-Ocean). Its course then takes the form of a giant counterclockwise arc, flowing to the northwest, west, and southwest before draining into the [Atlantic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean) at [Banana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Banana-Democratic-Republic-of-the-Congo) (Banane) in the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Democratic-Republic-of-the-Congo). Its [drainage basin](https://www.britannica.com/science/drainage-basin), covering an area of 1,335,000 square miles (3,457,000 square km), takes in almost the entire territory of that country, as well as most of the [Republic of the Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Republic-of-the-Congo), the [Central African Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Central-African-Republic), eastern [Zambia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Zambia), and northern [Angola](https://www.britannica.com/place/Angola) and parts of [Cameroon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cameroon) and [Tanzania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tanzania).

With its many tributaries, the Congo forms the continent’s largest network of navigable waterways. Navigability, however, is limited by an [insurmountable](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/insurmountable) obstacle: a series of 32 cataracts over the river’s lower course, including the famous [Inga Falls](https://www.britannica.com/place/Inga-Falls). These cataracts render the Congo unnavigable between the seaport of [Matadi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Matadi), at the head of the Congo estuary, and [Malebo Pool](https://www.britannica.com/place/Malebo-Pool), a lakelike expansion of the river. It was on opposite banks of Malebo Pool—which represents the point of departure of inland navigation—that the capitals of the former states of the [French Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/French-Congo) and the [Belgian Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgian-Congo) were founded: on the left bank [Kinshasa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kinshasa) (formerly Léopoldville), now the capital of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and on the right bank [Brazzaville](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brazzaville), now the capital of the Republic of the Congo.

The [Amazon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Amazon-River) and the Congo are the two great rivers of the world that flow out of equatorial zones where heavy rainfall occurs throughout all or almost all of the year. Upstream from Malebo Pool, the [Congo basin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-Basin) receives an average of about 60 inches (1,500 mm) of rain a year, of which more than one-fourth is [discharged](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/discharged) into the Atlantic. The drainage basin of the Congo is, however, only about half the size of that of the Amazon, and the Congo’s rate of flow—1,450,000 cubic feet (41,000 cubic meters) per second at its mouth—is considerably less than the Amazon’s flow of more than 6,180,000 cubic feet (175,000 cubic meters) per second.

While the Chambeshi River, as the remotest source, may form the Congo’s original main stream in terms of the river’s length, it is another tributary—the [Lualaba](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lualaba-River), which rises near Musofi in southeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo—that carries the greatest quantity of water and thus may be considered as forming the Congo’s original main stream in terms of water volume.

When the river first became known to Europeans at the end of the 15th century, they called it the Zaire, a corruption of a word that is variously given as _nzari_, _nzali_, _njali_, _nzaddi_, and _niadi_ and that simply means “river” in local African languages. It was only in the early years of the 18th century that the river was first called the “Rio Congo,” a name taken from the kingdom of [Kongo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kongo-historical-kingdom-Africa) that had been situated along the lower course of the river. During the period (1971–97) when the Democratic Republic of the Congo was called Zaire, the government also renamed the river the Zaire. Even during that time, however, the river continued to be known throughout the world as the Congo. To the literary-minded the river is [evocative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/evocative) of the famous 1902 [short story](https://www.britannica.com/art/short-story) “[Heart of Darkness](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Heart-of-Darkness)” by [Joseph Conrad](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Conrad). His book conjured up an atmosphere of foreboding, treachery, greed, and exploitation. Today, however, the Congo appears as the key to the economic development of the central African interior.
