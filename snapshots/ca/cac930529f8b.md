**human sacrifice**, the offering of the life of a [human being](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-being) to a deity. The occurrence of human [sacrifice](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sacrifice-religion) can usually be related to the recognition of human blood as the sacred life force. Bloodless forms of killing, however, such as strangulation and drowning, have been used in some [cultures](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultures). The killing of a human being, or the substitution of an animal for a person, has often been part of an attempt to commune with a god and to participate in divine life. Human life, as the most valuable material for sacrifice, has also been offered in an attempt at expiation.

There are two primary types of human sacrifice: the offering of a human being to a god and the entombment or slaughter of servants or slaves intended to accompany the deceased into the [afterlife](https://www.britannica.com/topic/afterlife-religion). The latter practice was more common. In various places in [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/African-religions), where human sacrifice was connected with ancestor [worship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/worship), some of the slaves of the [deceased](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/deceased) were buried alive with him, or they were killed and laid beneath him in his grave. The [Dahomey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dahomey-historical-kingdom-Africa) instituted especially elaborate sacrifices at yearly ceremonies related to the cult of deceased [kings](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sacred-kingship). Excavations in [Egypt](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ancient-Egyptian-religion) and elsewhere in the [ancient Middle East](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Middle-East) have revealed that numerous servants were at times interred with the funerary equipment of a member of the royal family in order to provide that person with a retinue in the next life. The Chinese practice of burying the emperor’s retinue with him continued intermittently until the 17th century.

The sacrificial offering of humans to a god has been well [attested](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/attested) only in a few cultures. In what is now Mexico the belief that the sun needed human nourishment led to the sacrifice of thousands of victims annually in the [Aztec](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aztec) and [Nahua](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nahua) calendrical maize (corn) ritual. The [Inca](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Inca-religion) confined wholesale sacrifices to the occasion of the accession of a ruler. The burning of children seems to have occurred in [Assyrian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Assyria) and [Canaanite](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Syrian-and-Palestinian-religion) religions and at various times among the Israelites. Among the African [Asante](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Asante), the victims sacrificed as [first-fruit offerings](https://www.britannica.com/topic/agriculture) during the Festival of New Yams were usually criminals, though slaves also were killed.

Accusations of human sacrifice in ancient and modern times have been far more widespread than the [ritual](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ritual) practice ever was. The ancient Greeks told many [myths](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/myths) that involved human sacrifice, which has led some researchers to posit that rites among the Greeks and Romans which involved the killing of animals may have originally involved human victims; at the end of the 20th century, however, archaeological evidence did not support this claim. Some early Christians were falsely accused of [cannibalism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cannibalism-human-behavior), consuming sacrificial victims at nocturnal feasts, a misunderstanding probably due to the secrecy surrounding the Eucharistic rite and the use of the words *body* and *blood*. From the Middle Ages until quite recently, Jews were often maliciously accused of having sacrificed Christian children at [Passover](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Passover), an accusation which has been termed the [blood libel](https://www.britannica.com/topic/blood-libel).

Top Questions

### What is capital punishment?

Capital punishment, also known as the death penalty, is the execution of an offender who is sentenced to death after being convicted of a criminal offense by a court of law.

### How was capital punishment viewed in ancient times?

In ancient times, capital punishment was frequently used in many societies for crimes like murder and treason. It was sanctioned by many major religions but often avoided through alternatives like banishment or compensation.

### What are some historical methods of execution?

Historical methods of execution included cruel forms such as crucifixion, boiling, burning at the stake, and hanging. By the 20th century, many jurisdictions adopted lethal injection, but some countries still used methods like beheading and stoning.

### What are the main arguments for and against capital punishment?

Supporters argue it is just retribution and a deterrent, while opponents claim it is immoral, counterproductive, and unfairly applied, often affecting minorities and the poor disproportionately.

### How has the abolition movement impacted capital punishment globally?

The abolition movement, influenced by the Enlightenment, led to many jurisdictions abolishing or limiting capital punishment. By the late 20th century, capital punishment had become an international human rights issue, with many countries ceasing executions. However, many countries have retained capital punishment, and some have even extended its scope.

Capital [punishment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/punishment) is the execution of an offender who is sentenced to [death](https://www.britannica.com/science/death) after being convicted of a criminal offense by a [court of law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/court-law). Capital punishment should be distinguished from extrajudicial executions carried out without [due process](https://www.britannica.com/topic/due-process) of [law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/law). The term *death penalty* is sometimes used interchangeably with *capital punishment*, though imposition of the penalty is not always followed by execution (even when it is upheld on appeal), because of the possibility of commutation to life imprisonment.

## Historical considerations

Capital punishment for [murder](https://www.britannica.com/topic/murder-crime), [treason](https://www.britannica.com/topic/treason), [arson](https://www.britannica.com/topic/arson), and [rape](https://www.britannica.com/topic/rape-crime) was widely employed in [ancient Greece](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Greece) under the laws of [Draco](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Draco-Greek-lawgiver) (fl. 7th century bce), though [Plato](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Plato) argued that it should be used only for the [incorrigible](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/incorrigible). The Romans also used it for a wide range of offenses, though citizens were exempted for a short time during the republic. It also has been sanctioned at one time or another by most of the world’s major religions. Followers of [Judaism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Judaism) and Christianity, for example, have claimed to find justification for capital punishment in the [biblical](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hebrew-Bible) passage “Whosoever sheddeth man’s blood, by man shall his blood be shed” ([Genesis](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Genesis-Old-Testament) 9:6). Yet capital punishment has been prescribed for many crimes not involving loss of life, including [adultery](https://www.britannica.com/topic/adultery) and [blasphemy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/blasphemy). The ancient legal principle *Lex talionis* ([talion](https://www.britannica.com/topic/talion))—“an [eye for an eye](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eye-for-an-eye-law), a tooth for a tooth, a life for a life”—which appears in the Babylonian [Code of Hammurabi](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Code-of-Hammurabi), was [invoked](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/invoked) in some societies to ensure that capital punishment was not disproportionately applied.

 

The prevalence of capital punishment in ancient times is difficult to [ascertain](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ascertain) precisely, but it seems likely that it was often avoided, sometimes by the [alternative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alternative) of [banishment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/exile-law) and sometimes by payment of [compensation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/compensation-law). For example, it was customary during [Japan’s](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) peaceful [Heian period](https://www.britannica.com/event/Heian-period) (794–1185) for the emperor to commute every death [sentence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sentence-law) and replace it with [deportation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/deportation) to a remote area, though executions were reinstated once civil war broke out in the mid-11th century.

 

In [Islamic law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sharia), as expressed in the [Qurʾān](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Quran), capital punishment is [condoned](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/condoned). Although the Qurʾān prescribes the death penalty for several *ḥadd* (fixed) crimes—including robbery, adultery, and [apostasy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/apostasy) of [Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islam)—murder is not among them. Instead, murder is treated as a civil [crime](https://www.britannica.com/topic/crime-law) and is covered by the law of *qiṣās* (retaliation), whereby the relatives of the victim decide whether the offender is punished with death by the authorities or made to pay *diyah* (wergild) as compensation.

 

Death was formerly the penalty for a large number of offenses in [England](https://www.britannica.com/topic/English-law) during the 17th and 18th centuries, but it was never applied as widely as the law provided. As in other countries, many offenders who committed capital crimes escaped the death penalty, either because juries or courts would not convict them or because they were pardoned, usually on condition that they agreed to banishment; some were sentenced to the lesser punishment of transportation to the then [American colonies](https://www.britannica.com/topic/American-colonies) and later to Australia. Beginning in the Middle Ages, it was possible for offenders guilty of capital offenses to receive [benefit of clergy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/benefit-of-clergy), by which those who could prove that they were ordained priests (clerks in Holy Orders) as well as [secular](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/secular) clerks who assisted in divine service (or, from 1547, a peer of the realm) were allowed to go free, though it remained within the judge’s power to sentence them to [prison](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prison) for up to a year, or from 1717 onward to transportation for seven years. Because during [medieval](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/medieval) times the only proof of ordination was literacy, it became customary between the 15th and 18th centuries to allow anyone convicted of a felony to escape the death sentence by proving that he (the privilege was extended to women in 1629) could read. Until 1705, all he had to do was read (or recite) the first verse from [Psalm 51](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Psalm-51) of the Bible—“Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love; according to your abundant mercy blot out my transgressions”—which came to be known as the “neck verse” (for its power to save one’s neck). To ensure that an offender could escape death only once through benefit of clergy, he was branded on the brawn of the thumb (*M* for murder or *T* for theft). Branding was [abolished](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/abolished) in 1779, and benefit of clergy ceased in 1827.

From ancient times until well into the 19th century, many societies administered exceptionally cruel forms of capital punishment. In [Rome](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-law) the condemned were hurled from the Tarpeian Rock (*see* [Tarpeia](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tarpeia)); for parricide they were drowned in a sealed bag with a dog, cock, ape, and viper; and still others were executed by forced gladiatorial combat or by [crucifixion](https://www.britannica.com/topic/crucifixion-capital-punishment). Executions in ancient [China](https://www.britannica.com/place/China) were carried out by many painful methods, such as sawing the condemned in half, flaying him while still alive, and [boiling](https://www.britannica.com/topic/boiling-punishment). Cruel forms of execution in [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) included “breaking” on the wheel, boiling in oil, [burning at the stake](https://www.britannica.com/topic/burning-at-the-stake), [decapitation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/beheading) by the [guillotine](https://www.britannica.com/topic/guillotine) or an axe, [hanging](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hanging), [drawing and quartering](https://www.britannica.com/topic/drawing-and-quartering), and drowning. Although by the end of the 20th century many [jurisdictions](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/jurisdictions) (e.g., nearly every [U.S. state](https://www.britannica.com/topic/state-United-States-political-subdivision) that employs the death penalty, Guatemala, the [Philippines](https://www.britannica.com/place/Philippines), [Taiwan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taiwan), and some Chinese provinces) had adopted [lethal injection](https://www.britannica.com/topic/lethal-injection), offenders continued to be beheaded in [Saudi Arabia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saudi-Arabia) and occasionally stoned to death (for adultery) in [Iran](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran) and [Sudan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sudan). Other methods of execution were [electrocution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/electrocution), gassing, and the firing squad.

 

Historically, executions were public events, attended by large crowds, and the mutilated bodies were often displayed until they rotted. [Public executions](https://www.britannica.com/topic/public-execution) were banned in England in 1868, though they continued to take place in parts of the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) until the 1930s. In the last half of the 20th century, there was considerable debate regarding whether executions should be broadcast on television, as has occurred in Guatemala. Since the mid-1990s public executions have taken place in some 20 countries, including Iran, Saudi Arabia, and [Nigeria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nigeria), though the practice has been condemned by the United Nations Human Rights Committee as “incompatible with human dignity.”

In many countries death sentences are not carried out immediately after they are imposed; there is often a long period of uncertainty for the convicted while their cases are appealed. Inmates awaiting execution live on what has been called “[death row](https://www.britannica.com/topic/death-row)”; in the United States and Japan, some prisoners have been executed more than 15 years after their [convictions](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/convictions). The [European Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Union) regards this phenomenon as so inhumane that, on the basis of a binding ruling by the [European Court of Human Rights](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Court-of-Human-Rights) (1989), EU countries may extradite an offender accused of a capital crime to a [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state) that practices capital punishment only if a guarantee is given that the death penalty will not be sought.
