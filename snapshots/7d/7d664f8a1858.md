# Mumbai’s dabbawalas face decline as remote work and food apps reshape demand

## Mumbai’s iconic dabbawala network is shrinking as remote work and food delivery apps reduce demand, forcing many workers to leave the profession or seek additional sources of income.

![Mumbai’s dabbawalas face decline as remote work and food apps reshape demand](https://images.storyboard18.com/storyboard18/2026/06/dabbawalas-2026-06-2d9106334c90ec75340c6d5b15763515.jpg "Mumbai’s dabbawalas face decline as remote work and food apps reshape demand")

ADVERTISEMENT

For over a century, Mumbai’s dabbawalas have been a symbol of efficiency and reliability, delivering home-cooked meals across the city’s business districts with near-perfect accuracy.

But today, this iconic system is facing a steady decline, driven by changing work patterns and evolving consumer habits.

At their peak, the dabbawalas formed a network that transported thousands of lunchboxes daily using a simple yet highly effective system built without modern technology. Meals were collected from homes, sorted using a coded system, transported via Mumbai’s local trains and delivered to offices before lunchtime — before being returned to households later in the day. The model earned global recognition, including case studies at top business schools and attention from world leaders.

However, the system has lost scale in recent years. According to a report, the number of registered dabbawalas has dropped significantly from around 4,500 in 2018 to roughly 1,500 today.

A major turning point was the Covid-19 pandemic. With offices shut during lockdowns, demand for daily lunch deliveries collapsed. Even after restrictions were lifted, the widespread adoption of work-from-home and hybrid models meant fewer people returning to offices full-time. “Some people now go to the office only two or three times a week. This had a big impact on Mumbai’s dabbawalas,” said Kiran Gavande of the Mumbai Tiffin Box Suppliers Association.

The pressure has been compounded by the rise of food delivery platforms such as Swiggy and Zomato. Office-goers now have access to a wide variety of meals through mobile apps, often at competitive prices, reducing reliance on home-cooked lunch deliveries. At the same time, changing family structures and lifestyles mean fewer households prepare traditional meals for delivery.

The decline has had a direct impact on livelihoods. Many dabbawalas have been forced to leave the profession or take up additional work. Some have shifted to other occupations entirely, while others juggle multiple jobs to sustain income. “There are no customers, no money – what should we do?” said one former dabbawala who has since taken up driving work.

Efforts are now being explored to allow workers to combine delivery work with part-time jobs, but uncertainty remains over whether the system can regain its earlier scale.

Once a defining part of Mumbai’s daily rhythm, the dabbawala network now stands at a crossroads, caught between its legacy and a rapidly changing urban economy.
