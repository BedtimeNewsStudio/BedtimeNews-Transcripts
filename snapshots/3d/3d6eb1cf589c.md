Colton Little has had recurring roles on “Days of Our Lives” and “General Hospital,” and was a guest star on “NCIS Origins.” This month, he became one of thousands of [SAG-AFTRA](https://variety.com/t/sag-aftra/) members who were terminated by the union because he did not pay his dues.

“I fell behind on my dues over a year ago,” he tells _Variety_, saying he owes about $2,000. “I was going through a lot. It got to the point where I had to prioritize paying my dues or paying my rent.”

He is far from alone. For the last six years, SAG-AFTRA has refrained from kicking out members who fall behind on their dues, given how hard it has been for actors to find work during a pandemic, multiple strikes and a severe downturn in production.

But on Nov. 1, the union reimposed its pre-COVID policy that anyone who falls behind by 18 months or more will be terminated. That decision, made by the board more than a year ago, has contributed to an atmosphere of discontent among some members. Another factor is the board decision last month to extend the contract of [Duncan Crabtree-Ireland](https://variety.com/t/duncan-crabtree-ireland/), the union’s national executive director, for four years and to give him a 15% raise.

Crabtree-Ireland was already the highest-paid union official in Hollywood, with a salary of $1.1 million. For some, the timing of the extension — just a few days before thousands of members were kicked out — was a bad look.

“It just feels like, OK, he gets a boost while we get booted,” says Taji Coleman, who was threatened with termination but has since resolved her dues issue.

Crabtree-Ireland’s contract was not due to expire until June 2028. Sean Astin, the union president, defended the decision to act now as a “bold move.”

“Negotiating Duncan’s contract early and securing his services for an extended period of time is a sign of strength and stability,” Astin said, adding that the deal was approved by the union’s finance and executive committees before going to the full board at an in-person meeting. “The fact that the union was not in the middle of a major negotiation or an internal election cycle created a healthy space to evaluate such a consequential decision.”

The sole “no” vote on the contract, Peter Antico, ran unsuccessfully for secretary-treasurer in 2025 and has been a longstanding critic of union leadership.

“I wanted to fire him,” Antico tells _Variety_. “I’m not in favor of giving this guy money.”

Chuck Slavin, who ran unsuccessfully for president last year, argued that the union needs “change at the top.”

“SAG-AFTRA is in a full-blown crisis of confidence, driven by the removal of thousands of members, a disturbing lack of financial transparency, disastrous contract decisions, growing threats to pension and healthcare security, inadequate AI protections and leadership that has become increasingly disconnected from the very members it is supposed to serve,” Slavin said.

Crabtree-Ireland has sought to stay out of the internal political fray, which will get more intense as the union heads into its election season next spring. Extending his deal now gives him some assurance that his tenure will not depend on the outcome of that vote.

In an interview, Crabtree-Ireland declined to comment on his contract, preferring to leave that to others. But he did address concerns about member terminations, healthcare, and other issues confronting the union.

“The industry is in this moment right now where people are very anxious because of the downturn in production and what’s gone on with Warner Bros. and Paramount,” Crabtree-Ireland says. “There’s a lot of general anxiety that people are feeling in this, making (the terminations) even more nerve-wracking.”

SAG-AFTRA members pay 1.575% of their covered earnings in dues. The union is unusual in that members who are not working can remain active, so long as they pay $246 a year in “base dues.” Some members may not have worked in decades. But Crabtree-Ireland argues that it’s important for the union’s finances for members to pay something.

“I think members who are paying dues want there to be that fairness to it,” he says.

The slowdown has also caused many actors to lose their union health insurance. In recent months, Drake Bell and Heather Matarazzo [posted](https://variety.com/2026/tv/news/drake-bell-sag-aftra-lose-health-insurance-1236910684/) about being unable to qualify, while Cheyenne Jackson [launched](https://variety.com/2026/film/columns/actors-losing-sag-aftra-health-insurance-one-job-away-instagram-campaign-cheyenne-jackson-1236931317/) a campaign to help actors who are “one job away” from meeting the threshold.

The earnings threshold is set to rise to $28,933 in January. The vast majority of SAG-AFTRA members do not make enough from acting to qualify for coverage. As members lose insurance, many are forced into the individual marketplace, where premiums are far more expensive and are rising fast, partly due to cuts in federal support.

That, in turn, has led to frustration with the union.

“I believe the main reason this is happening now is because of the downturn in work, not because of any changes to the health plan or eligibility,” Crabtree-Ireland says, noting that the earnings threshold is rising 3%.

Little, who was among those terminated from the union this month, says he ended up working multiple side jobs to make ends meet. When he realized he was about to lose his membership, he reached out to the union multiple times to try to work out some dues arrangement.

“I was treated with almost an indifference when I called one time,” he says. “It felt so frustrating.”

He does not bear the union any ill will, he says.

“I understand the need to pay dues,” he says. “There’s also a reality. They have to take into account the struggles their members are facing.”

On Oct. 31, he posted an [update on Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/p/DfLdMJEun7c/) in the style of a “get ready with me” lifestyle influencer video.

“Get ready with me to get kicked out of SAG-AFTRA,” he said in the video, going on to explain the dire circumstances facing L.A. actors. “There are no auditions. There are no parts. There is nothing filming in L.A. It’s really bleak … After a lot of introspection, I’ve decided to pull back. I’ve decided not to pay my dues.”

The video was meant as an update for his friends, but it resonated with a wider group of actors facing the same crossroads.

“It’s not that I’m done with acting,” he tells _Variety_. “I’m an artist and an actor. I love the whole process. At the same time, I think I feel just like I need to take a breath.”
