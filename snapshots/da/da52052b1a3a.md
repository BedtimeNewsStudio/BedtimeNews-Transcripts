- Who is Muhoozi Kainerugaba?

[Muhoozi Kainerugaba](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Muhoozi-Kainerugaba/Introduction#ref1) is a Ugandan military general, born on April 24, 1974, and the son of Uganda's President Yoweri Kaguta Museveni.

Kainerugaba joined the Uganda People’s Defense Forces (UPDF) in 1999 and rose through the military ranks, becoming a lieutenant general in 2019. He served as head of the UPDF’s special forces command and commander of the UPDF’s land forces. In March 2024, Museveni appointed Kainerugaba as chief of defense forces.

Kainerugaba is known for his controversial statements on Twitter (now X) and his presidential aspirations. He was rumored to be succeeding his father as president. In 2024, the MK Movement transformed into the Patriotic League of Uganda (PLU). He later announced he would not run for president in 2026.

**Muhoozi Kainerugaba** (born April 24, 1974, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania) is a [Ugandan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uganda) military general and the son of Ugandan Pres. [Yoweri Kaguta Museveni](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yoweri--Museveni) (1986– ). Kainerugaba is known for his military commands and for making controversial statements on the social media platform Twitter (now known as [X](https://www.britannica.com/money/Twitter)).

## Early life, education, and family

Kainerugaba was born in [Tanzania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tanzania), the eldest of four children born to Janet and Yoweri Museveni. He was given the name Kainerugaba in honour of his mother’s brother, Henry Kainerugaba. Muhoozi Kainerugaba’s parents were Ugandans living in exile because of the political climate in their home country. While in Tanzania, his father was heavily involved in efforts to depose Uganda’s president at the time, the dictator [Idi Amin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Idi-Amin). After Amin was removed in 1979, Kainerugaba, his mother, and his siblings moved to Uganda for only a couple of years before needing to leave in exile due to rising political tensions. They returned to Uganda after his father overthrew the government and installed himself as president in 1986. Because of the family’s journeys, Kainerugaba attended schools in multiple countries during his childhood. After he completed his secondary schooling in Uganda, he attended the University of Nottingham in the [United Kingdom](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom). Kainerugaba was raised in the Christian faith. He married Charlotte Kutesa in 1999; they have three children.

## Military career

In 1999 Kainerugaba joined the military, the Uganda People’s Defense Forces (UPDF), and enrolled at the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst in the [United Kingdom](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom). The next year, he was made second lieutenant in the UPDF. During the 2000s and 2010s, he continued his rapid climb in the military ranks, reaching the rank of lieutenant general in 2019. Kainerugaba also completed military training programs in other countries during this time, including at the Command and General Staff College at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), and published a book, _Battles of the Ugandan Resistance: A Tradition of Maneuver_ (2010). As a military officer, he has served in various capacities, including as the head of the UPDF’s special forces command and as commander of the UPDF’s land forces.

Kainerugaba was one of several military officers or government officials who were accused of having committed human rights violations in the time surrounding the country’s 2021 elections, when he was serving as head of the special forces command. Many people, including opposition leader [Bobi Wine](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bobi-Wine), some of his supporters, and other critics of the Museveni-led government alleged that they had been abducted, unlawfully detained, and tortured by Ugandan forces; Kainerugaba’s spokesperson denied that he had been involved.

## Succession rumours, presidential aspirations, and social media controversies

For years, many suspected that Kainerugaba was being groomed by Museveni to succeed him as president of Uganda. These suspicions were further fueled in 2013, when a high-ranking military officer alleged that there was a secret “Muhoozi project,” which actively worked toward having Kainerugaba succeed his father when he finally stepped down, as well as to assassinate those who objected to the plan. The Ugandan government and Kainerugaba both denied this allegation. In the years after his father’s 2021 reelection, however, Kainerugaba became very open about his presidential aspirations and, at times, seemed impatient to claim the presidency. The MK Movement, a loosely organized initiative with different factions, but all in support of Kainerugaba, emerged in 2022 and openly called on him to run for president. His MK Movement supporters, often clad in bright yellow shirts, organized community projects as well as festivities in Kainerugaba’s honour. In July 2023 he merged the disparate factions and signaled that he would now oversee the MK Movement. In February 2024 the MK Movement was transformed into the Patriotic League of Uganda (PLU), described by its leaders as being a civic organization with a broader scope than the MK Movement. The next month, succession rumours were fueled again when Museveni named Kainerugaba as chief of defense forces, the influential post that commands all elements of the UPDF forces.

Kainerugaba was a prolific user of the social media platform [Twitter](https://www.britannica.com/money/Twitter) (since April 2023, known as X). He became known for his provocative tweets that sometimes embarrassed his father and had the potential to cause diplomatic rows, such as the series of tweets from him in 2021 that praised and offered support for the Tigrayan rebels fighting against the [Ethiopian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ethiopia) government troops in that country’s civil war, earning him the ire of the Ethiopian government. The next year, he generated controversy when he tweeted in support of Russian Pres. [Vladimir Putin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin) and [Russia’s invasion of Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War). A tweet from him in early October 2022 appeared to offer 100 cows as a [bride-price](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bridewealth) for [Giorgia Meloni](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Giorgia-Meloni), who at that time was poised to become [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy)’s next prime minister. He followed up, saying he would have to capture [Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rome) if his offer were rejected.

Though these tweets were dismissed by Ugandan officials as not being serious, Kainerugaba apparently crossed a line shortly thereafter with a tweet boasting that he and his troops could invade neighbouring [Kenya](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kenya) and capture [Nairobi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nairobi) within two weeks. Museveni quickly made a public apology to Kenyans for his son’s words, asking for their forgiveness, and removed Kainerugaba from his current military post as commander of land forces. However, Museveni then promoted Kainerugaba to four-star general and gave him a new title as special adviser to the president. Museveni also said that his son would no longer comment on the affairs of other countries on Twitter, which Kainerugaba promptly refuted in a tweet.

- What is eastern Africa?

Eastern Africa, a part of sub-Saharan Africa, includes two traditionally recognized regions: East Africa, which is made up of Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, and the Horn of Africa, which is made up of Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, and Ethiopia.

Eastern Africa consists mainly of plateaus and has most of the continent's highest elevations. The Eastern, or Great, Rift Valley runs through the region.

The climate is generally tropical, with temperatures lowered by the high elevations. Woodlands and grasslands are common in wetter regions, while thornbushes grow in semiarid areas. The grasslands of Tanzania and Kenya are known for their wildlife.

Eastern Africa is ethnically diverse, with 160 or more ethnic groups. Languages spoken in the region include Cushitic, Nilo-Saharan, and Bantu.

- What is the climate like in eastern Africa?

Eastern Africa's climate varies greatly depending on elevation and latitude. Lower elevations typically experience tropical savanna or desert climates, while higher elevations have temperate conditions.

Across much of Eastern Africa, seasonal changes are influenced by pressure systems from the western Indian Ocean. From December to March, winds are mostly dry, with potential for local rain. A rainy season occurs from late March to May, with air flowing eastward. June to August sees little precipitation.

Ethiopia has three seasons: the long, dry _bega_ (September to February), the short, rainy _belg_ (March and April), and the long rainy _kremt_ (June, July, and August).

The East African lakes at lower altitudes (610 meters or less) have hot, dry climates, while higher-altitude areas have conditions similar to the highlands. The Western Rift, encompassing Lakes Tanganyika and Kivu, experiences more humid conditions.

- What are some important cultures or languages found in eastern Africa?

Eastern Africa is ethnically complex, with a rich tapestry of cultures and languages. The region is home to diverse groups, each contributing to its unique cultural heritage.

Important languages and groups include:

  - **Swahili** As a lingua franca, Swahili is widely spoken in Tanzania, Kenya, Congo (Kinshasa), and Uganda. It originated from contacts between Arabian traders and East African inhabitants and has been influenced by Arabic.

  - **Cushitic languages** These languages are spoken in Ethiopia, Eritrea, Djibouti, Somalia, and northwestern Kenya. Examples include Oromo, Somali, and Saho-Afar.

  - **Eastern Sudanic languages** These are spoken by pastoralists of the Nile Valley, such as the Shilluk, Dinka, and Luo.

  - **Semitic languages** In Ethiopia, the Amhara and Tigre speak Semitic languages.

  - **Bantu languages** Most of the remaining people in the region are Bantu speakers, who are mainly subsistence farmers.

- How have geography and history shaped the economies of eastern African countries?

Eastern Africa's economic landscape has been significantly shaped by its geography and history. The region consists largely of plateaus, with the highest elevations on the continent, and twin parallel rift valleys run through the area.

Historically, eastern Africa has transitioned from traditional farming using simple tools to a system dominated by the export of a few key agricultural products and minerals. Colonial rule introduced wage labor and improved transportation, but also made economies vulnerable to commodity price fluctuations. Post-independence, efforts to promote industrial development were hampered by political fragmentation and rapid population growth.

The exploitation of resources and the imposition of economic systems designed to benefit colonizing powers disrupted precolonial economic systems and trade patterns.

**Eastern Africa**, part of sub- [Saharan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sahara-desert-Africa) Africa [comprising](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprising) two traditionally recognized regions: East Africa, made up of [Kenya](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kenya), [Tanzania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tanzania), and [Uganda](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uganda); and the [Horn of Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Horn-of-Africa), made up of [Somalia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Somalia), [Djibouti](https://www.britannica.com/place/Djibouti), [Eritrea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Eritrea), and [Ethiopia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ethiopia).

Eastern Africa consists largely of plateaus and has most of the highest elevations in the [continent](https://www.britannica.com/science/continent). The two most striking highlands are in Ethiopia and Kenya, respectively, where large areas reach elevations of 6,500 to 10,000 feet (2,000 to 3,000 metres). Twin parallel rift valleys that are part of the [East African Rift System](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-African-Rift-System) run through the [region](https://www.britannica.com/science/region-geography). The Eastern, or Great, Rift Valley extends from the [Red Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Red-Sea)’s junction with the [Gulf of Aden](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gulf-of-Aden) southward across the highlands of Ethiopia and Kenya and continues on into Tanzania. The [Western Rift Valley](https://www.britannica.com/place/Western-Rift-Valley) curves along the western borders of Uganda and Tanzania. Between the two rift valleys lies a plateau that [comprises](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprises) most of Uganda and western Tanzania and includes [Lake Victoria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Victoria). The volcanic massif of [Kilimanjaro](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kilimanjaro), the highest mountain in Africa, reaches 19,340 feet (5,895 metres) in northeastern Tanzania. The [Horn of Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Horn-of-Africa), a major peninsular extension of the African mainland into the [Arabian Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arabian-Sea), contains the vast lowland coastal plains of Somalia.

The climate of eastern Africa is generally tropical, though average temperatures tend to be reduced by the region’s high elevations. Precipitation also is affected by varying elevation: Uganda, Tanzania, and western Kenya receive plentiful rainfall, while Somalia, eastern Ethiopia, and northeastern Kenya receive far less. The region’s vegetation ranges from woodlands and grasslands in the wetter regions to thornbushes in semiarid areas. The grasslands of Tanzania and Kenya are renowned for their wildlife, in particular large migratory herds of ungulates (e.g., gnus, zebras, and gazelles) and predators (lions, hyenas, and leopards).

Eastern Africa is populated by 160 different ethnic groups or more, depending on the method of counting. Most of the peoples of Eritrea and Ethiopia—and some of those in Tanzania and Kenya—speak languages belonging to the [Cushitic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Cushitic-languages) branch of the [Afro-Asiatic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Afro-Asiatic-languages) languages. Speakers of [Nilo-Saharan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nilo-Saharan-languages) languages populate Uganda and the rift valley portions of Kenya and Tanzania, while speakers of [Bantu](https://www.britannica.com/art/Bantu-languages) languages [constitute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitute) much of the remainder of these countries’ population.

The largest ethnic groups in eastern Africa are the [Oromo](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oromo), Cushitic speakers who occupy much of southern Ethiopia, and the related [Somali](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Somali-people), who occupy all of Somalia, southeastern Ethiopia, and much of Djibouti. The [Afar](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Afar) are found in both Eritrea and Djibouti. The main ethnic groups of Eritrea, the [Tigray](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tigray-central-Eritrean-people) and the [Tigre](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tigre-northwestern-Eritrean-people), are speakers of [Semitic languages](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Semitic-languages). Both the Tigray and the [Amhara](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Amhara), another Semitic-speaking group, dominate northwestern Ethiopia. The ethnic fabric in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda is much more fragmented, with many smaller peoples intermingled or occupying discrete territories. The largest numbers of Nilo-Saharan speakers belong to the [Luo](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Luo-people), [Lango](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lango), [Kalenjin](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kalenjin), [Maasai](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Maasai), and [Karimojong](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Karimojong) peoples, while the principal Bantu-speaking ethnic groups are the [Kikuyu](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kikuyu), [Chaga](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chaga), and [Kamba](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kamba).

## East Africa

## The coast until 1856

The earliest written accounts of the East African coast occur in the _[Periplus Maris Erythraei](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Periplus-Maris-Erythraei)_—apparently written by a Greek merchant living in Egypt in the second half of the 1st century ce—and in Ptolemy’s _Guide to Geography_, the East African section of which, in its [extant](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/extant) form, probably represents a [compilation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compilation) of geographic knowledge available at Byzantium about 400. The _Periplus_ describes in some detail the shore of what was to become northern Somalia. Ships sailed from there to western [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) to bring back cotton cloth, grain, oil, sugar, and ghee, while others moved down the Red Sea to the East African coast bringing cloaks, tunics, copper, and tin. Aromatic gums, tortoiseshell, ivory, and slaves were traded in return.

## Azania

Because of offshore islands, better landing places, and wetter climate, [Arab](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arab) traders from about 700 seem to have preferred the East African coast to the south of modern Somalia. They sailed there with the northeast [monsoon](https://www.britannica.com/science/monsoon), returning home in the summer with the southwest. They [dubbed](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/dubbed) the part of the coast to which they sailed Azania, or the [Land of Zanj](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kingdom-of-Zanj)—by which they meant the land of the blacks and by which they knew it until the 10th century. South of Sarapion, Nikon, the Pyralaae Islands, and the island of Diorux (about whose precise location only speculation seems possible), the chief town was Rhapta, which may lie buried in the Rufiji delta of present-day Tanzania. Here the situation differed somewhat from that in the north, and, though tortoiseshell and rhinoceros horn were exported from there—as were quantities of ivory and coconut oil—no mention is made of slaves. Rhapta’s main imports were metal weapons and iron tools—suggesting that iron smelting was not [yet](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/yet) known. [Mafia Island](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mafia-Island), which lies out to sea here, could perhaps be Menouthias, the only island named in both the _Periplus_ and the _Guide_, although this could also be either Pemba or Zanzibar (perhaps there has been a conflation of all three in the one name).

There is little information concerning the period until the 8th century. Greek and Roman coins have been found, and there are some accounts of overseas migrations to the coast. No settlements from this period have been found.

A new period opened, it seems, in the 9th century. The first identifiable building sites are dated from this time, and, according to Arab geographers, the East African coast was then generally thought of as being divided into four: (1) Berber (Amazigh) lands, which ran down the Somalian coast to the Shabeelle River, (2) Zanj proper, (3) the land of [Sofala](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sofala) in present-day [Mozambique](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mozambique), whence gold was beginning to be shipped by about the 10th century, and (4) a vaguely described land of Waq waq, beyond. The only island that is mentioned is Qanbalu, which appears to have been what is now Tanzania’s [Pemba Island](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pemba-Island). Though there is some suggestion that in the 10th century the Muslims had not yet begun to move farther south than Somalia, on Qanbalu they soon became rulers of a pagan population, whose language they adopted. Moreover, at Zanzibar an [extant](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/extant) Kūfic inscription (the only one) recording the construction of a mosque by Sheikh al-Sayyid Abū ʿImrān Mūsā ibn al-Ḥasan ibn Muḥammad in 1107 confirms that by this time substantial Muslim settlements had been established.

The main coastal settlements were situated on islands, largely, no doubt, because of the greater security these provided against attacks from the mainland; and their populations seem mostly to have been made up from migrants from the [Persian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Persian) Gulf—some from the great port of Sīrāf, others from near Bahrain—though conceivably some too came from Daybul, at the mouth of the [Indus River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indus-River), in northwestern India. They exported ivory (some of it went as far as China) and also tortoiseshell, ambergris, and [leopard](https://www.britannica.com/animal/leopard) skins. Such trade goods as they obtained from the interior were apparently bought by barter at the coast.

Ruins at [Kilwa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kilwa), on the southern Tanzanian coast, probably date from the 9th or perhaps from the 8th century. They have revealed an [extensive](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/extensive) pre-Muslim settlement standing on the edge of what was the finest harbour on the coast. Though there is little evidence to suggest that its inhabitants had any buildings to begin with, wattle-and-daub dwellings appeared in due course, and by the 10th century short lengths of coral masonry wall were being built. The inhabitants, whose main local currency was cowrie shells, traded with the peoples of the [Persian Gulf](https://www.britannica.com/place/Persian-Gulf) and, by the early 11th century, had first come under Muslim influence. By 1300, like the inhabitants of neighbouring Mafia, they were living in Muslim towns, the rulers of which were Shīʿites. Although no houses were being built of coral, stone mosques were being constructed.

External trade was increasing: glass beads were being imported from India, and porcelains, transshipped either in India or in the Persian Gulf, were arriving from China. There appears also to have been a rather extensive trade with the island of [Madagascar](https://www.britannica.com/place/Madagascar).

The most important site of this period yet to have been found is at Manda, near Lamu, on the Kenyan coast. Apparently established in the 9th century, it is distinguished for its seawalls of coral blocks, each of which weighs up to a ton. Though the majority of its houses were of [wattle and daub](https://www.britannica.com/technology/wattle-and-daub), there were also some of stone. Trade, which seems to have been by barter, was considerable, with the main export probably of ivory. Manda had close trading connections with the Persian Gulf—with Sīrāf in particular. It imported large quantities of Islamic pottery and, in the 9th and 10th centuries, Chinese porcelain. There is evidence of a considerable iron-smelting industry at Manda and of a lesser one at Kilwa.
