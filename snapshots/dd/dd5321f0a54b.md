- What is Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf?

The Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) is a political party in Pakistan that was founded in 1996 by [Imran Khan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Imran-Khan), a popular cricketer and philanthropist. Khan created the PTI with the goals of fighting corruption and promoting social welfare. The PTI organized large rallies and courted several veteran politicians, taking a decidedly populist approach to politics. In the 2013 elections, the party received the second highest number of votes for seats in the National Assembly. From August 2018 to April 2022, the PTI government was in power under Khan’s premiership.

- How did PTI perform in Pakistan's national elections in recent years?

The Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI), founded in 1996 by Imran Khan, initially had little political support but grew in influence over time. In the 2013 general elections, PTI became the second largest party. They also won a majority of legislative seats in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province, where they formed a government known for reforms that improved transparency and public services.

PTI gained power in 2018 amid antiestablishment sentiment. However, in January 2024, the Supreme Court prohibited the PTI from formally appearing on the ballot. In February 2024, PTI candidates ran as independents and again won the largest share of directly elected seats in the National Assembly. The Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz (PML-N) formed a government with the support of the Pakistan People’s Party (PPP).

**Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI)**, [political party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/political-party) of [Pakistan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pakistan) founded in 1996 by [Imran Khan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Imran-Khan), a popular [cricketer](https://www.britannica.com/sports/cricket-sport) and philanthropist, with the goals of fighting corruption and promoting social [welfare](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/welfare). After becoming the second largest party in the 2013 legislative elections, Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) became one of the most prominent political players in the country’s politics.

## Founding and political orientation

The PTI was founded to address dissatisfaction among Pakistan’s primary ruling entities, including the Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz (PML-N), the Pakistan People’s Party (PPP), and the armed forces. Khan, who had retired from [cricket](https://www.britannica.com/sports/cricket-sport) in 1992 at the height of his celebrity, was deeply affected by the red tape involved in pursuing his philanthropic efforts, including the [bureaucratic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bureaucracy) difficulty he encountered in opening a [hospital](https://www.britannica.com/science/hospital) for cancer patients in 1994. As the country became increasingly [polarized](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/polarized) amid a public scandal involving Prime Minister [Benazir Bhutto](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benazir-Bhutto)’s family, Khan founded the party on April 25, 1996. Just days before the official announcement of the party’s launch, his hospital for cancer patients was the target of a bombing.

The party was [populist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/populism) in nature: while condemning government corruption, it aimed to improve the [standard of living](https://www.britannica.com/money/standard-of-living) across Pakistan and called for increasing the rights of women and religious minorities. But it did not necessarily gain traction from the [grassroots](https://www.britannica.com/topic/grassroots). In many ways, the party served as a political vehicle for Khan, and from the time of its launch it was vague on how it aimed to achieve the [ambiguous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ambiguous) goals outlined in its platform. The party had virtually no following in the first decade of its existence. Indeed, it gained only one seat in the [National Assembly](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Assembly-historical-French-parliament) in 2002, and that was occupied by Khan himself.

## Rise to prominence

From 1999 to 2008 [Pakistan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pakistan) was under the authority of [Pervez Musharraf](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pervez-Musharraf), a military officer who had taken power in a coup. While the PTI had little support before the coup, matters improved for the party after the country returned to civilian rule and electoral competition was restored. By the end of Musharraf’s presidency, the modes of communication (and political organization) were radically transforming as [mobile telephones](https://www.britannica.com/technology/mobile-telephone) became commonplace and offered widespread access to the [Internet](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Internet). The PTI successfully mobilized support through [social media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-media) campaigns, and in October 2011 it organized its first mass rally ( _jalsa_), in [Lahore](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lahore), Pakistan’s largest city.

While the party had previously focused on issues of [infrastructure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/infrastructure) and [justice](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/justice), it now took a more vocal stance against Pakistan’s reliance on foreign actors. Its [rhetoric](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/rhetoric) resounded especially after years of [military rule](https://www.britannica.com/topic/military-rule) during which the U.S.-led [Afghanistan War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Afghanistan-War) (2001–14) had had an outsized impact on policy in the country. Moreover, the party’s [nuanced](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/nuanced) approach to dealing with the Afghan [Taliban](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taliban) garnered support from the country’s [Pashtuns](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pashtun), who had been disproportionately affected by military activity along the northern borderlands.

By the time general elections were held in 2013, Khan had become one of the most popular political figures in Pakistan and had recruited the support of several veteran politicians. In addition to winning a handful of seats in the National Assembly, the PTI won a majority of the legislative seats in the predominantly Pashtun province of [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa) and was able to form a government there.

The PTI gained a reputation for reform in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa even as the central government, under the premiership of the embroiled [Nawaz Sharif](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nawaz-Sharif), became mired in scandal. The PTI’s policies in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa had improved accountability in the province by increasing transparency and giving citizens greater access to legal recourse. Medical services and public education had also improved under the party’s [tenure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/tenure). By contrast, Sharif was linked in 2016 to shell companies abroad, and in 2017 he was disqualified by Pakistan’s Supreme Court from holding public office. Although Khan was also found to be holding assets abroad—after very publicly promising to “keep all my wealth in Pakistan”—he was not disqualified, and the PTI swept to power in 2018 amid a wave of antiestablishment [sentiment](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sentiment).

## Government of Imran Khan

The PTI government, in power from August 2018 to April 2022 under Khan’s premiership, faced [formidable](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/formidable) domestic challenges. It inherited a balance-of-payments crisis, spurred by enormous debt commitments, such as those imposed by the [China-Pakistan Economic Corridor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/China-Pakistan-Economic-Corridor) (CPEC) [initiative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/initiative). The [COVID-19](https://www.britannica.com/science/COVID-19) pandemic, which began in early 2020, added to the financial stress as the PTI remained hesitant to [implement](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implement) lockdowns and soaring infections placed tremendous strain on the health care system. Meanwhile, after [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) launched a brief air assault on Pakistan in February 2019 over the latter’s [alleged](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alleged) provision of safe havens for militants seeking to end Indian administration in parts of [Kashmir](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kashmir-region-Indian-subcontinent), Khan stirred controversy by carrying out a crackdown on suspected militants and closing religious schools for alleged indoctrination.

Khan attempted to reorient Pakistan’s [foreign policy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/foreign-policy). He tried to court [foreign aid](https://www.britannica.com/money/foreign-aid) and investment from an increasingly wide range of countries and under favourable conditions, hoping to reduce Pakistan’s reliance on any particular foreign entity. Meanwhile, he became an arbiter between the [Taliban](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taliban) and the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), which helped bring about the 2021 [withdrawal of U.S. troops from Afghanistan](https://www.britannica.com/event/withdrawal-of-United-States-troops-from-Afghanistan) as well as improved relations between Pakistan and the Taliban. Controversially, just hours after [Russia invaded Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War) in February 2022, Khan attended a long-planned meeting with Russian Pres. [Vladimir Putin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin) as much of the world was seeking to isolate the [bellicose](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bellicose) leader.

Pakistan’s ailing economy and the government’s crackdown on militants polarized the PTI’s supporters and critics, while the government’s attempt to steer foreign policy independently of the country’s armed forces unsettled its relationship with the military establishment. To make matters worse for the party, in October 2021 Khan attempted to influence the appointment of the chief of Pakistan’s Inter-Service Intelligence (ISI) directorate, stoking additional [animosity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/animosity) from the armed forces. In March 2022 the political opposition in the National Assembly moved to hold a [vote of confidence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/vote-of-confidence) in Khan’s government. On April 10 he was removed from office after a handful of Assembly members from the PTI refused to vote in his favour.

## Crackdown on the PTI

Pakistan remained polarized even after the opposition took the reins. In July—only months after Khan had been ousted from the premiership—the PTI won most of the contested seats in an election in [Punjab](https://www.britannica.com/place/Punjab-province-Pakistan), the country’s largest province and traditionally a [stronghold](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/stronghold) for the ruling PML-N party. As the PTI gained renewed momentum, however, Khan began facing a slew of legal challenges. In October he was barred from holding public office for several years after the election commission found that he sold state gifts and concealed assets while [prime minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prime-minister). In November, as emotions in the country mounted, he was shot in the leg in an assassination attempt.

Khan repeatedly asserted that a military officer was responsible for November’s assassination attempt, an accusation that the military publicly dismissed as “highly irresponsible and baseless” on May 8 after Khan had made the claim at a recent rally. As Khan appeared for court hearings in [Islamabad](https://www.britannica.com/place/Islamabad) on May 9, he was taken into custody by dozens of paramilitary officers for lack of cooperation in ongoing corruption investigations. The dramatic arrest prompted demonstrations and violence directed toward military installations and government buildings by Khan’s supporters. The PTI was subsequently subjected to a crackdown, media coverage and Internet access were temporarily restricted, and dozens of prominent members of the party defected.

In January 2024, just a month before elections were set to take place, the Supreme Court upheld an earlier decision by the electoral commission to prohibit the PTI from appearing formally on the ballot, claiming that there were irregularities in internal elections for the party. When elections were held in February, the PTI’s candidates ran as independents and the party again gained the largest share of the National Assembly’s directly elected seats. It fell short of the number of seats needed to form a government on its own, however, and the PML-N was able to form a government with the support of the PPP. The PTI also attempted to gain seats proportionally [allocated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/allocated) to political parties by having its independent parliamentarians register with a small party, Sunni Ittehad Council (SIC), but the electoral commission determined that the SIC had not submitted a list of candidates in time to claim seats. After all the seats were allocated, representatives from the PML-N outnumbered those from the PTI in the National Assembly, despite the PTI winning a larger number of elections.

With Khan’s seemingly indefinite imprisonment, his once reclusive wife Bushra Khan (often called Bushra Bibi) assumed her first public role in the PTI in November 2024. She led a march of PTI supporters in [Islamabad](https://www.britannica.com/place/Islamabad) to demand Khan’s release from prison, although the protest was quickly suppressed after the capital went into lockdown. Bushra escaped the [chaos](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/chaos), but in December both she and Khan were charged with murder for the death of a law enforcement officer who was killed during the confrontation.

**Imran Khan** (born October 5, 1952, Lahore, Pakistan) is an antiestablishment politician in [Pakistan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pakistan) who in 2022 became the first [prime minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prime-minister) (2018–22) to be removed by a parliamentary vote. He rose to fame as a [cricket](https://www.britannica.com/sports/cricket-sport) player who led Pakistan’s national team to a [Cricket World Cup](https://www.britannica.com/sports/Cricket-World-Cup) victory in 1992. He later entered politics as a critic of government corruption in Pakistan, although he faced corruption charges of his own in 2022 after falling out with the politically powerful army.

## Early life and cricket career

Khan was born into an [affluent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/affluent) [Pashtun](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pashtun) family in [Lahore](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lahore) and was educated at elite schools in Pakistan and the [United Kingdom](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom), including the Royal Grammar School in Worcester and Aitchison College in Lahore. There were several accomplished cricket players in his family, including two elder cousins, Javed Burki and Majid Khan, who both served as captains of the Pakistani national team. Imran Khan played cricket in Pakistan and the United Kingdom in his teens and continued playing while studying philosophy, politics, and economics at [the University of Oxford](https://www.britannica.com/topic/University-of-Oxford). Khan played his first match for Pakistan’s national team in 1971, but he did not take a permanent place on the team until after his graduation from Oxford in 1976.

By the early 1980s Khan had distinguished himself as an exceptional bowler and all-rounder, and he was named captain of the Pakistani team in 1982. Khan’s athletic talent and good looks made him a celebrity in Pakistan and [England](https://www.britannica.com/place/England), and his regular appearances at fashionable London nightclubs provided [fodder](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/fodder) for the British tabloid press. In 1992 Khan achieved his greatest athletic success when he led the Pakistani team to its first [World Cup](https://www.britannica.com/sports/Cricket-World-Cup) title, defeating England in the final. He retired that same year, having secured a reputation as one of the greatest cricket players in history.

After 1992 Khan remained in the public eye as a philanthropist. He experienced a religious awakening, embracing [Sufi mysticism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sufism) and shedding his earlier playboy image. In one of his philanthropic endeavours, Khan acted as the primary fund-raiser for the Shaukat Khanum Memorial Cancer Hospital, a specialized cancer hospital in Lahore, which opened in 1994. The hospital was named after Khan’s mother, who had died of cancer in 1985.

## Entry into politics

[![Imran Khan leads a crowd of Tehreek-e-Insaf party supporters holding red and green flags during a protest rally in Lahore, with his fist raised in the air.](https://cdn.britannica.com/96/244896-050-E902C934/Imran-Khan-Tehreek-e-Insaf-party-2008.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/96/244896-050-E902C934/Imran-Khan-Tehreek-e-Insaf-party-2008.jpg)

[Imran Khan and Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI)](https://cdn.britannica.com/96/244896-050-E902C934/Imran-Khan-Tehreek-e-Insaf-party-2008.jpg) Imran Khan leading supporters of his party, Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI), in a protest against Pervez Musharraf, Lahore, Pakistan, February 24, 2008.(more)

After his retirement from cricket, Khan became an outspoken critic of government mismanagement and corruption in Pakistan. He founded his own [political party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/political-party), [Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pakistan-Tehreek-e-Insaf) (Pakistan [Justice](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Justice) Movement; PTI), in 1996. In national elections held the following year, the newly formed party won less than 1 percent of the vote and failed to win any seats in the [National Assembly](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Assembly-historical-French-parliament), but it fared slightly better in the 2002 elections, winning a single seat that Khan filled. Khan maintained that vote rigging was to blame for his party’s low vote totals. In October 2007 Khan was among a group of politicians who resigned from the National Assembly, protesting Pres. [Pervez Musharraf](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pervez-Musharraf)’s candidacy in the upcoming presidential election. In November Khan was briefly imprisoned during a crackdown against critics of Musharraf, who had declared a state of emergency. The PTI condemned the state of emergency, which ended in mid-December, and [boycotted](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycotted) the 2008 national elections to protest Musharraf’s rule.

In spite of the PTI’s struggles in elections, Khan’s [populist](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/populist) positions found support, especially among young people. He continued his [criticism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/criticism) of corruption and economic inequality in Pakistan and opposed the Pakistani government’s cooperation with the United States in fighting militants near the Afghan border. He also launched broadsides against Pakistan’s political and economic elites, whom he accused of being Westernized and out of touch with Pakistan’s religious and cultural norms.

Khan’s writings included _Warrior Race: A Journey Through the Land of the Tribal Pathans_ (1993) and _Pakistan: A Personal History_ (2011).

## Political ascent

In the months leading up to the legislative elections scheduled for early 2013, Khan and his party drew large crowds at rallies and attracted the support of several veteran politicians from Pakistan’s established parties. Further evidence of Khan’s rising political fortunes came in the form of an [opinion poll](https://www.britannica.com/topic/public-opinion-poll) in 2012 that found him to be the most popular political figure in Pakistan.

Just days before legislative elections in May 2013, Khan injured his head and back when he fell from a platform at a campaign rally. He appeared on television from his hospital bed hours later to make a final appeal to voters. The elections produced the PTI’s highest totals [yet](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/yet), but the party still won less than half the number of seats won by the Pakistan Muslim League–Nawaz (PML-N), led by [Nawaz Sharif](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nawaz-Sharif). Khan accused the PML-N of rigging the elections. After his calls for an investigation went unmet, he and other opposition leaders led four months of protests in late 2014 in order to pressure Sharif to step down.

The protests failed to oust Sharif, but suspicions of corruption were amplified when the [Panama Papers](https://www.britannica.com/money/Panama-Papers) linked his family to offshore holdings. Khan organized a new set of protests in late 2016 but called them off at the last minute after the Supreme Court agreed to open an investigation. The investigation disqualified Sharif from holding public office in 2017, and he was forced to resign from office. Khan, meanwhile, was also revealed to have had offshore holdings but, in a separate case, was not disqualified by the Supreme Court.

Elections were held the following year, in July 2018. Khan ran on a platform of fighting corruption and poverty, even as he had to fight off accusations that he was too cozy with the military establishment. The PTI won a plurality of seats in the National Assembly, allowing Khan to seek a coalition with independent members of the parliament. He became prime minister on August 18.

## Premiership

As prime minister, Khan faced a mounting balance-of-payments crisis. Though the economy was experiencing growth, imports and debt commitments from before his term had skyrocketed in recent years, especially because of the [China-Pakistan Economic Corridor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/China-Pakistan-Economic-Corridor) (CPEC) [initiative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/initiative). Just weeks into his term as prime minister, the crisis worsened when the United States withheld $300 million in promised military aid, saying Pakistan had not done enough to stem terrorism. Khan attempted to seek [foreign aid](https://www.britannica.com/money/foreign-aid) from “friendly countries” first; because a dozen previous packages from the [International Monetary Fund](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Monetary-Fund) (IMF) had failed to solve Pakistan’s macroeconomic problems, his avoidance of an IMF bailout reflected popular fatigue with the IMF. After he was unable to secure foreign aid on favourable conditions from other countries, however, Pakistan submitted a request for emergency lending from the IMF. He continued to seek foreign aid from other sources and later received promises of investments from [China](https://www.britannica.com/place/China), [Saudi Arabia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saudi-Arabia), and the [United Arab Emirates](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Arab-Emirates).

Aside from courting foreign aid, Khan oversaw several significant developments in Pakistan’s [foreign relations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/international-relations). The [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state) successfully brought the [Taliban](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taliban) to negotiations with the United States, improving relations with the country and with neighbouring [Afghanistan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Afghanistan). In February 2019, in a show of force against militants in [Kashmir](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kashmir-region-Indian-subcontinent), who had recently staged a suicide attack killing 40 Indian security personnel, [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) launched an air assault in Pakistan for the first time in five decades, raising fears of a new conflict between the two countries. Pakistan downplayed the impact and appeared to avoid escalating the situation. When India again entered Pakistan’s [air space](https://www.britannica.com/topic/air-space-air-law), Pakistan shot down two fighter jets and captured a pilot but returned the pilot to India soon afterward. After the incident, Khan [implemented](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implemented) a crackdown on militants, issuing arrests, closing a large number of religious schools, and promising to update existing laws to reflect international standards.

The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in early 2020, aggravated the country’s economic woes. Relative to his critics, Khan was slow to [endorse](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/endorse) a lockdown. In contrast, the provincial government in Sindh, controlled by an opposition party, was quick to [implement](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implement) a strict lockdown in March. Khan eventually imposed a nationwide lockdown in April; in May his government began restricting lockdowns to localities with high infection rates.

## Removal from office, subsequent political activity, and imprisonment

Meanwhile, Khan continued to face opposition for his close relationship with the military establishment, his crackdown on militants, and the fragile state of the economy. In late 2020 the major opposition parties formed a coalition, the Pakistan Democratic Movement (PDM), with the stated goal of increasing the independence of civilian government from the military establishment. Protests and rallies organized by the PDM accused Khan of being a puppet of the army and called on him to step down. In March 2021 these parties [boycotted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/boycotted) a [vote of confidence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/vote-of-confidence) initiated by Khan’s government, which he survived narrowly with the support of his coalition partners. Later that year Khan fell out with the military establishment after a failed attempt by Khan to influence its top posts.

As frustrations rose over sustained inflation, the opposition moved in March 2022 to hold its own vote of confidence; key allies of the PTI withdrew from the ruling coalition, and several members of the party also defected. As his removal from office looked increasingly assured, Khan addressed a rally of supporters on March 27 and [alleged](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alleged) he had proof that the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) was conspiring against him for visiting Russian Pres. [Vladimir Putin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin) in February after [Russia had invaded Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War). He waved around a diplomatic cipher, the contents of which were later [leaked](https://theintercept.com/2023/08/09/imran-khan-pakistan-cypher-ukraine-russia/) in August 2023 to _The Intercept_, that registered the United States’ displeasure with Khan’s visit to [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia), although it also indicated that the National Assembly had already set in motion Khan’s removal from office: “Let us wait for a few days to see whether the political situation changes.” The vote was ultimately held on April 10, and Khan became the first prime minister in Pakistan’s history to be removed by a no-confidence measure.

The economy only worsened under a new government led by [Shehbaz Sharif](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Shehbaz-Sharif) and the PML-N, setting the stage for a remarkable [comeback](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/comeback) by the PTI months after it was ousted. In a surprise landslide victory, the PTI won 15 of 20 seats in the July 2022 legislative elections of [Punjab](https://www.britannica.com/place/Punjab-province-Pakistan) province, Pakistan’s largest province and a traditional stronghold for the PML-N.

But as Khan sought to ride his momentum in rallies held around the country, he ran into significant challenges. He came under fire in August for threatening in a speech to sue police officers and a judge in [Islamabad](https://www.britannica.com/place/Islamabad). He afterward faced charges for that speech, the first charge in a wave of legal troubles in the months ahead. In October he was temporarily barred from holding public office after the election [commission](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/commission) alleged that he was guilty of corrupt practices. While heading a protest convoy from the city of Lahore to Islamabad in November, he was shot in the leg in an apparent assassination attempt.

Khan made repeated assertions that a military officer was responsible for the assassination attempt, an accusation that the military publicly dismissed as “highly irresponsible and baseless” after a rally in May 2023. Days later he was taken into custody by dozens of paramilitary officers for lack of cooperation in ongoing corruption investigations. The arrest was dramatic and prompted demonstrations and violence by Khan’s supporters against military installations and government buildings. The PTI was subsequently subjected to a crackdown while dozens of prominent members of the party defected.

In August 2023 Khan was convicted of corrupt practices after a trial court heard the [electoral commission’s](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Electoral-Commission) case against him. Khan, who had not cooperated with the trial, was sentenced to three years in prison. In a prerecorded message he urged his supporters to protest peacefully. In light of the crackdown on the PTI, however, the response on the streets was muted. Khan appealed the court’s decision and, weeks later, he was granted a retrial and his [conviction](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conviction) was suspended. He nonetheless remained in custody while under investigation for the diplomatic cipher that he flaunted in March 2022, and in October he was formally indicted on charges of revealing [classified material](https://www.britannica.com/topic/classified-document).

Although Khan’s legal woes seemed set to bar a return to office any time soon, he continued to push for the PTI’s return to power as the February 2024 elections approached. In December 2023 the PTI stirred controversy when it used [artificial intelligence](https://www.britannica.com/technology/artificial-intelligence) to deliver a speech written by Khan behind bars. Days later Khan announced his intention to run for the National Assembly in three [constituencies](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituencies), but neither he or his party were allowed to appear on the ballot. Ten days before the election Khan was convicted for the diplomatic communication he leaked in March 2022; on the following day he was convicted of selling state gifts. Despite the controversy—and the Supreme Court upholding a decision by the electoral commission to prohibit the PTI from appearing formally on the ballot—other PTI candidates ran as independents and the party again won the largest number of directly elected seats in the National Assembly. Nonetheless, the party did not gain enough seats to form a government on its own and it was unable to claim seats that were awarded to political parties based on [proportional representation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/proportional-representation).

In November 2024 Khan’s once reclusive wife, Bushra Khan (often called Bushra Bibi), made her public debut when she led a march on Islamabad to demand Khan’s release. The protest was quickly suppressed as the capital went into lockdown. Bushra escaped the [chaos](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/chaos), but in December both she and Khan were charged with murder for the death of a law enforcement officer who was killed during the confrontation.
