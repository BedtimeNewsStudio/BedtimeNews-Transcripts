Cuba's sugar industry was the backbone of the island's economy since Spanish colonial times up through 1989 when the country became the world's largest sugar exporter. Production started plummeting in the 1990s and now Cuba imports sugar to meet local needs. Luis Chirino reports from Havana.

This is sugar cane juice, a refreshing energy drink known as Guarapo made from raw sugar cane juice often mixed with lime and ice – and sometimes fermented with honey. For years, large sugar cane plantations gave Cuba important benefits: sugar, and by-products like alcohol for rum distilleries and the biotech industries, molasses for animal feed, fibers called "bagasse" used to make paper and foodservice packaging. Sugar mills burn bagasse to produce electricity. But in the early 1990s Cuba's sugar industry went into steep decline.

OMAR EVERLENY, Economist "This country used to export over 6 million tons of sugar. But the sugar industry consumes lots of raw material, like steel, fuels, and others, so its production costs are very high, and by the 1990s, sugar prices at the world market began to fall, and the industry became unsustainable by 2002. The decision was to shut down a large number of sugar factories."

The deactivation in 2002 of 71 out of over 150 sugar mills had an economic and also a social impact: hundreds of workers relocated to other agricultural activities, lots of idle lands and machinery and the death an active life in communities around the mills.

LUIS CHIRINO, Havana "Cubans consume some 700,000 metric tons of sugar annually, but with the production plunging to less than 200 thousand tons in 2025, the country has had to import sugar to meet domestic demand."

Cuba now imports sugar from countries such as Brazil, Colombia, Spain, the U.S., and Chile. Over the past years, the Cuban government has taken steps to revive its national sugar industry, a goal considered viable by experts.

OMAR EVERLENY, Economist "In a country, where you find vast experience in the sugar industry, many mechanical engineers and other professionals in the field, you have what you need to recover this national industry. I would not dare say that sugar production would again reach historically large outputs, but at least 4 to 5 million tons of sugar a year. There is interest in recovering the industry, but it requires foreign investment."

Cuba's National Innovation Council recently proposed a plan of action to revitalize the country's sugar industry. The initiative aims to increase production of sugar and sugarcane byproducts to generate export revenues and boost the country's struggling economy. Luis Chirino, CGTN. Havana.
