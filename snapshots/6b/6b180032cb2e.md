- What was the Weimar Republic?

The [Weimar Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1) was the German government from 1919 to 1933, named after the city where its constitution was adopted. Burdened by the Treaty of Versailles, the government faced immense financial strain, particularly in making reparation payments. Instead of addressing the economic issues, the government chose to print more money, leading to [hyperinflation](https://www.britannica.com/event/hyperinflation-in-the-Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1).

By November 1923, the exchange rate reached 4,200,000,000,000 marks to one U.S. dollar. This hyperinflation impoverished millions, eroded savings, and destabilized law and order. The economic disaster paved the way for extremist ideologies, including the rise of the Nazi Party. In August 1924, the [Rentenmark](https://www.britannica.com/event/hyperinflation-in-the-Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1), backed by U.S. gold reserves, was introduced to stabilize the economy.

- What does hyperinflation mean?

Hyperinflation is extreme economic inflation where prices rise rapidly over a short period.

In the Weimar Republic (1922–23), hyperinflation impoverished millions and contributed to the rise of the Nazi Party. Post-World War I, the Weimar government faced financial pressure from the Treaty of Versailles, requiring reparations to be paid to the Allies in gold-backed currency. Instead, the government printed more money to pay its debts, causing prices to rise dramatically. By November 1923, one U.S. dollar equaled 1,000 billion marks. The introduction of the Rentenmark, backed by U.S. gold reserves, helped stabilize the economy in 1924.

- Why did hyperinflation occur in Germany during the 1920s?

The hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic from 1922–23 was an economic disaster that impoverished millions and fostered the rise of the Nazi Party. Germany's abandonment of its gold-backed currency during World War I, combined with the Treaty of Versailles' demands for reparations in gold-backed currency, put immense financial strain on the Weimar government.

Rather than address the rising costs, the government chose to print more money to pay its debts. The rising cost of goods, along with an increased money supply, created perfect conditions for inflation. By November 1923, one U.S. dollar equaled 1,000 billion marks. This hyperinflation undermined Germany's democracy, encouraged conspiracy theories, and enabled extremist political views.

- How did hyperinflation affect people's daily lives in Germany?

Hyperinflation in Weimar Germany (1922–23) dramatically altered daily life. As the government printed money to pay off debts, the [mark's value plummeted](https://www.britannica.com/event/hyperinflation-in-the-Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1).

Before World War I, one U.S. dollar equaled just over four marks. By November 1923, it equaled one trillion marks. A wheelbarrow of money couldn't buy a newspaper. A student saw coffee prices rise from 5,000 to 7,000 marks between cups.

Savings were destroyed; a lifetime of savings could not buy a newspaper. Shopkeepers struggled to restock, farmers refused to sell food for worthless money, and [food riots broke out](https://www.britannica.com/event/hyperinflation-in-the-Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1). Law and order collapsed, extremist views became acceptable, and [hyperinflation ultimately helped Adolf Hitler gain power](https://www.britannica.com/event/hyperinflation-in-the-Weimar-Republic/Introduction#ref1).

- What were the lasting effects of hyperinflation on Germany's economy and society?

The hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic from 1922-1923 had devastating and lasting effects on Germany's economy and society. The Weimar government printed excessive amounts of money to pay off debts from World War I, causing the mark to plummet.

By November 1923, one U.S. dollar was equivalent to one trillion marks. Savings were destroyed, and food riots broke out. The middle class and pensioners were the hardest hit, while some business owners and industrialists profited. This economic disaster undermined the German attempt at democracy and facilitated the rise of extremist political views, including those of Adolf Hitler. A new currency, the Rentenmark, was introduced in August 1924, which was backed by U.S. gold reserves.

**hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic**, economic disaster in the [Weimar Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Weimar-Republic) in 1922–23 that impoverished millions of German citizens and paved the way for the rise of the [Nazi Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nazi-Party).

During [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I), prices in [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany) had doubled, but that was just the start of the country’s economic troubles. In 1914, Germany abandoned its gold-backed currency, certain that the war would be short and could be financed by government borrowing until the costs were ultimately borne by the Allied powers. Instead, Germany was defeated, and the new German Weimar government was bound by the [Treaty of Versailles](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Versailles-1919), which was designed to ensure that Germany could never wage war on its neighbours again. The treaty’s terms—requiring, among other things, that payments to the Allies be made with gold-backed currency—put the Weimar government under significant financial pressure, such that it was unlikely that the ailing German economy would recover. The huge amount the Germans had to pay the Allies in reparation for the war meant they could not afford to buy imported goods, and the loss of their colonies meant they could not rely on cheap raw materials. At the same time, billions of [marks](https://www.britannica.com/money/mark-German-currency) hoarded during the war suddenly came back into circulation.

The rising cost of goods combined with a dramatic increase in the money supply created perfect conditions for inflation. Before World War I, the exchange rate was just over four marks to the U.S. dollar. By 1920 the value of the mark was 16 times less. It stabilized at 69 marks to the dollar for some months. The Weimar government was still in a position to get a grip on the economy; instead, it chose to print yet more money in order to pay the reparation debt. By July 1922 prices had risen by some 700 percent, and [hyperinflation](https://www.britannica.com/money/hyperinflation), with too much money chasing goods that were far too scarce, had arrived.

The government had to print million-mark notes, then billion-mark notes. By November 1923 one U.S. dollar was equivalent to 1,000 billion (a trillion) marks. Famously, it was observed at the time, a wheelbarrow full of money could not buy a newspaper, while one German student recalled ordering a cup of coffee for 5,000 marks and then a second whose cost had risen to 7,000 marks in the brief time it took him to finish the first. Shopkeepers could not replenish their stock fast enough to keep up with prices, farmers refused to sell their produce for worthless money, food riots broke out, pensioners starved, and townspeople marched into the countryside to loot the farms. Law and order broke down. The German attempt at democracy had been completely undermined. Conspiracy theories sprouted, and extremist political views became acceptable as Weimar’s currency became valueless to the point of meaninglessness. Ultimately, hyperinflation enabled [Adolf Hitler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Adolf-Hitler) to gain power, rising along with the leaders of a coalition of extreme right-wing parties before gaining control of the movement.

A new currency, the Rentenmark, was introduced in August 1924, backed by the U.S. gold reserve, and realistic reparation payments were agreed to. Although economic mismanagement on the part of the Weimar government is usually blamed for causing hyperinflation, evidence suggests that speculators were also responsible: they started “short selling,” effectively betting on the value of the mark dropping.

### How does the printing press work?

Printing presses push [paper](https://www.britannica.com/technology/paper) against [inked](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ink-writing-medium) movable type materials to transfer text and images from the type onto the paper. Medieval presses used a handle to turn a wooden [screw](https://www.britannica.com/technology/screw) that moved the platen on which the paper was mounted; the platen squeezed the paper against the type, which was locked in place in a frame, or form. Metal presses, developed late in the 18th century, used steam to drive a cylinder press. [Flatbed presses](https://www.britannica.com/technology/flatbed-press), emerging early in the 19th century, used flat beds to hold the type and either a reciprocating platen or a cylinder to hold paper.

### Why is the printing press important?

The printing press is a useful tool for communicating and disseminating ideas on paper quickly and at a large scale. Before the advent of [radio](https://www.britannica.com/topic/radio), [television](https://www.britannica.com/technology/television-technology), the [Internet](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Internet), and other forms of mass media, printed materials (such as treatises, [books](https://www.britannica.com/topic/book-publication), bulletins, [newspapers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/newspaper), and [magazines](https://www.britannica.com/topic/magazine-publishing)) were used to share ideas quickly and efficiently for the purposes of informing large numbers of people about current events, business opportunities, and cultural and religious practices and for educational purposes.

### When was the printing press invented?

Movable type and paper were invented in China, and printing with movable type was undertaken in Korea by the 14th century, if not earlier. The printing press first became mechanized in Europe. The earliest mention of a printing press in Europe appears in a lawsuit in [Strasbourg](https://www.britannica.com/place/Strasbourg) in 1439. It reveals construction of a press for [Johannes Gutenberg](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johannes-Gutenberg) and his associates. The earliest European printing presses owed much to the medieval paper press, which was in turn modeled after the ancient wine-and-olive press of the Mediterranean area.

**printing press**, machine by which text and images are transferred from movable type to [paper](https://www.britannica.com/technology/paper) or other media by means of [ink](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ink-writing-medium). Movable type and paper were invented in China, and the oldest known [extant](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/extant) [book](https://www.britannica.com/topic/book-publication) printed from movable type was created in Korea in the 14th century. [Printing](https://www.britannica.com/topic/printing-publishing) first became mechanized in Europe during the 15th century.

How Did Gutenberg's Printing Press Work?Johannes Gutenberg invented one of the first printing presses using movable type.(more)

[See all videos for this article](https://www.britannica.com/technology/printing-press/images-videos)

The earliest mention of a mechanized printing press in Europe appears in a lawsuit in Strasbourg in 1439; it reveals construction of a press for [Johannes Gutenberg](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johannes-Gutenberg) and his associates. Gutenberg’s press and others of its era in Europe owed much to the [medieval](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/medieval) paper press, which was in turn modeled after the ancient wine-and-olive press of the Mediterranean area. A long handle was used to turn a heavy wooden screw, exerting downward pressure against the paper, which was laid over the type mounted on a wooden platen. Gutenberg used his press to [print](https://www.britannica.com/art/printmaking) [an edition of the Bible in 1455](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Gutenberg-Bible); [this Bible](https://www.themorgan.org/collections/works/gutenberg/page/1) is the first complete extant book in the West, and it is one of the earliest books printed from movable type. ( _Jikji_, [a book of the teachings of Buddhist priests](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10527116j.r=Jikji?rk=21459;2), was printed by hand from movable type in Korea in 1377.) In its essentials, the wooden press used by Gutenberg reigned supreme for more than 300 years, with a hardly varying rate of 250 sheets per hour printed on one side.

Metal presses began to appear late in the 18th century, at about which time the advantages of the cylinder were first perceived and the application of [steam power](https://www.britannica.com/science/steam-power) was considered. By the mid-19th century [Richard M. Hoe](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Richard-March-Hoe) of [New York](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-York-state) had perfected a power-driven cylinder press in which a large central cylinder carrying the type successively printed on the paper of four impression cylinders, producing 8,000 sheets an hour in 2,000 revolutions. The [rotary press](https://www.britannica.com/technology/rotary-press) came to dominate the high-speed [newspaper](https://www.britannica.com/topic/newspaper) field, but the [flatbed press](https://www.britannica.com/technology/flatbed-press), having a flat bed to hold the type and either a [reciprocating](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/reciprocating) platen or a cylinder to hold the paper, continued to be used for job printing.

A significant [innovation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovation) of the late 19th century was the [offset press](https://www.britannica.com/technology/offset-printing), in which the printing (blanket) cylinder runs continuously in one direction while paper is impressed against it by an impression cylinder. [Offset printing](https://www.britannica.com/technology/offset-printing) is especially valuable for [colour printing](https://www.britannica.com/technology/color-printing), because an offset press can print multiple colours in one run. Offset [lithography](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lithography)—used for books, newspapers, magazines, business forms, and direct mail—continued to be the most widely used printing method at the start of the 21st century, though it was challenged by ink-jet, laser, and other printing methods.

[![A modern printing press that uses UV technology](https://cdn.britannica.com/85/171285-050-12311890/Printing-press.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/85/171285-050-12311890/Printing-press.jpg)

[A modern printing press that uses UV technology](https://cdn.britannica.com/85/171285-050-12311890/Printing-press.jpg) The modern press applies UV light to freshly applied ink, leading to instant drying and durability.(more)

Apart from the introduction of [electric power](https://www.britannica.com/technology/electric-power), advances in press design between 1900 and the 1950s consisted of a great number of relatively minor mechanical modifications designed to improve the speed of the operation. Among these changes were better paper feed, improvements in plates and paper, automatic paper reels, and photoelectric control of colour register. The introduction of computers in the 1950s revolutionized printing [composition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/composition), with more and more steps in the print process being replaced by digital data. At the end of the 20th century a new electronic printing method, print-on-demand, began to compete with offset printing, though it—and printing generally—came under increasing pressure in developed countries as publishers, newspapers, and others turned to online means of distributing what they had previously printed on paper.
