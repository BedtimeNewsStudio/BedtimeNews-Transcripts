The **European Exchange Rate Mechanism** ( **ERMII**) is a system introduced by the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community "European Economic Community") on 1 January 1999 alongside the introduction of a [single currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_union "Currency union"), the [euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro "Euro") (replacing ERM 1 and the euro's predecessor, the [ECU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Currency_Unit "European Currency Unit")) as part of the [European Monetary System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Monetary_System "European Monetary System") (EMS), to reduce [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate "Exchange rate") variability and achieve monetary stability in Europe.

Following the adoption of the euro, policy changed to linking currencies of EU countries outside the [eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurozone "Eurozone") to the euro (having the common currency as a central point). The goal was to improve the stability of those currencies, as well as to gain an evaluation mechanism for potential eurozone members. As of January 2026, the [Danish krone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_krone "Danish krone") is the only currency participating in the ERM II.

## Intent and operation

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Intent and operation")\]

The ERM is based on the concept of fixed currency exchange rate margins, but with exchange rates variable within those margins. This is also known as a semi-pegged system. Before the introduction of the euro, exchange rates were based on the [European Currency Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Currency_Unit "European Currency Unit") (ECU), the European unit of account, whose value was determined as a weighted average of the participating currencies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1)

A grid (known as the Parity Grid) of bilateral rates was calculated on the basis of these central rates expressed in ECUs, and currency fluctuations had to be contained within a margin of 2.25% on either side of the bilateral rates (with the exception of the [Italian lira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_lira "Italian lira"), the [Spanish peseta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_peseta "Spanish peseta"), the [Portuguese escudo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_escudo "Portuguese escudo") and [Pound sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sterling "Pound sterling"), which were allowed to fluctuate by ±6%).[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2) Determined intervention and loan arrangements protected the participating currencies from greater exchange rate fluctuations.

[United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom") [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer "Chancellor of the Exchequer") [Denis Healey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Healey "Denis Healey") reportedly chose not to join the ERM in 1979 owing to concerns that it would benefit the German economy by preventing the Deutsche Mark from appreciating, at the expense of the economies of other countries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-IBT19Oct2015DHIM-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-IBT19Oct2015DHIM) The UK did join the ERM in October 1990 under Chancellor [John Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Major "John Major"), in a move which at the time was largely supported by business and the press,[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4) but was forced to leave again two years later on [Black Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Wednesday "Black Wednesday").[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5)

## Historical exchange-rate regimes for EU members

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Historical exchange-rate regimes for EU members")\]

Further information: [History of the euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_euro "History of the euro")

The chart below provides a full summary of all applying [exchange-rate regimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-rate_regime "Exchange-rate regime") for [EU members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_state_of_the_European_Union "Member state of the European Union"), since the [European Monetary System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Monetary_System "European Monetary System") with its Exchange Rate Mechanism and the related new common currency [ECU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Currency_Unit "European Currency Unit") came into being on 13 March 1979. The euro replaced the ECU 1:1 at the exchange rate markets, on 1 January 1999. Between 1979 and 1999 the [Deutsche Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mark "Deutsche Mark") functioned as a _de facto_ anchor for the ECU, meaning there was only a minor difference between pegging a currency against the ECU and pegging it against the Mark.

The [eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurozone "Eurozone") was established with its first 11 member states on 1 January 1999. The first [enlargement of the eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlargement_of_the_eurozone "Enlargement of the eurozone"), to Greece, took place on 1 January 2001, one year before the euro had physically entered into circulation. The zone's next enlargements were with states that [joined the EU in 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_enlargement_of_the_European_Union "2004 enlargement of the European Union"), and then joined the eurozone on 1 January in the mentioned year: Slovenia (2007), Cyprus (2008), Malta (2008), Slovakia (2009), Estonia (2011), Latvia (2014), Lithuania (2015). Croatia, which joined in the EU 2013, adopted the euro in 2023, while Bulgaria did in 2026.

All new EU members having joined the bloc after the signing of the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty "Maastricht Treaty") in 1992 are obliged to adopt the euro under the terms of their accession treaties.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-:0) However, the last of the five economic [convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria "Euro convergence criteria"), which need to be complied with in order to qualify for euro adoption, is the exchange rate stability criterion. This requires having been a member of the ERM for a minimum of two years without the presence of "severe tensions" for the currency exchange rate.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-:1)

### Parity break of the Irish pound and sterling

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Parity break of the Irish pound and sterling")\]

To participate in the European Monetary System, Ireland chose to break the [Irish pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_pound "Irish pound")'s parity with [sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sterling "Pound sterling") in 1979, because the UK had decided not to participate.[&#8202;](#ref-back-ICBpound-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-ICBpound) (The Irish Central Bank had maintained parity with sterling since the foundation of the state in 1922. "It was only in the 1970s, when high inflation in the UK threatened price stability in Ireland, that alternatives were seriously considered".[&#8202;](#ref-back-ICBpound-1)[\[8\]](#refnote-ICBpound))

### Forced withdrawal of the UK

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Forced withdrawal of the UK")\]

Main article: [Black Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Wednesday "Black Wednesday")

The United Kingdom entered the ERM in October 1990, but was forced to exit the programme within two years after [sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sterling "Pound sterling") came under major pressure from currency [speculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speculation "Speculation"). The ensuing crash of 16 September 1992 was subsequently dubbed " [Black Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Wednesday "Black Wednesday")". There was some revision of attitude towards this event given the UK's strong economic performance after 1992, with some commentators dubbing it "White Wednesday".[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-9)

Some commentators, following [Norman Tebbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Tebbit "Norman Tebbit"), took to referring to ERM as an "Eternal Recession Mechanism",[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10) after the UK fell into recession in 1990. The UK spent over £6billion trying to keep the currency within the narrow limits with reports at the time widely noting that the controversial [Hungarian-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Americans "Hungarian Americans") investor [George Soros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Soros "George Soros")'s individual profit of £1billion equated to over £12 for each man, woman and child in Britain and dubbing Soros "the man who broke the Bank of England".[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12)[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13)

Britain's membership of the ERM was also blamed for prolonging the recession at the time,[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14) and Britain's exit from the ERM was seen as an economic failure which contributed significantly to the defeat of the [Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_(UK) "Conservative Party (UK)") government of [John Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Major "John Major") at the [general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_United_Kingdom_general_election "1997 United Kingdom general election") in May 1997, despite the strong economic recovery and significant fall in unemployment which that government had overseen after Black Wednesday.[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-15)

### Increase of margins

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Increase of margins")\]

In August 1993, the margin was expanded to 15% to accommodate speculation against the [French franc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_franc "French franc") and other currencies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:2-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-:2)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: History")\]

On 31 December 1998, the European Currency Unit (ECU)[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17) exchange rates of the [eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurozone "Eurozone") countries were frozen and the value of the euro, which then superseded the ECU at par, was thus established.

In 1999, ERM II replaced the original ERM.[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] The [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_drachma "Modern drachma") and Danish currencies were part of the new mechanism, but when Greece joined the euro in 2001, the [Danish krone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_krone "Danish krone") was left at that time as the only participant member. A currency in ERM II is allowed to float within a range of ±15% with respect to a central rate against the euro. In the case of the krone, [Danmarks Nationalbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danmarks_Nationalbank "Danmarks Nationalbank") keeps the exchange rate within the narrower range of ± 2.25% against the central rate of EUR 1 = DKK 7.46038.[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19)[&#8202;](#ref-back-ECB_list_of_euro_central_rates-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-ECB_list_of_euro_central_rates)

EU countries that have not adopted the euro are expected to participate for at least two years in ERM II before joining the [eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurozone "Eurozone").[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-:0)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-1)[\[7\]](#refnote-:1)

### New EU members

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: New EU members")\]

On 1 May 2004, the ten [national central banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank "Central bank") (NCBs) of the new member countries became party to the ERM II Central Bank Agreement. The national currencies themselves were to become part of the ERM II at dates to be agreed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-21)

The [Estonian kroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_kroon "Estonian kroon"), [Lithuanian litas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_litas "Lithuanian litas"), and [Slovenian tolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenian_tolar "Slovenian tolar") were included in the ERM II on 28 June 2004; the [Cypriot pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypriot_pound "Cypriot pound"), the [Latvian lats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_lats "Latvian lats") and the [Maltese lira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_lira "Maltese lira") on 2 May 2005; the [Slovak koruna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_koruna "Slovak koruna") on 28 November 2005.[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22)

On 10 July 2020 it was announced that the [Bulgarian lev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_lev "Bulgarian lev") (which had joined the EU on 1 January 2007) and [Croatian kuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_kuna "Croatian kuna") (which had joined the EU on 1 July 2013) would be included in the ERM II.[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23)[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24)

These states have all since joined the [eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurozone "Eurozone"), and hence left ERM II: [Slovenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenia "Slovenia") (1 January 2007), [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") (1 January 2008), [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta "Malta") (1 January 2008), [Slovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovakia "Slovakia") (1 January 2009), [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia "Estonia") (1 January 2011), [Latvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvia "Latvia") (1 January 2014), [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania "Lithuania") (1 January 2015),[&#8202;](#ref-back-:3-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-:3) [Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia "Croatia") (1 January 2023),[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-auto) and [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria") (1 January 2026).

### Current status

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Current status")\]

As of January 2026, the [Danish krone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_krone "Danish krone") is the only currency participating in ERM II. The currencies of Sweden (the [Swedish krona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_krona "Swedish krona")), the three largest countries which joined the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union "European Union") on 1 May 2004 (the [Polish złoty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_z%C5%82oty "Polish złoty"), the [Czech koruna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_koruna "Czech koruna"), and the [Hungarian forint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_forint "Hungarian forint")), and Romania which joined on 1 January 2007 (the [Romanian leu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_leu "Romanian leu")), are required to join in accordance with the terms of the applicable treaties of accession.

Sweden voted in a [2003 referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Swedish_euro_referendum "2003 Swedish euro referendum") to stay out of the mechanism, despite being expected to join by the ECB, since Sweden has no opt-out like Denmark. EU members are required to join the ERM by the [Maastricht convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria "Euro convergence criteria").[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-27) Romania aims to meet the conditions to join the ERM II mechanism and enter the eurozone by the end of 2030.[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28)
