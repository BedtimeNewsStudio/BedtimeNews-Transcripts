**Janissary**, member of an elite corps in the standing army of the [Ottoman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottoman-Empire) from the late 14th century to 1826. Highly respected for their [military](https://www.britannica.com/topic/armed-force) prowess in the 15th and 16th centuries, the Janissaries became a powerful political force within the Ottoman state. During peacetime they were used to garrison frontier towns and police the capital, [Istanbul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Istanbul). They [constituted](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituted) the first modern standing army in Europe.

[![Inside the powerful Ottoman army](https://cdn.britannica.com/82/179482-138-CE1A09E1/Ottoman-Empire-army-archers.jpg?w=800&h=450&c=crop)](https://www.britannica.com/video/Ottoman-Empire-army-archers/-194281)

The Janissary corps was originally staffed through [_devşirme_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devsirme), a system of tribute by which Christian youths were taken from the [Balkan provinces](https://www.britannica.com/place/Balkans), converted to [Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islam), and drafted into Ottoman service. Subject to strict rules, including celibacy, they were organized into three unequal divisions ( _cemaat_, _bölükhalkı_, and _segban_) and commanded by an [_ağā_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/aga). In the late 16th century the celibacy rule and other restrictions were relaxed, and by the early 18th century the original method of recruitment had been abandoned, opening the ranks to Muslim Turks. The Janissaries were known particularly for their [archery](https://www.britannica.com/sports/archery), but by the 16th century they had also become a [formidable](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/formidable) firepower [contingent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/contingent).

[![Osman II](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/173029-050-AC2EAA0D/Osman-II-procession-manuscript-illustration-guards-Janissaries.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/173029-050-AC2EAA0D/Osman-II-procession-manuscript-illustration-guards-Janissaries.jpg)

[Osman II](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/173029-050-AC2EAA0D/Osman-II-procession-manuscript-illustration-guards-Janissaries.jpg) Osman II in a procession of Janissaries and guards, manuscript illustration, Turkey, c. 1620–22; in the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (M.85.237.42).(more)

The supreme prowess and [discipline](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/discipline) of the Janissaries allowed them to become increasingly powerful in the palace. From the reign of [Bayezid II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bayezid-II) (1481–1512), they regularly required [sultans](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sultan-Islamic-title) to provide extra pay in exchange for the support of the corps. The maintenance costs of the armed forces proved increasingly unaffordable for the empire, however, and augmented the growing tensions between the Janissaries and the [sultan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sultan-Islamic-title). An attempt by [Osman II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Osman-II) (1618–22) to discipline them and cut their pay led to his execution at their hands. They frequently engineered palace coups thereafter. In one instance, they conspired with court officials and overthrew [İbrahim](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ibrahim-Ottoman-sultan) for his sheer incompetence in governance.

In the early 19th century the Janissaries resisted the adoption of European reforms by the Ottoman army. Their end came in June 1826 in the so-called [Auspicious Incident](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Auspicious-Incident). On learning of the formation of new, Westernized troops, the Janissaries revolted. Sultan [Mahmud II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mahmud-II-Ottoman-sultan) declared war on the rebels and, on their refusal to [surrender](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/surrender), had cannon fire directed on their barracks. Most of the Janissaries were killed, and those who were taken prisoner were executed.

Top Questions

### Where did the Ottoman Empire start?

The Ottoman Empire was founded in [Anatolia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Anatolia), the location of modern-day Turkey. Originating in Söğüt (near [Bursa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bursa-Turkey), Turkey), the Ottoman dynasty expanded its reign early on through extensive raiding. This was enabled by the decline of the [Seljuq](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Seljuq) dynasty, the previous rulers of Anatolia, who were suffering defeat from Mongol invasion.

### How did the Ottoman Empire start?

The Ottoman Empire began at the very end of the 13th century with a series of raids from Turkic warriors (known as ghazis) led by [Osman I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Osman-I), a prince ( [bey](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bey)) whose father, Ertugrul, had established a power base in Söğüt (near [Bursa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bursa-Turkey), Turkey). Osman and his warriors took advantage of a declining [Seljuq](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Seljuq) dynasty, which had been severely weakened by the Mongol invasions. The Ottoman dynasty continued to expand for several generations, controlling much of southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa at its peak. Osman’s grandson [Murad I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Murad-I) laid the foundation for an institutionalized Ottoman state, continued by Murad’s son [Bayezid I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bayezid-I).

### Why was the Ottoman Empire called “the sick man of Europe”?

After the peak of Ottoman rule under [Süleyman the Magnificent](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Suleyman-the-Magnificent) in the 16th century, the Ottoman Empire struggled to maintain its bloated bureaucracy and decentralized political structure. Several attempts at reform kept the empire afloat but mostly addressed immediate issues, and any success was short-lived. The most far-reaching of these reforms, the [Tanzimat](https://www.britannica.com/event/Tanzimat), contributed to a debt crisis in the 1870s. Its fragile state left it unable to withstand defeat in [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I), and most of its territories were divided as spoils as the empire disintegrated.

### How did the Ottoman Empire end?

The Ottoman Empire disintegrated and was partitioned after its defeat in [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I). The empire had already been in decline for centuries, struggling to maintain a bloated bureaucracy or a centralized administrative structure after various attempts at reform. The problem was exacerbated further by the rise of more localized interests across the empire, such as the rise of [nationalist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nationalism) movements. Upon the Ottomans’ defeat in World War I, a combination of nationalist movements and partition agreements among the Allied powers forced its disintegration into numerous territories, with [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) as the empire’s immediate successor.

**Ottoman Empire**, empire created by Turkish tribes in [Anatolia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Anatolia) (Asia Minor) that grew to be one of the most powerful states in the world during the 15th and 16th centuries. The Ottoman period spanned more than 600 years and came to an end only in 1922, when it was replaced by the [Turkish Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) and various successor states in southeastern [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) and the [Middle East](https://www.britannica.com/place/Middle-East). At its height the [empire](https://www.britannica.com/topic/empire-political-science) [encompassed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encompassed) most of southeastern Europe to the gates of [Vienna](https://www.britannica.com/place/Vienna), including present-day [Hungary](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hungary), the [Balkan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Balkans) region, [Greece](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greece), and parts of [Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ukraine); portions of the Middle East now occupied by [Iraq](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iraq), [Syria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Syria), [Israel](https://www.britannica.com/place/Israel), and [Egypt](https://www.britannica.com/place/Egypt); [North Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Africa) as far west as [Algeria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Algeria); and large parts of the [Arabian Peninsula](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arabia-peninsula-Asia). The term Ottoman is a dynastic appellation derived from [Osman I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Osman-I) (Arabic: [ʿUthmān](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Osman-I)), the nomadic [Turkmen](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Turkmen-people) chief who founded both the [dynasty](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dynasty) and the empire about 1300.

## The Ottoman state to 1481: the age of expansion

The first period of Ottoman history was characterized by almost continuous territorial expansion, during which Ottoman dominion spread out from a small northwestern Anatolian principality to cover most of southeastern Europe and Anatolia. The political, economic, and social institutions of the classical Islamic empires were amalgamated with those inherited from [Byzantium](https://www.britannica.com/place/Byzantine-Empire) and the great Turkish empires of [Central Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Central-Asia) and were reestablished in new forms that were to characterize the area into modern times.

## Origins and expansion of the Ottoman state, c. 1300–1402

In their initial stages of expansion, the Ottomans were leaders of the Turkish warriors for the faith of [Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islam), known by the honorific title _[ghāzī](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ghazi-Ottoman-soldier)_ (Arabic: “raider”), who fought against the shrinking Christian [Byzantine](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Byzantine) state. The ancestors of Osman I, the founder of the dynasty, were members of the Kayı tribe who had entered Anatolia along with a mass of Turkmen [Oğuz](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oguz) nomads. Those nomads, migrating from Central Asia, established themselves as the [Seljuq dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Seljuq) in [Iran](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran) and [Mesopotamia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mesopotamia-historical-region-Asia) in the mid-11th century, overwhelmed Byzantium after the [Battle of Manzikert](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Manzikert) (1071), and occupied eastern and central Anatolia during the 12th century. The ghazis fought against the [Byzantines](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Byzantines) and then the Mongols, who invaded Anatolia following the establishment of the [Il-Khanid](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Il-Khanid-dynasty) (Ilhanid) empire in Iran and Mesopotamia in the last half of the 13th century. With the disintegration of [Seljuq](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Seljuq) power and its replacement by [Mongol](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mongol) suzerainty, enforced by direct military occupation of much of eastern Anatolia, independent Turkmen principalities—one of which was led by Osman—emerged in the remainder of Anatolia.
