### What is Standard Oil?

[Standard Oil](https://www.britannica.com/money/Standard-Oil) (in full, Standard Oil Company and Trust) was an American company and corporate trust built by [John D. Rockefeller](https://www.britannica.com/money/John-D-Rockefeller) and associates. Incorporated in Ohio in 1870, it expanded through [acquisitions, mergers](https://www.britannica.com/money/mergers-and-acquisitions-strategy), and favorable railroad rebates. In 1882, Standard Oil and its affiliated companies were combined into the Standard Oil Trust. By then, it controlled the refining of 90% to 95% of all [oil](https://www.britannica.com/science/petroleum) produced in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States).

### When was Standard Oil founded?

[Standard Oil Company](https://www.britannica.com/money/Standard-Oil) was incorporated in [Ohio](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ohio-state) in 1870, but the company’s origins date to 1863, when [John D. Rockefeller](https://www.britannica.com/money/John-D-Rockefeller) joined Maurice B. Clark and Samuel Andrews in a [Cleveland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cleveland-Ohio), Ohio, [oil-refining](https://www.britannica.com/technology/petroleum-refining) business. Rockefeller bought out Clark in 1865, and [Henry M. Flagler](https://www.britannica.com/money/Henry-M-Flagler) became a partner in the venture in 1867. The firm of Rockefeller, Andrews, and Flagler was operating the largest refineries in Cleveland when Standard Oil Company was incorporated.

### Why was Standard Oil considered a monopoly?

[Standard Oil](https://www.britannica.com/money/Standard-Oil) grew by eliminating competitors, merging with other firms, and obtaining favorable railroad rebates. By 1880, it controlled the refining of 90% to 95% of all oil produced in the United States. Standard Oil was an American company and corporate trust that from 1870 to 1911 was the industrial empire of [John D. Rockefeller](https://www.britannica.com/money/John-D-Rockefeller) of the famed [Rockefeller family](https://www.britannica.com/money/Rockefeller-family), controlling almost all [oil](https://www.britannica.com/science/petroleum) production, processing, marketing, and transportation in the United States.

## The rise of Standard Oil

The company’s origins date to 1863, when Rockefeller joined Maurice B. Clark and Samuel Andrews in a Cleveland, Ohio, oil-refining business. In 1865 Rockefeller bought out Clark, and two years later he invited [Henry M. Flagler](https://www.britannica.com/money/Henry-M-Flagler) to join as a partner in the venture. By 1870 the firm of Rockefeller, Andrews, and Flagler was operating the largest refineries in Cleveland, and these and related facilities became the property of the new Standard Oil Company, incorporated in Ohio in 1870. By 1880, through elimination of competitors, mergers with other firms, and use of favorable railroad rebates, it controlled the refining of 90 to 95 percent of all oil produced in the United States.

## How one company reshaped  American business

© Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.

By the late 19th century, [John D. Rockefeller (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/money/John-D-Rockefeller)’s [Standard Oil Company (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/money/Standard-Oil) dominated the U.S. oil industry. Its enormous scale helped reduce costs and made distribution more efficient—but it also raised a question the country was only beginning to confront: **How much economic power should one company be allowed to have?**

## Ida Tarbell takes on Standard Oil

© Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.

In 1904, muckraking journalist [Ida Tarbell (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ida-Tarbell) published **The History of the Standard Oil Company,** a detailed account of Rockefeller’s rise and the tactics Standard Oil used to dominate the industry. Her reporting helped turn the company into a national symbol of [monopoly power (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/money/monopoly-economics) and added momentum to the growing [antitrust (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/money/antitrust-law) movement.

## The antitrust fight reaches  the Supreme Court

© Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.

Congress had passed the [Sherman Antitrust Act (opens in a new tab)](https://www.britannica.com/event/Sherman-Antitrust-Act) in 1890, giving the federal government a tool to challenge monopolistic business practices. In 1906, the government sued Standard Oil. Five years later, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the company had violated the law and ordered it dissolved.

## The breakup

© Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.

In 1911, Standard Oil was divided into **34 independent companies.** Some disappeared. Others merged, changed names, or combined with former rivals. A handful of those lines can still be traced to major energy companies today—including **ExxonMobil, Chevron, BP, ConocoPhillips, Marathon Petroleum, Phillips 66, and Shell.**

## The Standard Oil Trust and antitrust

![Rockefeller, John D.](https://cdn.britannica.com/11/70911-050-6B0D14FE/John-D-Rockefeller.jpg?w=385)

Open full sized image

John D. Rockefeller.

Library of Congress, Washington, D.C.

In 1882 the Standard Oil Company and affiliated companies that were engaged in producing, refining, and marketing oil were combined in the Standard Oil Trust, created by the Standard Oil Trust Agreement signed by nine trustees, including Rockefeller. By the agreement, companies could be purchased, created, dissolved, merged, or divided; eventually, the trustees governed some 40 corporations, of which 14 were wholly owned.

Founded in 1882, Standard Oil of New Jersey was one component of the trust; by design the Standard Oil Trust embraced a maze of legal structures, which made its workings virtually impervious to public investigation and understanding. As [Ida Tarbell](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ida-Tarbell) wrote in her _History of the Standard Oil Company_ (1904), “You could argue its existence from its effects, but you could not prove it.” In 1892 the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved, but it effectively continued to operate from headquarters in New York City.

In 1899, however, the company renamed its New Jersey firm Standard Oil Company (New Jersey) and incorporated it as a holding company. All assets and interests formerly grouped in the trust were transferred to the New Jersey company.

![Standard Oil Strike](https://cdn.britannica.com/10/134110-050-881603F3/rioting-Workers-strike-Standard-Oil-Bayonne-New-1915.jpg?w=385)

Although consolidation did advance the large-scale production and distribution of oil products, many critics believed that the resulting concentration of economic power was becoming excessive. In 1906 the U.S. government brought suit against Standard Oil Company (New Jersey) under the [Sherman Antitrust Act](https://www.britannica.com/event/Sherman-Antitrust-Act) of 1890; in 1911 the New Jersey company was ordered to divest itself of its major holdings—33 companies in all.

## The breakup and Standard Oil’s legacy

In 1911, after dissolution of the Standard Oil empire, eight companies retained “Standard Oil” in their names, but by the late 20th century the name had almost passed into history. In 1931 Standard Oil Company of New York merged with Vacuum Oil Company (another trust company) to form Socony-Vacuum, which in 1966 became [Mobil Oil Corporation](https://www.britannica.com/money/Mobil-Corporation). Standard Oil (Indiana) absorbed Standard Oil of Nebraska in 1939 and Standard Oil of Kansas in 1948 and was renamed [Amoco Corporation](https://www.britannica.com/money/Amoco-Corporation) in 1985.

Standard Oil of California acquired Standard Oil of Kentucky in 1961 and was renamed [Chevron Corporation](https://www.britannica.com/money/Chevron-Corporation) in 1984. Standard Oil Company (New Jersey) changed its name to [Exxon Corporation](https://www.britannica.com/money/Exxon-Corporation) in 1972. [British Petroleum Company PLC](https://www.britannica.com/money/BP-PLC) completed the purchase of Standard Oil Company (Ohio) in 1987, and in 1998 British Petroleum (renamed [BP](https://www.britannica.com/money/BP-PLC)) merged with Amoco. Exxon and Mobil merged in 1999, and Chevron merged with [Texaco](https://www.britannica.com/money/Texaco-Inc) in 2001.

Other companies that once were part of the trust included [Atlantic Richfield Company](https://www.britannica.com/money/Atlantic-Richfield-Company) (acquired by BP in 2000), Buckeye Pipe Line Company (Ohio), Chesebrough-Pond’s Inc. (now part of [Unilever](https://www.britannica.com/money/Unilever)), [Pennzoil Company](https://www.britannica.com/money/Pennzoil-Company) (acquired by [Shell PLC](https://www.britannica.com/money/Shell-PLC) in 2002), and Union Tank Car Company (New Jersey). More than a century after the breakup, Standard Oil’s legacy remains embedded in the structure of the [global energy sector](https://www.britannica.com/money/energy-stocks).
