# The Awful Truth: ACLU study of low-level arrests and race casts a harsh light on Minneapolis

[**Mike Mosedale**](https://minnlawyer.com/author/mikemosedale/)//June 4, 2015//

Contrary to the impression created by the initial news coverage, there were at least three non-appalling statistics buried in the latest study of race and policing practices in Minneapolis.

No racial disparities were identified in the Minneapolis Police Department’s enforcement of possession of injection equipment, dog leash/cleanup violations and unauthorized beach swimming.

But aside from the apparently equitable treatment of the junkie/pet scofflaw/skinny dipper cohort, there were not many heartening numbers to be gleaned from the American Civil Liberties Union’s deep data dive — a demographic analysis of nearly 100,000 low-level arrests made the Minneapolis Police Department.

For all low-level arrests over the 33-month study period, however, blacks were 8.7 times more likely to be arrested than whites. The report — titled “Picking up the Pieces: Policing in America, a Minneapolis Case Study” — documented a similarly grim dynamic for Native Americans.

Broadly speaking, such findings are nothing new.

For more than two decades, Minneapolis’ reputation as a model of progressive governance has been dinged by a steady stream of studies highlighting the gaps between the city’s relatively prosperous white majority and its poorer minority communities.

Whether the focus is on education, income, health, home ownership or the criminal justice system, the bottom line is usually the same: Minneapolis, you’ve got a problem.

That said, some of the multipliers in the ACLU report are pretty staggering. For the crime of loitering with intent to commit a narcotics offense blacks were arrested at 26 times the rate for whites, 23 times the rate for whites for excessive noise from a motor vehicle, and 20 times the rate for whites for truancy and contempt of court.

While cause and effect are always subject to debate, some of the report’s findings are highly suggestive of racial profiling. Most notable in that regard: while black motorists were markedly more likely than whites to be pulled over for driving violations, the report found that those disparities were lowest at night “when visibility is limited and officers are less likely to be able to identify the race of drivers.”

With the national conversation increasingly focused on issues of race and criminal justice in the wake of Ferguson and Baltimore, the release of the report could not have been much more timely, said Emma Andersson, staff attorney at the ACLU’s Criminal Law Reform Project and the lead author of the study, which was produced in collaboration with the ACLU of Minnesota.

“Criminal justice reform didn’t used to be a subject that many people cared about or wanted to talk about,” said Andersson, who noted that the project was already underway before the Ferguson unrest.

In an interview with Minnesota Lawyer, Andersson — a 2008 graduate of Yale Law School who had never set foot in Minneapolis prior to the assignment — talked about the report’s findings, the surprisingly positive reception from some local officials and why the ACLU decided to target Minneapolis in the first place.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

**Minnesota Lawyer:** So why did the ACLU want to focus on Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** In 2013, we published a nationwide look at marijuana arrests with a state by state ranking, “Marijuana in Black and White.” Of the 50 states and D.C., Minnesota was the third-worst in terms of disparities. We wanted to see whether this phenomenon was just with marijuana possession, or whether these disparities would exist with other types of low level offenses. Being that Minneapolis is the largest city in the state and hearing lots of complaints about problems with the Minneapolis Police Department in particular, we decided to look there.

**ML:** The ACLU has also examined race and policing practices in other cities, including Detroit, Philadelphia, Chicago and New York. Where does Minneapolis stack up?

**Andersson:** At this point, we can’t do much ranking. The only nationwide comparison we have is the marijuana arrest report, where the disparities in Minnesota, and in Minneapolis, were bad. But that’s just one offense.

Each department keeps data in a different way and most don’t publish data on any regular basis, which is emblematic of the larger problems with police data. We don’t even know how many people are killed by the police around the country.

The New York report looked at stop-and-frisk. Minneapolis doesn’t yet keep track on a systematic basis of its equivalent of stop-and-frisk, which is officer initiated suspicious person stops. Some of the other studies didn’t look at the same range of offenses we looked at here.

**ML:** One of the more intriguing parts of the report looked at eight officers who made a disproportionate number of low-level arrests, including one officer who made more than 2,000 of them over the study period. The median number of such arrests for all officers was around 50. Why didn’t you identify that officer?

**Andersson:** Our data includes demographic information of the arrestee, time of day, location, and offense. But it does not include all 96,000 arrest reports. We’d need to look at the arrest reports before making larger conclusions about those individual officers. But this really isn’t about just those eight officers. It’s about systems change. When we took out the arrests from those eight officers and recalculated the disparities, they were still about the same, still very bad.

**ML:** Did you ask the police leadership for any explanations about the officer with 2,000 plus arrests?

**Andersson:** They know that data point is there. I don’t think it’s our job to talk about the discipline or retraining of one officer. We’re trying to spend our time talking about system reform.

**ML:** Over the study period, you also documented a sizeable number of low-level arrests that were classified “doesn’t fit any crim.” [Sic] What’s that?

**Andersson:** I was told that’s what the computer spits out when an officer makes an arrest and enters a description that the computer doesn’t understand. I have no way of checking that. It doesn’t particularly make me feel better. Part of an officer’s job is to know what the laws are, what they’re allowed to enforce. If there’s a problem with the description such that it can’t be recognized by the computer, maybe there’s also a problem with that officer’s understanding of the law.

**ML:** And there were quite a lot of those “doesn’t fit any crime” cases, right?

**Andersson:** Nine hundred and seven. It’s more concerning when you couple that with the fact that 72 percent of the arrestees were black.

**ML:** Does the MPD incentive its officers to make low level arrests?

**Andersson:** It’s hard to know. For instance, each precinct has its own goals and recommendations for the officers who work in that precinct and then they get layered on top of the overall police department evaluation process. Those goals and recommendations have not been made available to us.

It’s not just a question of do the evaluation metrics incentive low level arrests? It’s also, do they incentive other, better things?  Do they provide measures for officers to show that they’re doing positive things in the community without racking up arrest numbers?

**ML:** Did you have trouble getting other information from the department?

**Andersson:** The department has a disciplinary matrix — sort of like sentencing guidelines — where you have certain alleged misconduct with various mitigating and aggravating factors, and it gives you a presumptive range of what the discipline should be. We’d like to know how these decisions are made. The process should be publically available.

**ML:** Did they say that’s not public?

**Andersson:** They just that it hasn’t been made public and they’re looking into it.

**ML:** What have you made of the response to the report from Police Chief Janee´ Harteau?

**Andersson:** After we released a much smaller data set in October — different data, pulled from uniform crime reports — the chief said, ‘I’ve seen this ACLU report and I’m troubled by the numbers.’  That’s not the response we usually get when we tell a police department they have a racial disparity problem. More often, we wind up getting into arguments about what the data shows.

This data came directly from the Minneapolis Police Department, so it’s hard to argue with. In the last day or two, the chief has said our data is somewhat misleading because we’re not accounting for the number of repeat offenders and that there are a lot of people who are repeat offenders.

**ML:** Do you think that’s a legitimate criticism?

**Andersson:** Not at all. If we see the same people arrested again and again for these non-felony offenses, I think that shores up our point. The criminal justice system isn’t the right way to deal with this kind of behavior because arresting people over and over again doesn’t appear to work and it doesn’t appear to be a good use of police time.

**ML:** But does it skew the analysis?

**Andersson:** No. We wanted to know that, so we took out those eight officers and recalculated the disparities. The numbers were around the same and still very bad.

**ML:** Have you looked at how all these low-level arrests wind up getting adjudicated?

**Andersson:** I would love to do that, but I don’t know that we have the resources. I think we’ll try to do a sampling.

**ML:** When it’s all said and done, do you think Minneapolis is an outlier?

**Andersson:** The scary and sad thing is that we find these dynamics whenever we look at a police department. It happens in unique ways in each city. But everywhere we look we find some kind of disparity in the policing and the criminal justice overall.

**ML:** You found that black drivers in Minneapolis were pulled over at a higher rate during daylight hours than at night, when they are less visible and therefore less easily identified by race. Can you see any explanations for that aside from profiling?

**Andersson:** That’s the one that jumps out most clearly to us. But we don’t have the arrest reports, so there are certainly details we’re missing. I’d be interested in hearing from the police what they think might account for that. I’m not sure what else it could be.

**ML:** Whom did you talk to on your trips to Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** We had several meetings with the police chief and her executive team over the last year or so. We met with the mayor’s chief of staff and some other folks to give them a sense of what we’re doing as part of our research and our due diligence. And we talked to a lot of folks on the ground to get the human side of that story. We didn’t want to just rely on numbers. We wanted to have a better sense of folks’ experience, both currently and historically.

**ML:** Was the MPD cooperative?

**Andersson:** We didn’t have to go to court and sue to get this data. They complied with our public records request, which doesn’t always happen, but we also had to pay about a thousand dollars for that data.

**ML:** Is that typical?

**Andersson:** The public records laws vary by state. When we do these large data requests, the one thing that’s consistent — it always costs a pretty penny. It’s not really accessible unless you’re wealthy or you have some institution behind you. The quality of policing is something we should constantly be talking about, but it’s hard to do when the information isn’t available.

**ML:** What struck you most about Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** I was thrilled to see all the energy and activism and passion. There is a very rich community of people who are trying to make a difference. And as the Black Lives Matter movement has gained steam, I think they’ve done a wonderful job of trying to seize the moment. I hope to see more of that.

“Criminal justice reform didn’t used to be a subject that many people cared about or wanted to talk about,” said Andersson, who noted that the project was already underway before the Ferguson unrest.

In an interview with Minnesota Lawyer, Andersson — a 2008 graduate of Yale Law School who had never set foot in Minneapolis prior to the assignment — talked about the report’s findings, the surprisingly positive reception from some local officials and why the ACLU decided to target Minneapolis in the first place.

This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

**Minnesota Lawyer:** So why did the ACLU want to focus on Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** In 2013, we published a nationwide look at marijuana arrests with a state by state ranking, “Marijuana in Black and White.” Of the 50 states and D.C., Minnesota was the third-worst in terms of disparities. We wanted to see whether this phenomenon was just with marijuana possession, or whether these disparities would exist with other types of low level offenses. Being that Minneapolis is the largest city in the state and hearing lots of complaints about problems with the Minneapolis Police Department in particular, we decided to look there.

**ML:** The ACLU has also examined race and policing practices in other cities, including Detroit, Philadelphia, Chicago and New York. Where does Minneapolis stack up?

**Andersson:** At this point, we can’t do much ranking. The only nationwide comparison we have is the marijuana arrest report, where the disparities in Minnesota, and in Minneapolis, were bad. But that’s just one offense.

Each department keeps data in a different way and most don’t publish data on any regular basis, which is emblematic of the larger problems with police data. We don’t even know how many people are killed by the police around the country.

The New York report looked at stop-and-frisk. Minneapolis doesn’t yet keep track on a systematic basis of its equivalent of stop-and-frisk, which is officer initiated suspicious person stops. Some of the other studies didn’t look at the same range of offenses we looked at here.

**ML:** One of the more intriguing parts of the report looked at eight officers who made a disproportionate number of low-level arrests, including one officer who made more than 2,000 of them over the study period. The median number of such arrests for all officers was around 50. Why didn’t you identify that officer?

**Andersson:** Our data includes demographic information of the arrestee, time of day, location, and offense. But it does not include all 96,000 arrest reports. We’d need to look at the arrest reports before making larger conclusions about those individual officers. But this really isn’t about just those eight officers. It’s about systems change. When we took out the arrests from those eight officers and recalculated the disparities, they were still about the same, still very bad.

**ML:** Did you ask the police leadership for any explanations about the officer with 2,000 plus arrests?

**Andersson:** They know that data point is there. I don’t think it’s our job to talk about the discipline or retraining of one officer. We’re trying to spend our time talking about system reform.

**ML:** Over the study period, you also documented a sizeable number of low-level arrests that were classified “doesn’t fit any crim.” [Sic] What’s that?

**Andersson:** I was told that’s what the computer spits out when an officer makes an arrest and enters a description that the computer doesn’t understand. I have no way of checking that. It doesn’t particularly make me feel better. Part of an officer’s job is to know what the laws are, what they’re allowed to enforce. If there’s a problem with the description such that it can’t be recognized by the computer, maybe there’s also a problem with that officer’s understanding of the law.

**ML:** And there were quite a lot of those “doesn’t fit any crime” cases, right?

**Andersson:** Nine hundred and seven. It’s more concerning when you couple that with the fact that 72 percent of the arrestees were black.

**ML:** Does the MPD incentive its officers to make low level arrests?

**Andersson:** It’s hard to know. For instance, each precinct has its own goals and recommendations for the officers who work in that precinct and then they get layered on top of the overall police department evaluation process. Those goals and recommendations have not been made available to us.

It’s not just a question of do the evaluation metrics incentive low level arrests? It’s also, do they incentive other, better things?  Do they provide measures for officers to show that they’re doing positive things in the community without racking up arrest numbers?

**ML:** Did you have trouble getting other information from the department?

**Andersson:** The department has a disciplinary matrix — sort of like sentencing guidelines — where you have certain alleged misconduct with various mitigating and aggravating factors, and it gives you a presumptive range of what the discipline should be. We’d like to know how these decisions are made. The process should be publically available.

**ML:** Did they say that’s not public?

**Andersson:** They just that it hasn’t been made public and they’re looking into it.

**ML:** What have you made of the response to the report from Police Chief Janee´ Harteau?

**Andersson:** After we released a much smaller data set in October — different data, pulled from uniform crime reports — the chief said, ‘I’ve seen this ACLU report and I’m troubled by the numbers.’  That’s not the response we usually get when we tell a police department they have a racial disparity problem. More often, we wind up getting into arguments about what the data shows.

This data came directly from the Minneapolis Police Department, so it’s hard to argue with. In the last day or two, the chief has said our data is somewhat misleading because we’re not accounting for the number of repeat offenders and that there are a lot of people who are repeat offenders.

**ML:** Do you think that’s a legitimate criticism?

**Andersson:** Not at all. If we see the same people arrested again and again for these non-felony offenses, I think that shores up our point. The criminal justice system isn’t the right way to deal with this kind of behavior because arresting people over and over again doesn’t appear to work and it doesn’t appear to be a good use of police time.

**ML:** But does it skew the analysis?

**Andersson:** No. We wanted to know that, so we took out those eight officers and recalculated the disparities. The numbers were around the same and still very bad.

**ML:** Have you looked at how all these low-level arrests wind up getting adjudicated?

**Andersson:** I would love to do that, but I don’t know that we have the resources. I think we’ll try to do a sampling.

**ML:** When it’s all said and done, do you think Minneapolis is an outlier?

**Andersson:** The scary and sad thing is that we find these dynamics whenever we look at a police department. It happens in unique ways in each city. But everywhere we look we find some kind of disparity in the policing and the criminal justice overall.

**ML:** You found that black drivers in Minneapolis were pulled over at a higher rate during daylight hours than at night, when they are less visible and therefore less easily identified by race. Can you see any explanations for that aside from profiling?

**Andersson:** That’s the one that jumps out most clearly to us. But we don’t have the arrest reports, so there are certainly details we’re missing. I’d be interested in hearing from the police what they think might account for that. I’m not sure what else it could be.

**Minnesota Lawyer:** So why did the ACLU want to focus on Minneapolis?

More often, we wind up getting into arguments about what the data shows.

This data came directly from the Minneapolis Police Department, so it’s hard to argue with. In the last day or two, the chief has said our data is somewhat misleading because we’re not accounting for the number of repeat offenders and that there are a lot of people who are repeat offenders.

**ML:** Do you think that’s a legitimate criticism?

**Andersson:** Not at all. If we see the same people arrested again and again for these non-felony offenses, I think that shores up our point. The criminal justice system isn’t the right way to deal with this kind of behavior because arresting people over and over again doesn’t appear to work and it doesn’t appear to be a good use of police time.

**ML:** But does it skew the analysis?

**Andersson:** No. We wanted to know that, so we took out those eight officers and recalculated the disparities. The numbers were around the same and still very bad.

**ML:** Have you looked at how all these low-level arrests wind up getting adjudicated?

**Andersson:** I would love to do that, but I don’t know that we have the resources. I think we’ll try to do a sampling.

**ML:** When it’s all said and done, do you think Minneapolis is an outlier?

**Andersson:** The scary and sad thing is that we find these dynamics whenever we look at a police department. It happens in unique ways in each city. But everywhere we look we find some kind of disparity in the policing and the criminal justice overall.

**ML:** You found that black drivers in Minneapolis were pulled over at a higher rate during daylight hours than at night, when they are less visible and therefore less easily identified by race. Can you see any explanations for that aside from profiling?

**Andersson:** That’s the one that jumps out most clearly to us. But we don’t have the arrest reports, so there are certainly details we’re missing. I’d be interested in hearing from the police what they think might account for that. I’m not sure what else it could be.

**ML:** Whom did you talk to on your trips to Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** We had several meetings with the police chief and her executive team over the last year or so. We met with the mayor’s chief of staff and some other folks to give them a sense of what we’re doing as part of our research and our due diligence. And we talked to a lot of folks on the ground to get the human side of that story. We didn’t want to just rely on numbers. We wanted to have a better sense of folks’ experience, both currently and historically.

**ML:** Was the MPD cooperative?

**Andersson:** We didn’t have to go to court and sue to get this data. They complied with our public records request, which doesn’t always happen, but we also had to pay about a thousand dollars for that data.

**ML:** Is that typical?

**Andersson:** The public records laws vary by state. When we do these large data requests, the one thing that’s consistent — it always costs a pretty penny. It’s not really accessible unless you’re wealthy or you have some institution behind you. The quality of policing is something we should constantly be talking about, but it’s hard to do when the information isn’t available.

**Andersson:** I was thrilled to see all the energy and activism and passion. There is a very rich community of people who are trying to make a difference. And as the Black Lives Matter movement has gained steam, I think they’ve done a wonderful job of trying to seize the moment. I hope to see more of that.

**ML:** Over the study period, you also documented a sizeable number of low-level arrests that were classified “doesn’t fit any crim.” [Sic] What’s that?

**Andersson:** I was told that’s what the computer spits out when an officer makes an arrest and enters a description that the computer doesn’t understand. I have no way of checking that. If there’s a problem with the description such that it can’t be recognized by the computer, maybe there’s also a problem with that officer’s understanding of the law.

**ML:** And there were quite a lot of those “doesn’t fit any crime” cases, right?

**Andersson:** Nine hundred and seven. It’s more concerning when you couple that with the fact that 72 percent of the arrestees were black.

**Andersson:** It’s hard to know. For instance, each precinct has its own goals and recommendations for the officers who work in that precinct and then they get layered on top of the overall police department evaluation process. Those goals and recommendations have not been made available to us.

It’s not just a question of do the evaluation metrics incentive low level arrests? It’s also, do they incentive other, better things?  Do they provide measures for officers to show that they’re doing positive things in the community without racking up arrest numbers?

**Andersson:** That’s the one that jumps out most clearly to us. But we don’t have the arrest reports, so there are certainly details we’re missing. I’d be interested in hearing from the police what they think might account for that. I’m not sure what else it could be.

**Andersson:** The public records laws vary by state. When we do these large data requests, the one thing that’s consistent — it always costs a pretty penny. **ML:** Whom did you talk to on your trips to Minneapolis?

**Andersson:** We had several meetings with the police chief and her executive team over the last year or so. We wanted to have a better sense of folks’ experience, both currently and historically.
