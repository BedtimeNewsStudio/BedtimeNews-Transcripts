**SINGAPORE --** **A walkover combined with a landslide to keep the long-ruling People's Action Party in power as Singapore settled on a status quo result in Saturday's general election.**

Facing Singapore's worst economic crisis in decades, 75.29 percent of voters in the Southeast Asian city-state gave their support to the party that has monopolized parliament since independence in 1965.

A crushing PAP victory was never in doubt as the fragmented opposition contested just 29 of 84 seats.

The result was the best showing by the PAP since it won 77.7 percent of the vote in 1980.

"The people have given us a very strong mandate," Prime Minister Goh Chok Tong told a news conference. "The next two years will be very tough years."

Goh, who plans to step down during the next five-year parliament, said the government would form an "economic committee to look into the medium-term restructuring of the economy."

His recession-battling program could include painful measures such as wage cuts.

Sixteen people were arrested in a demonstration on Friday but at the polls the tiny, fragmented opposition retained only the two seats they had previously held in the 93-member parliament. Nine seats were filled by appointment.

The Workers' Party and the Singapore Democratic Alliance (SDA) won one seat each. Under a "best loser" provision, Steve Chia of the four-party SDA is likely to get a seat.

"The whole machinery of the government is against us and we have won. I think that is a great victory," SDA chairman Chiam See Tong said after squeaking to re-election by just 751 votes.

Less than a third of the 2 million registered voters had to vote Saturday because most Singaporeans lived in uncontested constituencies. Voting is compulsory.

London-based human rights watchdog Amnesty International said in a statement Saturday that the "misuse of defamation suits by PAP leaders has contributed to a climate of self-censorship," the Associated Press news agency reported.

Days before Saturday's poll, opposition Singapore Democratic Party leader Chee Soon Juan publicly apologized to Goh and founding prime minister Lee Kuan Yew and agreed to pay them damages over campaign remarks.

PAP leaders say they have a right to use lawsuits to defend their reputations.

Even staunch critics call the PAP one of the world's cleanest governments, and credit the party with making Singapore one of Asia's wealthiest and most stable countries.

Lee, the ruling party's veteran leader, has argued that freewheeling Western-style democracy and too many individual freedoms could jeopardize Singapore's stability. Goh takes a similar approach.
