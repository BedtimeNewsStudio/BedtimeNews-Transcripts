### Who were the Five Good Emperors of the Roman Empire?

The Five Good Emperors of Rome were [Marcus Cocceius Nerva](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nerva-Roman-emperor) (reigned 96–98), [Trajan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Trajan) (98–117), [Hadrian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hadrian) (117–138), [Antoninus Pius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Antoninus-Pius) (138–161), and [Marcus Aurelius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Aurelius-Roman-emperor) (161–180). Their non-hereditary succession oversaw a golden age, which witnessed a considerable amount of expansion and consolidation.

### When did the Roman Empire fall?

The fall of the Western Roman Empire began in 410, when [Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rome) was sacked by the [Visigoths](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Visigoth), a wandering nation of [Germanic peoples](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Germanic-peoples) from the northeast. The fall was completed in 476, when the German chieftain [Odoacer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer) [deposed](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/deposed) the last Roman [emperor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/emperor-title) of the West, [Romulus Augustulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Romulus-Augustulus). The Eastern Roman Empire, always richer and stronger, continued as the [Byzantine Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Byzantine-Empire) until 1453.

### Who was the last Roman emperor?

The last emperor of the Western Roman Empire was [Romulus Augustulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Romulus-Augustulus). He was the son of the Western [empire’s](https://www.britannica.com/topic/empire-political-science) master of soldiers [Orestes](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Orestes-Roman-general). His original surname was [Augustus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Augustus-Roman-emperor), but it was changed to the diminutive because he was just 14 years old when his father, after driving the Western emperor [Julius Nepos](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Julius-Nepos) from [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy), elevated him to the throne on October 31, 475. For about 12 months Orestes ruled Italy in his son’s name, but eventually his troops mutinied and found a leader in the German warrior [Odoacer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer). Odoacer’s forces captured and executed Orestes on August 28, 476. Romulus, however, was spared because of his youth; Odoacer gave him a pension and sent him to live with his relatives in [Campania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Campania-region-Italy), a region of southern Italy. His subsequent fate is unknown.

[Domitian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Domitian) was succeeded by an elderly senator of some distinction, [Marcus Cocceius Nerva](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nerva-Roman-emperor) (96–98). Among the beloved rulers of Rome that succeeded him were [Trajan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Trajan) (reigned 98–117), [Hadrian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hadrian) (117–138), [Antoninus Pius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Antoninus-Pius) (138–161), and [Marcus Aurelius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Aurelius-Roman-emperor) (161–180). Together these are known as the [Five Good Emperors](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Five-Good-Emperors). Their non-hereditary succession oversaw a golden age, which witnessed a considerable amount of expansion and consolidation. But all the changes that occurred during this era, [beneficial](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/beneficial) as they were, brought with them the attendant evils of excessive centralization. The concentration of an empire in the hands of an emperor like [Commodus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Commodus) (180–192)—juvenile, incompetent, and decadent—was enough to steer it toward decline.

The following century was plagued by strife and mismanagement. When the commander of the [Danube](https://www.britannica.com/place/Danube-River) army, [Septimius Severus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Septimius-Severus), was swept to power in 193, he effectively made Rome a military monarchy. The “ [barbarian invasions](https://www.britannica.com/topic/barbarian-invasions)” weighed heavily on the empire, as did usurpations and political destabilization. The instability fed on itself and was responsible for heavy expenditure of both life and treasure. Disruptions in commerce, harsh taxation, inflation, and extortion from stationed troops all contributed to perpetual economic hardship for decades.

A period of recovery began with [Diocletian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Diocletian) (284–305), whose broad reforms renewed the [integrity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrity) and cohesion of the imperial administration. His most notable adjustment was the reorganization of the empire into a tetrarchy, wherein power was divided among himself, [Maximian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Maximian) (who became Augustus, or emperor, in 286), [Constantius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Constantius-I) (who became Caesar, or hereditary prince, in 293), and [Galerius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Galerius) (who also became Caesar in 293). The arrangement proved practical in stabilizing the empire for a time against usurpation, and it also promised the rulers legitimacy and regular succession.

The tetrarchy soon led to confusion, however, and by 308 there were seven pretenders to the title of Augustus. Among them was Constantius’s eldest son, [Constantine](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Constantine-I-Roman-emperor), who was passed over for formal [succession](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/succession). As a high-ranking military [tribune](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tribune-Roman-official), however, he had a forceful command and was able to eliminate his rivals successively in the West. He became the uncontested emperor of the West in 312 and, upon the defeat of his co-emperor in the East, he became the sole Augustus of the empire in 324.

Constantine’s reign (312–337) saw significant and lasting changes to the Roman Empire. [Christians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Christianity), who had been tolerated at best—but often tortured or killed—found new favor after the [Edict of Milan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Edict-of-Milan) (313) assured toleration for all religions. From about 320 the Roman state no longer [persecuted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/persecuted) Christians but rather showered Christian institutions with patronage. In 324 Constantine relocated the imperial capital to Byzantium (which was renamed Constantinople), a move whose strategic and economic benefits helped reinvigorate the state for some time. But Constantine failed to save the empire from decline. The last of his line, [Theodosius I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Theodosius-I) (379–395), was the last emperor to rule over a unified Roman Empire. The Western Empire, suffering from repeated invasions and the flight of the peasants into the cities, had grown weak compared with the East, where spices and other exports virtually guaranteed wealth and stability. When Theodosius died, in 395, Rome split into Eastern and Western empires.

The West was severely shaken in 410, when the city of Rome was sacked by the [Visigoths](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Visigoth), a wandering nation of Germanic peoples from the northeast. The fall of Rome was completed in 476, when the German chieftain [Odoacer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odoacer) deposed the last Roman emperor of the West, [Romulus Augustulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Romulus-Augustulus). The East, always richer and stronger, continued as the [Byzantine Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Byzantine-Empire) through the European [Middle Ages](https://www.britannica.com/event/Middle-Ages).

## Legacy of Rome

During the later republic and most of the empire, Rome was the dominant power in the entire Mediterranean basin, most of western [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe), and large areas of northern Africa. The Romans possessed a powerful army and were gifted in the applied arts of law, government, [city planning](https://www.britannica.com/topic/urban-planning), and statecraft, but they also acknowledged and adopted contributions of other ancient peoples—most notably, those of the [Greeks](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Greece), much of whose [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture) was thereby preserved.

The Roman Empire was distinguished not only for its outstanding army—the foundation upon which the whole empire rested—but also for its accomplishments in [intellectual](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intellectual) endeavors. [Roman law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-law), for example, was a considered and complex body of precedents and comments, which were all finally codified in the 6th century ( _see_ [Justinian, Code of](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Code-of-Justinian)). [Rome’s roads](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Roman-road-system) were without match in the ancient world, designed for comparatively fast transportation and adapted to a wide variety of functions: commerce, agriculture, mail delivery, pedestrian traffic, and military movements. Roman city planners achieved unprecedented standards of hygiene with their plumbing, sewage disposal, dams, and aqueducts. Roman architecture, though often imitative of Greek styles, was boldly planned and lavishly executed. Triumphal arches [commemorated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/commemorated) important state occasions, and the famous Roman baths were built to stir the senses as well as to cleanse the body.

Finally, [Latin](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Latin-language), the language of the Romans, became the medium for a significant body of original works in Western civilization. [Cicero](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cicero)’s speeches, the histories of [Livy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Livy) and [Tacitus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tacitus-Roman-historian), [Terence](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Terence)’s drama, and above all the poetry of [Virgil](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Virgil) are all part of the [legacy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legacy) of Rome.

- What was Carthage known for in the ancient world?

Carthage, an ancient city in present-day Tunisia, was founded by the Phoenicians in 814 BCE. Its location was strategic for controlling Mediterranean Sea trade between Sicily and North Africa.

Carthage was known for being a major maritime power and a thriving port and trade center. By the 5th century BCE, it had become the leader of the western Phoenicians, forming its own empire centered on North Africa.

The city maintained a strong grip on the coast, establishing new settlements to protect its trade monopoly. It grew to be possibly the wealthiest city in the world through this trade, dealing in textiles, metals, food, and slaves. Carthage was also known for its military strength and its religious beliefs.

Ultimately, Carthage was destroyed by Rome in 146 BCE during the Punic Wars, a series of conflicts that resulted in Roman control over the western Mediterranean.

- Why did Carthage and Rome become rivals?

Carthage and Rome's rivalry stemmed from their competing interests and expanding spheres of influence in the Mediterranean. By the 3rd century BCE, Rome's growing dominance in southern Italy brought it into contact with Carthage's sphere in Sicily. Both powers sought to control the strategic straits of Messana, leading to the First Punic War.

Carthage's resurgence in the 2nd century BCE, fueled by its commercial success, stirred unease among Rome's mercantile population. Influential Roman statesmen, like Cato the Censor, advocated for Carthage's destruction, leading to the Third Punic War and the city's obliteration in 146 BCE.

- What happened to Carthage after its wars with Rome?

After the Punic Wars, Carthage was completely destroyed by Rome in 146 BCE during the Third Punic War. The survivors were enslaved, and the territory became a Roman province named Africa.

A century later, Julius Caesar and Emperor Augustus rebuilt Carthage, calling it Colonia Julia Carthago, which grew to be a major city in the Western Roman Empire. It became a center of Roman administration and prospered for centuries, exporting goods and integrating into Roman society.

However, by the end of the 4th century CE, city life declined, and the Vandals captured Carthage in 439 CE, making it their capital. Although the Byzantines later defeated the Vandals, the city continued to decline, and the Roman province of Africa offered little resistance to Arab invaders, who took Carthage in 697 CE.

**Carthage**, great city of antiquity on the north coast of Africa, now a residential suburb of the city of [Tunis](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tunis), [Tunisia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tunisia). Built on a promontory on the Tunisian coast, it was placed to influence and control ships passing between [Sicily](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sicily) and the North African coast as they [traversed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/traversed) the [Mediterranean Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mediterranean-Sea). Rapidly becoming a thriving port and trading centre, it eventually developed into a major Mediterranean power and a rival to Rome. The archaeological site of Carthage was added to [UNESCO](https://www.britannica.com/topic/UNESCO)’s [World Heritage List](https://www.britannica.com/topic/World-Heritage-site) in 1979.

According to tradition, Carthage was founded by the [Phoenicians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Phoenician) of [Tyre](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tyre) in 814 bce; its Phoenician name means “new town.”

Carthage was probably not the earliest Phoenician settlement in the region; [Utica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Utica-Tunisia) may have predated it by half a century, and various traditions concerning the foundation of [Carthage](https://www.britannica.com/topic/World-Heritage-site) were current among the Greeks, who called the city Karchedon. The Roman tradition is better known, however, because of the [_Aeneid_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aeneid), which tells of the city’s foundation by the Tyrian princess [Dido](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Dido-Queen-of-Carthage), who fled from her brother Pygmalion (the name of a historical king of Tyre). The inhabitants of Carthage were known to the Romans as Poeni, a [derivation](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/derivation) from the word Phoenikes (Phoenicians), from which the adjective Punic is derived. The traditional date of the foundation of Carthage as 814 bce was probably exaggerated by the Carthaginians themselves, for it does not necessarily agree with the archaeological data. Nothing earlier than the last quarter of the 8th century bce has been discovered, a full century later than the traditional foundation date.

The Phoenicians selected the locations of their maritime colonies with great care, focusing on the quality of harbours and their proximity to trade routes. The site chosen for Carthage in the centre of the shore of the Gulf of Tunis was ideal; the city was built on a triangular peninsula covered with low hills and backed by the Lake of Tunis, with its safe anchorage and [abundant](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/abundant) supplies of fish. This location offered access to the Mediterranean but was shielded from many of the violent storms that afflicted other Mediterranean ports. The site of the city was well protected and easily defensible, and its proximity to the Strait of Sicily placed it at a strategic bottleneck in east-west Mediterranean trade. On the south the peninsula is connected to the mainland by a narrow strip of land. The ancient citadel, the Byrsa, was on a low hill overlooking the sea. Some of the earliest tombs have been found there, though nothing remains of Carthage’s domestic and public buildings.

Although Punic wealth was legendary, the standard of cultural life enjoyed by the Carthaginians may have been below that of the larger cities of the Classical world. Punic interests were turned toward [commerce](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/commerce) rather than art, and Carthage controlled much of the Western trade in the luxurious purple dye from the [murex](https://www.britannica.com/animal/murex-mollusk-family) shell. Arguments about the virtual lack of Punic literature are largely moot; when the Romans sacked the city, Carthage’s libraries and archives were either given to Numidian kings or did not survive the destruction. One notable exception was the work of a Carthaginian writer named [Mago](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mago-Carthaginian-general), whose 28 books on agriculture were translated into Greek by [Cassius Dionysius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cassius-Dionysius) and later cited by Romans such as [Lucius Junius Moderatus Columella](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lucius-Junius-Moderatus-Columella). In Roman times Punic beds, cushions, and mattresses were regarded as luxuries, and Punic joinery and furniture were copied. Much of the [revenue](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/revenue) of Carthage came from its exploitation of the [silver](https://www.britannica.com/science/silver) mines of [North Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Africa) and southern [Spain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain), begun as early as 800 bce near Gadir (modern [Cádiz](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cadiz-Spain), Spain) and in the 3rd century bce near what is now [Cartagena](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cartagena-Spain), Spain. From the middle of the 3rd century to the middle of the 2nd century bce, Carthage was engaged in a series of wars with Rome. These wars, which are known as the [Punic Wars](https://www.britannica.com/event/Punic-Wars), ended in the complete defeat of Carthage by Rome and the expansion of Roman control in the Mediterranean world. When Carthage finally fell in 146 bce, the site was plundered and burned, fulfilling the demand by the senator and orator [Cato the Elder](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Porcius-Cato-Roman-senator-95-46-BCE) that had been [distilled](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/distilled) in the phrase _delenda est Carthago_: “Carthage must be destroyed.” _See also_ [North Africa: The Carthaginian period](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Africa/Ancient-North-Africa#ref46460).

In 122 bce the Roman [Senate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Senate-Roman-history) entrusted [Gaius Gracchus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaius-Sempronius-Gracchus) and Marcus Fulvius Flaccus with the foundation of a colony on the site of Carthage. Though the venture was largely unsuccessful, [Julius Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Julius-Caesar-Roman-ruler) later sent a number of landless citizens there, and in 29 bce [Augustus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Augustus-Roman-emperor) centred the administration of the Roman province of [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa-Roman-territory) at the site. Thereafter it became known as Colonia Julia Carthago, and it soon grew prosperous enough to be ranked with [Alexandria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Alexandria-Egypt) and [Antioch](https://www.britannica.com/place/Antioch-modern-and-ancient-city-south-central-Turkey). Carthage became a favourite city of the emperors, though none resided there. Of its history during the later [empire](https://www.britannica.com/topic/empire-political-science), very little is known, but in the mid-3rd century, the city began to decline.

From the end of the 2nd century, it had its own Christian bishop, and among its luminaries were the [Church Fathers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Church-Father) [Tertullian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tertullian) and [St. Cyprian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Saint-Cyprian-Christian-bishop). Throughout the 4th and 5th centuries, Carthage was troubled by the [Donatist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Donatists) and [Pelagian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pelagianism) controversies.

In 439 ce the [Vandal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Vandal-Germanic-people) ruler [Gaiseric](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaiseric) entered almost unopposed and plundered the city. [Gelimer](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gelimer), the last Vandal king, was defeated at nearby Decimum by a [Byzantine](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Byzantine) army under [Belisarius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Belisarius), who entered Carthage unopposed (533 ce). After its capture by the Arabs in 705, Carthage was totally eclipsed by the new town of Tunis.

Though Roman Carthage was destroyed, much of its remains can be traced, including the outline of many fortifications and an [aqueduct](https://www.britannica.com/technology/aqueduct-engineering). The former Byrsa area was adorned with a large [temple](https://www.britannica.com/topic/temple-building) dedicated to [Juno](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Juno-Roman-goddess), [Jupiter](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jupiter-Roman-god), and [Minerva](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Minerva-Roman-goddess), and near it stood a temple to [Asclepius](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Asclepius). Also on the Byrsa site stood an open-air portico, from which the finest Roman sculptures at Carthage have survived. Additional remains of the Roman town include an odeum, another theatre constructed by [Hadrian](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hadrian), an amphitheatre modeled on the Roman [Colosseum](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Colosseum), numerous baths and temples, and a circus.

The Christian buildings within the city, with the exception of a few Vandal structures, are all Byzantine. The largest basilica was rebuilt in the 6th century on the site of an earlier one. Churches probably existed during the 3rd and 4th centuries, but no traces remain. Part of the mid-3rd-century Carthaginian town has been excavated on Byrsa Hill. Once occupied by the temple of a Carthaginian god and then by the Roman [forum](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/forum), it is now the site of a late 19th-century French cathedral dedicated to [Louis IX](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Louis-IX), the Crusading French king who died in Tunis in 1270.
