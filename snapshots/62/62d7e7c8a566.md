Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development

The **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development** ( **OECD**; [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"): _Organisation de coopération et de développement économiques_, **OCDE**) is an [intergovernmental organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_organization "International organization") with 38 member countries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-membership-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-membership)[&#8202;](#ref-back-38_countries-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-38_countries) It was founded in 1961 to stimulate economic progress and [world trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade "International trade"). The OECD is a [forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_(legal) "Forum (legal)") whose member countries describe themselves as committed to democracy and the [market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy "Market economy"), providing a platform for collective problem-solving, analysis, and coordination.

In 2026, their collective population was 1.38billion people with high [levels of human development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index "Human Development Index").[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5)[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-6)As of 2026[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=OECD&action=edit), OECD member countries collectively comprised 58.4% of [global nominal GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal) "List of countries by GDP (nominal)") and 38.9% of global GDP at [purchasing power parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP) "List of countries by GDP (PPP)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-GDP_IMF-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-GDP_IMF) The OECD is an official [United Nations observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly_observers "United Nations General Assembly observers").[&#8202;](#ref-back-8-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-8) Member states have strong [social security systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_spending "Welfare spending").[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-9) The OECD is headquartered at the [Château de la Muette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_la_Muette "Château de la Muette") in Paris, France.[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10) The OECD is funded by contributions from member countries and publishes annual reports on economic data as well as a variety of evaluations and rankings.[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)

## History

### Organisation for European Economic Co-operation

The OECD is the successor organisation to the **Organisation for European Economic Co-operation** ( **OEEC**),[&#8202;](#ref-back-oeec-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-oeec) [established in April 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_European_Economic_Co-operation "Committee of European Economic Co-operation") among the European recipients of [Marshall Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Plan "Marshall Plan") aid for the reconstruction of Europe after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13)[&#8202;](#ref-back-warren-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-warren)[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-15) Only Western European states were members of the OEEC, whose primary function was the allocation of American aid. Its Secretaries-General[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-16) were the Frenchmen [Robert Marjolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Marjolin "Robert Marjolin") (1948–1955) and René Sergent (1955–1960). It was headquartered in the [Château de la Muette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_la_Muette "Château de la Muette") in Paris.

Following the end of Marshall aid in 1952, the OEEC focused on economic issues.[&#8202;](#ref-back-oeec-1)[\[12\]](#refnote-oeec) Its coordinating role was challenged after the 1957 [Rome Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Rome "Treaty of Rome") establishing the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community "European Economic Community") and [Euratom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euratom_Treaty "Euratom Treaty"). The OEEC provided a framework for negotiations aimed at setting up a [European Free Trade Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Free_Trade_Area "European Free Trade Area"), to bring the EEC's [Inner Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Six "Inner Six") and other OEEC members together on a multilateral basis. In 1958, a [European Nuclear Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Nuclear_Energy_Agency "European Nuclear Energy Agency") was set up in response to Euratom.

By this time, some leading countries felt that the OEEC had outlived its purpose but could be adapted to fulfil a more global mission, which proved to be a cumbersome task. Following several (occasionally unruly) meetings at the [Hotel Majestic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Majestic_(Paris) "Hotel Majestic (Paris)") in Paris, which began in January 1960, a resolution was reached to create a body that would not only solve European and Atlantic economic issues, but also devise policies that could assist less developed countries. This reconstituted organisation would bring the US and Canada, who were already OEEC observers, on board as full members, and the OEEC would set to work straight away on convincing Japan to join the organisation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17)[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18)[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19)

### Founding

The Convention on the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development was signed on 14 December 1960, and the OECD officially superseded the OEEC in September 1961, consisting of the European founder countries of the OEEC, with the additions of the United States and Canada. Three countries, (the Netherlands, Luxembourg, and Italy)—all OEEC members—ratified the OECD Convention after September 1961, but are nevertheless considered founding members. The official founding members were Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, France, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, the United Kingdom, the United States, and [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany "West Germany").

Japan became a member in 1964, and over the following decade, [Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland "Finland"), Australia, and New Zealand also joined the organisation. [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SFR_Yugoslavia "SFR Yugoslavia") had observer status in the organisation, starting with the establishment of the OECD, until its [dissolution as a country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakup_of_Yugoslavia "Breakup of Yugoslavia").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Marković_&_Obadić-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-Marković_&_Obadić) The OECD also created agencies such as the [OECD Development Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Development_Centre "OECD Development Centre") (1961), [International Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency") (IEA, 1974), and [Financial Action Task Force on Money Laundering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Action_Task_Force_on_Money_Laundering "Financial Action Task Force on Money Laundering") (FATF).

The aims of the OECD are stated in Article 1 of the Convention[&#8202;](#ref-back-document/oecd-convention-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-document/oecd-convention) as:

1. To achieve the highest sustainable economic growth and employment and a rising standard of living in Member countries, while maintaining financial stability
2. To contribute to sound economic expansion in Member as well as non-member countries
3. To contribute to the expansion of world trade

### Enlargement to Central Europe

Following the [Revolutions of 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989 "Revolutions of 1989"), the OECD began assisting countries in Central Europe (especially the [Visegrád Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d_Group "Visegrád Group")) to prepare market economy reforms. In 1990, the Centre for Co-operation with European Economies in Transition (now succeeded by the Centre for Cooperation with Non-Members) was established, and in 1991, the programme "Partners in Transition" was launched to offer a partnership to [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia "Czechoslovakia"), [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary") and [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-czech-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-czech) including a membership option for these countries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-czech-1)[\[22\]](#refnote-czech) As a result of this, Poland,[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23) Hungary, the [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic "Czech Republic") and [Slovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovakia "Slovakia"), as well as [South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea "South Korea")[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-:1) and Mexico, became members of the OECD between 1996 and 2000.

### Reform and further enlargement

[East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany "East Germany") joined on 3 October 1990 through [reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification "German reunification") with West Germany. In the 1990s, several European countries, now members of the European Union, expressed their willingness to join the Organisation. In 1995, [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") applied for membership, but according to the Cypriot government, it was vetoed by [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Veto_of_Cypriot_application-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-Veto_of_Cypriot_application) In 1996, [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia "Estonia"), [Latvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvia "Latvia"), and [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania "Lithuania") signed a Joint Declaration expressing willingness to become members of the OECD,[&#8202;](#ref-back-26-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-26) and [Slovenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenia "Slovenia") also applied for membership that same year.[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-27) In 2005, [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta "Malta") applied to join the Organisation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28) The EU is lobbying for the admission of all [EU member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_member_states "EU member states").[&#8202;](#ref-back-29-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-29) [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania "Romania") reaffirmed in 2012 its intention to become a member of the Organisation through the letter addressed by Romanian Prime Minister [Victor Ponta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Ponta "Victor Ponta") to OECD Secretary-General [José Ángel Gurría](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_%C3%81ngel_Gurr%C3%ADa "José Ángel Gurría").[&#8202;](#ref-back-30-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-30) In September 2012, the government of [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria "Bulgaria") confirmed it would apply for membership before the OECD Secretariat.[&#8202;](#ref-back-31-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-31)

The OECD established a working group headed by ambassador Seiichiro Noboru to work out a plan for the enlargement with non-members. The working group defined four criteria that must be fulfilled: "like-mindedness", "significant player", "mutual benefit" and "global considerations." The working group's recommendations were presented at the OECD Ministerial Council Meeting on 13 May 2004, and on 16 May 2007, the OECD Ministerial Council decided to open accession discussions with Chile, [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia "Estonia"), Israel, Russia, and Slovenia, and to strengthen cooperation with Brazil, China, India, [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"), and South Africa through a process of enhanced engagement.[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated1-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-autogenerated1) Chile, Slovenia, Israel, and Estonia all became members in 2010.[&#8202;](#ref-back-33-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-33) In March 2014, the OECD halted membership talks with Russia in response to its [annexation of Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation "Annexation of Crimea by the Russian Federation").[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated6-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-autogenerated6)[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated7-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-autogenerated7)

In 2013, the OECD decided to open membership talks with [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombia "Colombia") and Latvia. In 2015, the Organisation opened talks with [Costa Rica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costa_Rica "Costa Rica") and [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania "Lithuania").[&#8202;](#ref-back-oecd300513-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-oecd300513) Latvia became a member on 1 July 2016, and Lithuania soon followed on 5 July 2018.[&#8202;](#ref-back-leta010716-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-leta010716)[&#8202;](#ref-back-vz050718-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-vz050718) Colombia signed the accession agreement on 30 May 2018 and became a member on 28 April 2020.[&#8202;](#ref-back-39-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-39) On 15 May 2020, the OECD decided to extend a formal invitation for Costa Rica to join the OECD,[&#8202;](#ref-back-oecd-costarica150520-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-oecd-costarica150520) which joined as a member on 25 May 2021.[&#8202;](#ref-back-CRI38-1)[\[2\]](#refnote-CRI38)

Other countries that have expressed interest in OECD membership are [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina "Argentina"), Brazil,[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-41) [Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia "Croatia"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-42-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-42) [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia")[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-43) and [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru").[&#8202;](#ref-back-44-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-44)

In January 2022, the OECD reported that membership talks were underway with Argentina, Brazil, Bulgaria, [Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia "Croatia"), [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru") and Romania.[&#8202;](#ref-back-45-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-45)

In March 2022, the OECD suspended the participation of Russia and [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus "Belarus") due to the ongoing [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "Russian invasion of Ukraine").[&#8202;](#ref-back-46-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-46)

In June 2022, during the annual OECD Ministerial Council Meeting, the Roadmaps for the Accession to the OECD Convention for Brazil, Bulgaria, Croatia, Peru and Romania were adopted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-47) In March 2024, the Roadmaps for the Accession to the OECD were adopted with Argentina[&#8202;](#ref-back-48-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-48) and [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-49-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-49) and in July 2024, also with [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand").[&#8202;](#ref-back-50-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-50)

## Objectives and issues

### Multilateral Surveillance

The OECD conducts multilateral surveillance as part of its economic monitoring and policy co-ordination activities among member countries. This function has developed through regular peer reviews, comparative analysis, and policy assessments carried out within the framework of the Organisation's committees and its Economics Department.

### Taxation

The OECD sets the rules governing [international taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_taxation "International taxation") for multinationals through the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and Tax Administrations, a Model Tax Convention and country-by-country reporting rules.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/49/Payroll_and_income_tax_by_country.png/500px-Payroll_and_income_tax_by_country.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Payroll_and_income_tax_by_country.png) [Payroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payroll_tax "Payroll tax") and [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax "Income tax") by OECD country

The OECD publishes and updates a model tax convention that serves as a template for allocating taxation rights between countries. This model is accompanied by a set of commentaries that reflect OECD-level interpretation of the content of the model convention provisions. In general, this model allocates the primary right to tax to the country from which capital investment originates (i.e., the home, or resident country) rather than the country in which the investment is made (the host, or source country). As a result, it is most effective between two countries with reciprocal investment flows (such as among the OECD member countries), but can be unbalanced when one of the signatory countries is economically weaker than the other (such as between OECD and non-OECD pairings). Additionally, the OECD has published and updated the Transfer Pricing Guidelines since 1995. The Transfer Pricing Guidelines serve as a template for the profit allocation of inter-company transactions to countries.

**Pillar 1**

An OECD proposal to allocate multinational profits (for taxing purposes) to countries where they do business, by a formula, including to markets which multinationals sell into without a physical presence. This is hoped to eliminate the need for [Digital Services Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_of_digital_goods "Taxation of digital goods") implemented by several countries, including France. There are exclusions and minimum thresholds, including banking and extractive industries. The proposal involves allocating only residual profit (i.e., profits above what is established through transfer pricing, thus creating a hybrid mechanism). This is essentially no change to what is currently allowed (routine profits allocated using transfer pricing + residual profits allocated through profit split).

**Pillar 2**

On 1 July 2021, finance officials from 130 countries agreed on plans for a new international taxation policy known as the [global minimum corporate tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_minimum_corporate_tax_rate "Global minimum corporate tax rate") (of 15%). If a country taxes a multinational at a lower rate, the multinational's HQ will receive the difference.

All the major economies agreed to pass national laws that would require corporations to pay at least 15% income tax in the countries they operate. This new policy would end the practice of locating world headquarters in small countries with very low taxation rates. Governments hope to recoup some of the lost revenue, estimated at $100billion to $240billion each year. The new system was promoted by the [Biden Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Joe_Biden "Presidency of Joe Biden") in the United States and the OECD. Secretary-General [Mathias Cormann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathias_Cormann "Mathias Cormann") of the OECD said, "This historic package will ensure that large multinational companies pay their fair share of tax everywhere."[&#8202;](#ref-back-51-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-51)

### Multinational corporations

The [OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Guidelines_for_Multinational_Enterprises "OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises") are a set of legally non-binding guidelines attached as an annexe to the OECD Declaration on International Investment and Multinational Enterprises. They are recommendations providing principles and standards for responsible business conduct for [multinational corporations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_corporations "Multinational corporations") operating in or from countries adhering to the Declaration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-52-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-52)

## Publications

The OECD publishes books, reports, statistics, working papers, and reference materials. All titles and databases published since 1998 can be accessed via OECD.org. The OECD Library & Archives collection dates from 1947, including records from the [Committee for European Economic Co-operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_European_Economic_Co-operation "Committee of European Economic Co-operation") (CEEC) and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC), predecessors of today's OECD. External researchers can consult OECD publications and archival material on the OECD premises by appointment.[&#8202;](#ref-back-oecd-archives-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-oecd-archives)

### Books

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/Reports_for_sale_at_the_OECD_bookshop_April_2019.jpg/500px-Reports_for_sale_at_the_OECD_bookshop_April_2019.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Reports_for_sale_at_the_OECD_bookshop_April_2019.jpg) Reports on a wide range of topics for sale at the OECD's Conference Centre Bookshop in 2019

The OECD releases about 600 books and over 400 papers yearly on topics spanning public policy. The publications are updated to the OECD.org. Most books are published in English and French. The OECD flagship titles include:

- The [OECD Economic Outlook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Economic_Outlook "OECD Economic Outlook"), published twice a year, with interim updates. It provides analysis and forecasts of major short-term global economic trends including for GDP growth, inflation and labour market developments across member and selected partner countries. The _OECD Economic Outlook_ helps policymakers assess near-term economic prospects, risks and policy options.[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-54)
- The [Main Economic Indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Main_Economic_Indicators "OECD Main Economic Indicators"), published monthly. It contains a large selection of timely statistical indicators.
- The [OECD Communications Outlook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Communications_Outlook?action=edit&redlink=1 "OECD Communications Outlook") and the OECD Internet Economy Outlook (formerly the Information Technology Outlook), which rotate every year. They contain forecasts and analysis of the communications and information technology industries in OECD member countries and non-member economies.
- In 2007 the OECD published _Human Capital: How what you know shapes your life_, the first book in the _OECD Insights_ series. This series uses OECD analysis and data to introduce important social and economic issues to non-specialist readers. Other books in the series cover sustainable development, international trade and international migration. The series was discontinued in 2017.[&#8202;](#ref-back-55-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-55)

All OECD books are available on www.OECD.org.[&#8202;](#ref-back-oecd-archives-1)[\[53\]](#refnote-oecd-archives)

### Magazine

_OECD Observer_, an award-winning magazine,[&#8202;](#ref-back-56-0)[\[n 1\]](#refnote-56) was launched in 1962.[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-57) The magazine appeared six times a year until 2010, and became quarterly in 2011 with the introduction of the _OECD Yearbook_, launched for the 50th anniversary of the organisation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-58-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-58) The online and mobile[&#8202;](#ref-back-59-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-59) editions contained news, analysis, reviews, commentaries and data on global economic, social and environmental challenges and listings of the latest OECD books.[&#8202;](#ref-back-60-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-60) An OECD Observer Crossword was introduced in Q2 2013.[&#8202;](#ref-back-61-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-61) The OECD Observer was last issued in the fourth quarter of 2019, with a double edition looking ahead at artificial intelligence, and a cover leading on why statistical offices should hire a comedian. The OECD Observer website closed in the first quarter of 2021; the archive can be consulted at www.oecd.org.

### Statistics

The OECD is known as a [statistical agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_and_international_statistical_services "List of national and international statistical services"), as it publishes comparable statistics on numerous subjects. In July 2014, the OECD publicly released its main [statistical databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_database "Statistical database") through the OECD Data Portal, an online platform that allows visitors to create custom charts based on official OECD indicators.[&#8202;](#ref-back-62-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-62)[&#8202;](#ref-back-63-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-63)

OECD statistics are available in several forms:

- as interactive charts on the OECD Data Portal,
- as interactive databases on iLibrary together with key comparative and country tables,
- as static files or dynamic database views on the OECD Statistics portal,
- as StatLinks (in most OECD books, there is a [URL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Resource_Locator "Uniform Resource Locator") that links to the underlying data).

In July 2024, the OECD announced that it "has transitioned to \[an\] open-access information model" and that Creative Commons [CC‑BY‑4.0 attribution licences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Commons_license "Creative Commons license") will be used on all data and publications.
[&#8202;](#ref-back-oecd-freely-available-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-oecd-freely-available)

### Papers

OECD directorates publish a wide range of working papers and policy papers covering various topics, including economics, education, development, statistics and more. Papers are accessible via the OECD working and policy papers search page.[&#8202;](#ref-back-65-0)[\[64\]](#refnote-65)

### Reference works

The OECD is responsible for the [OECD Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD_Guidelines_for_the_Testing_of_Chemicals "OECD Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals"), a continuously updated document that is a _de facto_ standard (i.e., soft law).[&#8202;](#ref-back-66-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-66)

It published the _OECD Environmental Outlook to 2030_ in March 2008, which argues that tackling key environmental problems—including [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change"), [biodiversity loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss "Biodiversity loss"), [water scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity "Water scarcity"), and the health impacts of pollution—is both achievable and affordable.[&#8202;](#ref-back-67-0)[\[66\]](#refnote-67)

## Structure

The OECD's structure consists of three main elements:[&#8202;](#ref-back-68-0)[\[67\]](#refnote-68)

- The OECD [member countries](#Member_countries), each represented by a delegation led by an ambassador. Together, they form the OECD Council. Member countries act collectively through the council (and its standing committees) to provide direction and guidance to the work of the organisation.
- The OECD substantive [committees](#Committees), one for each work area of the OECD, plus their various subsidiary bodies. Committee members are typically subject-matter experts from member and non-member governments. The committees oversee all the work on each theme (publications, task forces, conferences, and so on). Committee members then relay the conclusions to their capitals.
- The OECD [Secretariat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary-General_of_the_OECD "Secretary-General of the OECD"), led by the Secretary-General (currently [Mathias Cormann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathias_Cormann "Mathias Cormann")), provides support to standing and substantive committees. It is organised into directorates, which include about 2,500 staff.

### Meetings

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/dd/Entrance_to_the_OECD_Conference_Centre_April_2014.jpg/500px-Entrance_to_the_OECD_Conference_Centre_April_2014.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Entrance_to_the_OECD_Conference_Centre_April_2014.jpg) The main entrance to the OECD Conference Centre in Paris

Delegates from the member countries attend committee and other meetings. Former [Deputy Secretary-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_General "Secretary General") [Pierre Vinde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Vinde?action=edit&redlink=1 "Pierre Vinde")\[ [sv](https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre%20Vinde "sv:Pierre Vinde")\] estimated in 1997 that the cost borne by the member countries, such as sending their officials to OECD meetings and maintaining permanent delegations, is equivalent to the cost of running the secretariat.[&#8202;](#ref-back-69-0)[\[68\]](#refnote-69)

The OECD regularly holds minister-level meetings and forums as platforms for a discussion on a broad spectrum of thematic issues relevant to the OECD charter, member countries, and non-member countries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-OECDforums-0)[\[69\]](#refnote-OECDforums)

### Secretariat

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_la_Muette%2C_Paris_20_March_2019_004.jpg/500px-Ch%C3%A2teau_de_la_Muette%2C_Paris_20_March_2019_004.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ch%C3%A2teau_de_la_Muette,_Paris_20_March_2019_004.jpg) The exterior of the Château de la Muette and the grounds of the OECD Conference Centre

Exchanges between OECD governments benefit from the information, analysis, and preparation of the OECD Secretariat. The secretariat collects data, monitors trends, and analyses and forecasts economic developments. Under the direction and guidance of member governments, it also researches social changes or evolving patterns in trade, environment, education, agriculture, technology, taxation and other areas.

The secretariat is organised in directorates:[&#8202;](#ref-back-72-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-72)

- Development Co-operation Directorate
- Directorate for Education and Skills
- Directorate for Employment, Labour, and Social Affairs
- Directorate for Financial and Enterprise Affairs
- Directorate for Science, Technology, and Innovation
- Economics Department
- Environment Directorate
- Public Governance Directorate
- Statistics Directorate
- Trade and Agriculture Directorate
- General Secretariat
- Executive Directorate
- Directorate for Communications

### OECD Chief Economist

British economist [Christopher Dow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Dow "Christopher Dow") served as Chief Economist of the OECD from its establishment until 1973.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:2-0)[\[81\]](#refnote-:2)

In 1980, Canadian economist [Sylvia Ostry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvia_Ostry "Sylvia Ostry") became the first woman to be appointed OECD Chief Economist, holding the position until 1983.

In 1992, [Kumiharu Shigehara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumiharu_Shigehara "Kumiharu Shigehara"), former Chief Economist of the [Bank of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Japan "Bank of Japan"), became the first individual from outside the English-speaking world to be appointed OECD Chief Economist. At the time of his appointment, the _[Financial Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Times "Financial Times")_, in an article entitled "Japan’s Turn" (22 January 1992), described him as exemplifying a new generation of Japanese figures characterised by self-confidence, strong international awareness, and a willingness to express their views openly.

In February 2026, Italian Stefano Scarpetta was announced as the next Chief Economist.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:3-0)[\[82\]](#refnote-:3) Scarpetta used to lead the Directorate's work on employment, migration and health.

### Committees

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Chateau_de_la_muette%2C_grand_Salon.JPG/500px-Chateau_de_la_muette%2C_grand_Salon.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chateau_de_la_muette,_grand_Salon.JPG) A meeting room in the Château de la Muette

Representatives of member and observer countries meet in specialised committees on specific policy areas, such as economics, trade, science, employment, education, development assistance or financial markets. There are about 200 committees, working groups and expert groups. Committees discuss policies and review progress in the given policy area.[&#8202;](#ref-back-87-0)[\[86\]](#refnote-87)

## Voting

OECD decisions are made through voting, which requires unanimity among all of those voting. Each member country has one vote.[&#8202;](#ref-back-document/oecd-convention-1)[\[21\]](#refnote-document/oecd-convention) However, dissenting members which do not wish to block a decision but merely to signal their disapproval can abstain from voting.[&#8202;](#ref-back-CarrollKellow2011-0)[\[88\]](#refnote-CarrollKellow2011) 22 of the OECD member countries are also EU member states.[&#8202;](#ref-back-90-0)[\[89\]](#refnote-90)

### Accession talks terminated

- ![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/Flag_of_Belarus.svg/60px-Flag_of_Belarus.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)[Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus "Belarus")
- ![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f3/Flag_of_Russia.svg/60px-Flag_of_Russia.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)[Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia")

In May 2007, the OECD decided to open accession negotiations with Russia.[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated1-5)[\[32\]](#refnote-autogenerated1) In March 2014, the OECD halted membership talks in response to Russia's role in that year's [Crimean annexation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation "Annexation of Crimea by the Russian Federation") and continuous human and civil rights abuses.[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated6-1)[\[34\]](#refnote-autogenerated6)[&#8202;](#ref-back-autogenerated7-1)[\[35\]](#refnote-autogenerated7) On 25 February 2022, the OECD terminated the accession process with Russia after it [invaded Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine").[&#8202;](#ref-back-130-0)[\[129\]](#refnote-130) In March 2022, Belarus was suspended from any participation in the OECD.[&#8202;](#ref-back-131-0)[\[130\]](#refnote-131)

### Application vetoed

- ![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Flag_of_Cyprus.svg/60px-Flag_of_Cyprus.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)[Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus")

In 1995, Cyprus applied to join the OECD. The application for membership was vetoed by Turkey that year and continues to be vetoed ever since. Turkey vetoed Cyprus' participation in the OECD's survey of Teachers, Teaching and Learning in December 2005 and again in April 2006 in the OECD's Programme on Educational Building and the survey [Programme for International Student Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programme_for_International_Student_Assessment "Programme for International Student Assessment"), as well as in the participation of Cyprus (as an observer) in the OECD's Committee on Statistics. Given the fact that in order to become a member of the [International Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency"), Cyprus has to become a member of the OECD, its participation in the IEA is prevented by the Turkish veto.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Veto_of_Cypriot_application-1)[\[25\]](#refnote-Veto_of_Cypriot_application)

## Budget

The OECD operates on a two-year budget determined by member countries, with annual revenues over €900million during the most recent reporting period (2023–2024).[&#8202;](#ref-back-132-0)[\[131\]](#refnote-132)[&#8202;](#ref-back-fstate-1)[\[3\]](#refnote-fstate)

Totalling an estimated €229.9million in 2024, assessed contributions to the "Part I Budget" are the largest single source of revenue for the OECD and these contributions are based on both the number of OECD members and the proportional size of their national economies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-133-0)[\[132\]](#refnote-133)
