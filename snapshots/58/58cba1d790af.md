## Pioneers of Science

## About the project

In Pioneers of Science, the Max Planck Society is presenting the research of its Nobel laureates as part of a comprehensive digital story for the first time. A selection of topics and laureates from the digital story will also be presented in various German cities as part of the traveling Science Pavilion exhibition on the occasion of the 2023 anniversary year.  
  
 The Max Planck Society conducts basic research in the natural sciences, life sciences and humanities. With more than 85 institutes and facilities, it is the international flagship of science of German science. In 1948, the Max Planck Society took over the legacy of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society, which was founded in 1911 to modernize the German science system. It began promoting research in its own institutes outside of universities and pursued the structural principle of promotion of research centered on outstanding individual researchers as invented by Adolf von Harnack. Both traditions have been continued by the Max Planck Society since 1948, which was reorganized in the democratic system of the Federal Republic of Germany.  
  
 In its history, including the period of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society, the Max Planck Society has counted 27 Nobel Prizes for 30 Nobel Prize laureates (in two cases, Max Planck teams shared the award). This makes the Max Planck Society one of the three institutions whose researchers have received the most distinctions. The decisive factor for the count is whether the award winner was a Scientific Member of the Max Planck Society in the year the award was announced. This corresponds to the Nobel Foundation’s own criteria.

## What is the  
 Anthropocene?

To divide the life of the Earth into ages, geologists use characteristic traces in the Earth’s geology. About 11,700 years ago, the end of the last glacial period marks the beginning of the Holocene, the age in which we still live according to this classification. Around 20 years ago, Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer propose a new designation for the current age: the “Anthropocene”. Crutzen discusses the concept in 2002 in the journal “Nature”, dating its beginning to the industrialization that began in Britain towards the end of the 18th century. The basis for this is the beginning of the large-scale use of fossil fuels – as can be seen from the increase in CO₂.  
  
 Other researchers date the beginning of the Anthropocene to 1945 – the year of the first atomic bomb. This is because atomic bomb testing leaves novel radioactive markers in the Earth’s geology. It is also after the World War II that an economic boom affluent Western societies begins. The consumption of natural resources thus increases multiple times over, as do greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Nevertheless, the International Union of Geological Science (IUGS), which is responsible for the classification of the geological time scale, has not yet formally endorsed the concept of the Anthropocene.

## How short is an attosecond?

An attosecond is unimaginably short: one billionth of a billionth of a second. Film can visualize this time dimension. Light can circle the Earth about eight times in a second. Within an attosecond, it would travel just from one end of a single water molecule to another, which, with a diameter of only 0.3 nanometres, is incredibly small.

## The human family

![Image 2: The family of hominins has many branches.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-02/06-32-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-grafik-genfluss-menschenfamilie-vorab_0.png)

The family of hominins has many branches.

The graph shows the genetic contribution that extinct human forms that lived in Europe and Asia may have made to the genome of modern humans. It is unclear whether Denisova genes entered the genome of present-day Asians directly or via Oceania (dashed line).

© Kay Prüfer et al. Nature 000, 1-7 (2013) doi:10.1038 / nature12886

The evolution of hominins, a group of primates that includes not only humans living today but also our direct ancestors and extinct relatives, begins about six million years ago in Africa. Hominins are distinguished from other primates by their upright gait. The human lineage (_Homo_) has been recorded by fossil finds in Africa dating back around 2.8 million years; our own species (_Homo sapiens_) has been documented for some 300,000 years. Over the course of evolution, there have been many now-extinct species of humans living at the same time and often in close proximity. Genetic data from fossil bones also show mixing of different human species, such as our direct ancestors with Neanderthals.  
  
**The Denisova man**  
 In 2012 the team under Svante Pääbo deciphers the genome using a tiny bone found by archaeologists in the Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of western Siberia. The findings reveal it to be a hitherto unknown human species closely related to Neanderthals, which probably populated large parts of Asia. Denisova man contributes up to five percent of the genome of humans alive today. Research is currently ongoing into what Denisova humans looked like and how they interacted with our direct ancestors and Neanderthals.

## Konrad Lorenz  
 and politics

Using his research as a basis, Konrad Lorenz frequently made political statements. From the 1970s onwards, he becomes a fierce social critic. In 1972 the widely read Kronen-Zeitung newspaper reprints Konrad Lorenz’s essays “Civilized Man’s Eight Deadly Sins”, in which Lorenz reviles urbanization, nuclear weapons, the struggle for profit, and overpopulation. He also laments the “genetic decay” of society, thus drawing on the linguistic repertoire of National Socialism. When he is awarded the Nobel Prize a short time later, the critical public asks the prominent scientist uncomfortable questions about his role in the “Third Reich”. It is only under mounting pressure that Lorenz finally makes a reluctant statement on this question.

Since then, historians have proven that Lorenz was a supporter of the Nazi regime and favored its eugenics. In 1938 he welcomes the annexation of his native Austria by Hitler’s Germany with jubilation. The background to this is also Lorenz’s search for financial funding for his research, which is granted to him by the German Research Foundation shortly before that. He joins the Nazi party in 1938 and becomes a member of the party’s Office of Racial Policy with permission to speak. During World War II, Lorenz works as an army psychiatrist in occupied Posen, providing racial-psychological assessments.

## Father Goose

Konrad Lorenz initially conducts his research privately in the 1930s on his parents’ large estate in Altenberg. While raising waterfowl, Konrad Lorenz finds that a newly hatched chick has accidentally imprinted on him. He thus identifies the phenomenon of imprinting and the scope of his observation, as he discovers that imprinting is possible only in a short, sensitive phase. He christens the gosling Martina, and she follows him everywhere for the next few weeks. She becomes his roommate, sleeping on a heated pad next to his bed at night. The hand-raised gosling becomes an excellent subject of observation for the researcher over the course of a year. As an adult, she leaves him, just as a chick leaves its mother.

## What are the limits of the  
 electron microscope?

The resolution of the electron microscope is indeed theoretically limited by the wavelength of the beams used, but given the extremely short wavelengths of electron beams this is not relevant in practice. The problem of storing the microscope without any vibrations also no longer plays a role today.

![Image 13: Modern transmission electron microscope, 2015.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-02/05-14-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-modernes-transmissionselektronenmikroskop.jpg)

Modern transmission electron microscope, 2015.

© MPG / Frank Vinken

The limitations of the electron microscope (EM) lie in the very complex sample preparation required for this technology. Microscopy with electron beams will only work in a vacuum, so the samples have to be completely dry. Even the tiniest residues of water would evaporate abruptly in the vacuum, tearing the sample in the process. This alone is the most important limitation of electron microscopy, at least for the area of life sciences: Every sample examined in EM is dead, which means that life processes in cells cannot be investigated in this way, at least not directly. Drying a biological sample non-destructively– e.g.through freeze-drying– additionally is very complicated and costly.  
  
 The resolution that can be achieved in EM depends quite crucially on the quality of the preparation. The sample must be extremely thin so that the electrons can actually shine through it without destroying it in the process. These ultra-thin sections are produced with a special ultra-microtome, which has a particularly sharp blade made of diamond or glass. To obtain a high-contrast image, the sample also has to have the highest possible electrical conductivity. This is usually not the case with biological samples– and often not with inorganic material samples either. The ultrathin specimens are therefore vapor-deposited with a very thin layer of a heavy metal, e.g.gold.  
  
 With very well-prepared specimens, a resolution of less than one angstrom (one ten-millionth of a millimeter) can now be achieved with a modern electron microscope. This means an EM can make visible the atomic structure of matter.

Manfred Eigen is not only an outstanding scientist, he also has exceptional musical talent. He grows up in a musical home– his father is a cellist in the Bochum Symphony Orchestra. He already plays the piano at the age of five and gives his first concerts at twelve. Like Max Planck before him, he has to choose between a career in science or as a professional musician.  
  
 In the case of Manfred Eigen, however, the decision is made for him by the Second World War. At the age of fifteen, he and his classmates are drafted as Luftwaffe auxiliary personnel. That means he can give not even a thought to piano-playing. Manfred Eigen loses the years that are crucial for a young pianist – years that, he believes, are impossible to make up for. So after becoming a prisoner of war and subsequently escaping, he decides to study chemistry and physics. Yet playing the piano continues to be part of his life. In addition to his work as a scientist, Eigen occasionally performs in concerts and also undertakes recordings with renowned orchestras.

## The Compton effect

In the early 1920s, American physicist Arthur Holly Compton bombards crystals with hard X-rays. He discovers that an X-ray quantum that hits an electron in the outer atomic shell transfers some of its energy to that electron and then exits the atom as lower-energy scattered light. At the same time, the collision emits a recoil electron that leaves the atomic shell at a precisely predetermined angle – similar to a billiard ball that is played against the rail and bounces off in a precisely defined direction. According to Compton’s interpretation, the number of light quanta corresponds precisely to the number of recoil electrons. Energy and momentum in the system are preserved. This only works, however, if light has not only wave but also particle character.  
  
**The scientific dispute**  
 In February 1924 Niels Bohr and his collaborators submit an article to the physics journal Zeitschrift für Physik in which they theorize, among other things, that in the Compton effect the number of scattered light quanta and the number of recoil electrons coincide only in the average, but not in the atomic single process. Hence, in their opinion, an interpretation of the Compton effect based on light waves would also be possible. With such a proposition, however, they question the general validity of the law of conservation of energy – and shake the very foundations of physics.  
  
**The Measurements by Bothe and Geiger**  
 This fundamental dispute also draws in Walther Bothe and Hans Geiger. Shortly after Bohr’s publication, they announce an experiment that is intended to provide clarity in the same journal . The researchers have devised an ingenious measuring apparatus consisting of two so-called needle counters facing each other. This measuring device, developed by Geiger at the beginning of the 20th century, makes it possible for the first time to detect individual particles relatively easily. An X-ray beam is sent through the gap between the counters. While one needle counter responds only to the light quanta, the other registers only the recoil electrons. The deflections of “light counter” and “electron counter” are registered exactly in time on a bromide silver film, which – driven by a motor – moves past the counters at high speed.  
  
 The idea here is that if the light quantum and the recoil electron occur at exactly the same time (whih means their joint impact can thus be measured as a "coincidence"), then the particle nature of light is confirmed and at the same time the principle of conservation of energy is also ensured at the atomic level. If, on the other hand, the two counters react with a time delay, this argues against its particle nature and the strict validity of the law of conservation of energy.  
  
 It takes almost a year before Geiger and Bothe are able to present statistically confirmed results. The outcome is absolutely clear: With accuracy of one ten-thousandth of a second, “light counter” and “electron counter” respond simultaneously. Walther Bothe and Hans Geiger thus provide one of the strongest arguments for the existence of light quanta.

## Otto Warburg –  
 a quirky gentleman

![Image 15: Otto Heinrich Warburg, around 1940.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-01/04-09-01-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-otto-warburg-korbstuhl.jpg)

Otto Heinrich Warburg, around 1940.

© Archives of the Max Planck Society

Even during his lifetime, Warburg (1883-1970) enjoys the reputation of being a genius– and he himself is convinced of it. As the son of the director of the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt in Berlin, Warburg encounters the great minds of his time at an early age. Emil Fischer, Albert Einstein, Max Planck, and many others would come and go from his family home. As a young scientist, he works at the new research campus of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society in Dahlem, and as early as 1930 he is given his own Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Cell Physiology. Its nearby director’s house has a garden as well as stables, and he enjoys a morning ride before heading to the lab. This, combined with his general love of the aristocratic lifestyle, earns Otto Warburg the derisive title “Emperor of Dahlem”. At Warburg’s insistence, the architect also has to design the institute building in the style of a Prussian country mansion. The interior includes furniture from England and large-format oil paintings in which Warburg immortalizes his role models Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch, and Paul Ehrlich. He is also happy to sit for portraits himself, often in the company of his favorite dog.  
  
 Warburg lives in his villa together with his private secretary Jakob Heiss – although homosexuality was considered a crime and is subject to harsh punishment. On top of this, Warburg’s father is Jewish, but Otto Warburg nevertheless survives the National Socialist era unscathed and is able to continue his research almost unobstructed. The circumstances surrounding this cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

## And what’s all this about  
viruses?

Viruses are not living beings in the true sense of the word, because they have no metabolism of their own and cannot reproduce independently. The only thing they can do is infect a host cell. Only by “hijacking” the metabolism of this living cell can they live themselves.  
  
 Viruses consist only of their genetic material (DNA or RNA), an envelope, and sometimes some proteins that they need to infect a cell. They incorporate their genetic material into the genome of the host cell, which forces it to change its entire metabolism and produce new viruses in large numbers.

![Image 25: Even bacteria are attacked by viruses.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-01/04-04-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-bakteriophagen.jpg)

Even bacteria are attacked by viruses.

Here, such “bacteriophages” (also called “phages” for short) inject their genetic material, into a bacterial cell.

© eye of science / eyeofscience.com

Plants, fungi, animals, humans, and bacteria – as far as we know, all living things are attacked by viruses. These parasites are highly specialized and adapted to their respective hosts. Many human diseases are also caused by viruses, such as measles, herpes, chickenpox, influenza, and Covid-19.

![Image 26: The Coronavirus under the electron microscope.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-01/04-05-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-coronavirus.jpg)

The Coronavirus under the electron microscope.

The “spikes” are clearly visible here, protruding all around like the prongs of a crown. The right half of the picture is colored.

© Robert Koch Institute / Tobias Hoffmann, 2020

Viruses are very small, ranging in size from about 20 to 300 nanometers (a nanometer is one millionth of a millimeter). Only with the invention of the electron microscope(EM) have viruses become visible to the human eye. EM images are always black and white, but are often colored afterwards to make the structures better recognizable.

## What is a  
 model organism?

From a biological perspective, humans are animals– vertebrates, mammals, apes. Many basic biochemical reactions and principles function in human cells similarly or even exactly as in the cells of much simpler organisms. 95 percent of a mouse’s genes occur in the same or similar form in humans, and even in fruit flies the figure is still sixty percent. It is therefore possible to study, for example, the importance of individual genes and also the control of their activity in different phases of the organism’s development in so-called animal models.  
  
 Model animals have short generation times and many offspring, are easy to keep in the laboratory in a standardized way and, not least, there is already ample knowledge and documentation about them and their metabolisms. Today, the complete genome of these animals is generally known. Important animal models in research are nematodes, fruit flies, zebrafish, and mice.

Climate models – a world out of the computer

Climate models are created with the help of mainframe computers that must process enormous amounts of data for this purpose. ## Plastics and  
 naturally occurring substances

Materials colloquially referred to as “plastics” or “synthetics” are, in chemical terms, polymers. This means that they consist of one or a few simple chemical building blocks, known as monomers, which are strung together in long chains. The chains can be straight or branched, and this produces large or giant molecules. The low-pressure polyethylene produced with the method developed by Karl Ziegler consists of only one building block, the small hydrocarbon molecule ethylene. The chains are mostly unbranched.

![Image 42: Ethylene (also known chemically as ethene) is the basic building block of polyethylene.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-02/02-Kunststoff_weiss.png)

Ethylene (also known chemically as ethene) is the basic building block of polyethylene.

Countless monomers join together chemically with the aid of catalysis.

One example of a polymer made from two different building blocks is nylon. Nylon is first synthesized in 1935 and soon finds a wide variety of uses as a textile fiber. Pure nylon shirts are now a thing of the past, although modern fabrics often contain polyamides, a class of fabrics that also includes classic nylon. They make clothes stable and easy to care for.

![Image 43: Heroes would wear nylon.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-02/02-21-02-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-nylonhemden-werbung.jpg)

Heroes would wear nylon.

Advertisement for men’s shirts made of nylon, 1960s.

© picture alliance / The Advertising Archives

Polymers also exist in nature, however. The most abundant biomolecule of all, cellulose, is a natural polymer built from sugar. Cellulose is the main component of plant cell walls. When accumulated in even larger structures, it forms tear-resistant fibers and support structures, for example in wood. Collagen and keratin, which make up skin, hair, and bird feathers, are also natural polymers.  
  
 It is in the mid-19th century that the first plastics are produced from natural polymers. These include celluloid, which is produced from cellulose with the help of acids and camphor. It serves as an inexpensive substitute for costly natural materials such as ivory and amber – for billiard balls, for example. Galalith is also an early plastic; its basic substance is the natural milk protein casein.  
  
 It was not until the launch of Bakelite in 1909 that a fully synthetic, industrially produced plastic enters the market. It is made from the chemicals formaldehyde and phenol, which are cheaply available at the time. Bakelite is very hard and is insensitive to heat and acid, and it is soon being used for everyday objects, such as light switches and as housings for electrical appliances.

Natural polymers such as wood or starch are degraded by bacteria and fungi and therefore rot within a few years. This natural recycling is also referred to as the principle of “microbial infallibility”: For every substance formed by living organisms, at least one type of organism can be found in nature that can break it down. There are no such organisms or enzymes for plastic, and thus far there is no convincing evidence that these should arise through evolution. It is also likely that the enormous length of the chains in man-made polymers is problematic, since oil-eating bacteria are quite capable of metabolizing shorter simple petroleum hydrocarbons, which differ from polyethylene essentially in length.

## Why can  
 chiral molecules  
 be dangerous?

![Image 51: Chiral molecules behave to each other like right and left hands.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-01/02-38-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-chirale.png)

Chiral molecules behave to each other like right and left hands.

The way the atoms of a molecule are arranged three-dimensionally in space is important for its properties.

© Public domain

Although chiral molecules form the same substance in chemical terms, their two varieties (enantiomers) can exhibit quite different properties. One example is limonene, which is found in citrus fruits. One form, called (R)-Limonene, smells like orange, while the mirror-image (S)-Limonene smells like lemon. But the effects of the two chiral varieties of the same substance in the body can also differ – and then it can become very dangerous, as is tragically demonstrated by the example of thalidomide. Thalidomide becomes available on the market in 1957, and pregnant women start taking it. After a conspicuous number of children are born with severe malformations in the following years, the Grünenthal company takes the drug off the market in 1961.

![Image 52: Thalidomide preparation, around 1960.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-01/02-37-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-contergan.png)

Thalidomide preparation, around 1960.

© picture alliance / Joker / Jörg Loeffke

We now know that the active ingredient of thalidomide had such a devastating effect because it is chiral. While the R variant has a calming effect, the S variant damages the fetus. Yet the thalidomide scandal could not have been prevented even by separating the two variants, because both enantiomers can convert into each other inside the body.This is not the case with all chiral molecules, however, so it is hugely important when drugs are being produced to know the exact effect of both variants where there is chirality in the molecules.

As the nephew of company founder Robert Bosch, Carl Bosch (1874-1940) grows up in a technology-savvy environment. ## Einstein in the satnav

According to the general theory of relativity, in a strong gravitational field not only does light become redder, but clocks also run more slowly. Conversely, in a weaker field they move forward. The atomic clocks aboard GPS satellites, for example, “tick” faster because they orbit the Earth at an altitude of 20,000 kilometers and are thus exposed to a weaker gravitational field. This accounts for 46 millionths of a second per day.  
  
 In a navigation device, an additional consequence of special relativity has to be taken into account. Because satellite clocks move quickly, they run about seven millionths of a second slower per day than clocks on the ground.

![Image 55: Satellites orbit the Earth.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-02/07-25-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-satelliten-erde.jpg)

Satellites orbit the Earth.

Many satellites orbiting the Earth serve for communication. According to statista.de, there were around 5,500 of them in 2022.

© Adobe Stock / i-picture

Both effects together produce a time difference of 39 millionths of a second per day. This may seem ridiculously slight, but it has a significant impact on the accuracy of a navigator. Without corrections, the device would be “out of sync” by eleven kilometers after 24 hours.

## What is  
 the quantum leap?

![Image 56: The calculation of thermal radiation without the application of Planck’s formula results in the ultraviolet catastrophe.](https://www.nobel.mpg.de/system/files/2023-06/07-07-mpg-nobelpreis-digitalstory-strahlendiagramm_en_0.jpg)

The calculation of thermal radiation without the application of Planck’s formula results in the ultraviolet catastrophe.

The graph compares the curve obtained according to Max Planck’s radiation law (blue) with that obtained according to Rayleigh/Jeans (red) and Wien (yellow), which assume no quantization in the energy output. The curve shows the radiation intensity as a function of wavelength at a given temperature. In the range of small wavelengths, there are strong deviations.

© Gewerk

A black body swallows all the electromagnetic radiation hitting it and emits it as thermal radiation, or in small parts also as light. The intensity of the radiation here depends only on the temperature and not on the material of the black body. An idealized radiation source like this does not occur in nature. However, the sun is considered to be an almost perfect black body.  
  
 In the 1890s, the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt (PTR) in Berlin succeeds for the first time in building a model of a black body that could be used for experiments. In simple terms, it is a hollow cylinder with completely black inner walls that can be heated to high, precisely measurable temperatures.  
  
 If you take the intensity and the wavelength of the radiation at different temperatures in such an experiment and enter them in a diagram, the result is similar but mutually shifted curves. These show a simple correlation: The higher the temperature of a black body, the more intensively it emits energy at a certain wavelength. But how can the curve be explained theoretically?  
  
 Max Planck managed it with his new radiation law: He postulated that a black body does not emit its light continuously in an infinite number of arbitrarily small parts, but rather in the form of a finite number of energy packets. The researcher called these quanta. In his theoretical observation of the experimentally determined energy distribution of a blackbody radiator, he finds a natural constant that describes the relationship between the energy and frequency of the radiation. This is Planck’s constant, symbolized as h.  
  
 Today, the term “quantum leap” colloquially refers to a finding that significantly advances a development in a very short time. Historically, the term comes from one of the early applications of Planck’s quantum theory, namely Bohr’s atomic model: Here, an electron in an atom can only change its energy state by jumping from one shell to another – emitting a specific energy packet, a photon or quantum. The original “quantum leap” is therefore an extremely small one.

Black holes can be grasped only in mathematical terms and are otherwise inconceivable. The ring singularity has no expansion. More tangible, however, is the boundary of the black hole (event horizon). Within the ergosphere, everything must rotate with the black hole.

In the year 1783, the English natural philosopher and geologist John Michell ponders huge “dark stars” that do not emit light because of their extremely strong gravitation.  These phenomena are not made of matter, although they have a large mass. They bend space and time and have an almost irresistible attraction. The Schwarzschild radius indicates the size of the boundary separating a black hole from the outside world. The interior of a black hole is inaccessible. It cannot be described in physical terms.
