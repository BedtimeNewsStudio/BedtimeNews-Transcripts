There is no doubt that the fourth flight test of Starship was a sight to behold, reentering the atmosphere and limping its way through controlled descent. While the ship's resiliency was displayed during the flight, it's quite likely that a fair amount of luck was involved in the vehicle's survival.

With that said, it was never a secret that there were unknowns about the construction of Starship's heat shield, and the flaps specifically have been an area of concern for several years. With SpaceX's first kind-of-successful reentry completed, they can now work on improving the heat shield's design and quality.

> If you said, okay, what's the highest probability of failure on reentry? It's probably the hinge of the flaps. So, the rear hinge and the forward hinge of the flaps. 'Cause you have to have a rotating thing, but you can't just make everything out of tiles, so you have to have a seal.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [Starbase Tour with Elon Musk \[PART 2 // Summer 2021\]](https://youtu.be/SA8ZBJWo73E?si=LH8Op1HQosI_Jk4A&t=2897)", 30 July 2021![SunsetStack.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/f104b3e8-5250-4406-8f1a-08de5edb8696-large.jpg)The forward end of a Starship rocket.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

Prior to the fourth flight test, with the expectation that Ship 29 would at least make it to peak heating, Elon Musk reinforced the company's concerns about the heat shield and once again stated that the hinges were an area to watch.

It was not certain, and if anything was unlikely, that the ship would make it through the entry portion of the flight. Suffice to say, there were a lot of surprised people on June 6th.

> We have some unsolved questions on the heat shield, a bunch of which we don't even know that they are questions, and some we're aware of because they've been challenges in the past with the Space Shuttle. The hinge area, and the hinge gap, and sealing the hinge gap, and not having hot gas just go flowing super fast straight through \[the flap hinge\].

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [First Look Inside SpaceX's Starfactory w/ Elon Musk](https://youtu.be/aFqjoCbZ4ik?si=0LU53eeQDfLRZ_MW&t=729)", 5 June 2024![DiscoveryFlip.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/6b07e5b5-89fd-493c-8ae1-0d12c1190869-large.jpg)Discovery's heat shield on display during STS-114.  \|  Photo courtesy of [NASA](https://twitter.com/NASA)

During the construction of the first orbit-capable ships and the first three flight tests, SpaceX had already learned quite a bit about the tile attachment system, which meant that a lot of the simple issues had already been ironed out prior to the fourth flight.

However, atmospheric reentry and the forces that the ship needed to endure during this phase of flight were something that it hadn't done yet, and it was inevitable that at least some changes would have to be made.

### The Original Shield

Starship uses approximately 18,000 hexagonal heat shield tiles to cover the belly of the vehicle, many of which are a standardized hexagonal shape. While it is true that Starship has hundreds of unique tile shapes and sizes, the ease of installation and maintenance is vastly improved by having the majority of them as a common design.

These interlocking tiles are a prominent feature on any ship, making up the entire black half of the vehicle. Before discussing the changes made to the flight 5 heat shield, let's go over the details of what flew on flight 4.

![TileGlow.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/4e8d6a4c-6592-4fe7-98b6-9e2886d7fd01-large.jpg)Hexagonal heat shield tiles on a Starship.  \|  Photo courtesy of [RGV Aerial Photography](https://twitter.com/RGVaerialphotos)

#### Tiles & Attachment

There are really two main types of tiles on the ship. The vast majority, including nearly all of the standard hexagonal tiles, are attached to the ship using pins that are welded to the hull. For the curved areas and irregular shapes, SpaceX uses an adhesive to attach the tiles, however, they try to limit the number of areas that use this.

![TileF1RefCurveFlat.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/c910e225-b445-4e44-a65b-dd593618a33d-large.jpg)A tile that uses an adhesive for attachment (Left) and a tile that uses pins for attachment (Right).  \|  Photo courtesy of [SpaceX](https://twitter.com/SpaceX)

The mechanical attachment pins, which cover most of the Ship's windward side, are arranged in groupings of three per tile. Some tiles have fewer depending on the geometry of the area, but it's usually three. The pins can compress, and when the tile is pressed into place, lock into an internal core structure.

The length of these pins creates a gap space behind the heat tiles, however, this is not left open on the ship. Different materials can be placed behind this, and we'll discuss that throughout this article.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/1e87b0b4-2b89-4403-b0b2-fe2959633c4f-medium.jpg)Tile attachment pins on a Starship.  \|  Photo courtesy of [RGV Aerial Photography](https://twitter.com/RGVaerialphotos)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/9837a80d-c67a-4659-8a56-7ee63f46b109-medium.jpg)Tile attachment pins on a Starship.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Nic Ansuini](https://twitter.com/NicAnsuini) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

The hexagonal tiles that make up most of the heat shield on the tank section are a bit under 30cm across. On the inside, there are three core components with slots that receive the attachment pins. Also, notice how this style of tile has a lot of open space on the interior. This is not true for every tile on the vehicle, specifically the adhered ones.

When the pins clip into this core structure, they cannot be removed without destroying the tile. Removal requires three holes to be created over where the pins connect, allowing the attachment pins to be compressed from the inside and pulled out of the internal core.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/ee2a957d-5546-490e-b5d5-654a723ecbf9-medium.png)The backside of a standard heat shield tile.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Ron Parker](https://www.youtube.com/@ronhardrock468)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/a5be811f-26e6-455c-a0bf-4632f86038a9-medium.jpg)Damaged heat tiles with the core structures exposed.  \|  Photo courtesy of [BocaChicaGal](https://twitter.com/BocaChicaGal) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/c47ead22-1e07-4372-8370-c4d718899e8a-medium.jpg)Tile cores, without the heat tile itself, temporarily installed on a ship.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/6c693510-acae-4cc7-989a-067e259b5162-medium.jpg)A tile undergoing removal.  \|  Photo courtesy of [RGV Aerial Photography](https://twitter.com/RGVaerialphotos)

The adhered tiles, unlike the pinned ones, do not have an internal core because they do not have anything to lock into. Rather, the backside is much flatter in order to increase the surface area for the glue to bond to.

The images below show some closeup images of heat tiles that are adhered to the Ship. These are the tiles that come in a variety of shapes and sizes, so they all look a little different.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/d976b063-7be9-4bb8-9573-05835e20352d-medium.jpg)  The backside of an adhered heat shield tile, covered in blue adhesive.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Ron Parker](https://www.youtube.com/@ronhardrock468)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/de93631f-0e78-4035-8758-dffdf25bc265-medium.jpg)The backside of an adhered heat shield tile.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Ron Parker](https://www.youtube.com/@ronhardrock468)

On most areas of the ship, a red adhesive is used to bond the tiles to the steel. This includes the seams where barrel sections are joined, extremely curved areas, and some other random spots. Glue is applied to both the steel surface and the heat tile, and the tile is held in place by tape while the adhesive cures.

Originally, a blue adhesive was used for most areas, but following the abysmal performance of Ship 25's heat shield on the second flight test, many regions of tiles were replaced with red glue. Many areas were further replaced with this new adhesive following Ship 28's performance on the third flight test.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/a82e8b1a-6763-4d37-b849-b06ef37c8a65-medium.jpg)Adhesive applied on a ship immediately prior to seating a heat tile.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/28a713ac-fc0e-4acb-afd3-11ac6a3d9dfd-medium.jpg)Most of the adhered regions on a Starship's heat shield. This is does not depict every area and is only meant to give a rough visualization of the configuration.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

Since the tiles are adhered directly to the ship's steel surface, they must be a bit thicker than the pinned tiles. Remember, the attachment pins are designed to create a small gap for different materials behind the tiles, but these materials cannot be placed behind the adhered ones. The image below shows a few rows of adhered tiles in the middle, with pinned ones on the top and bottom.

![SideProfAdhereTile.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/4380ff8f-f81b-46fb-aa65-5a63ce86d45b-large.jpg)A side profile of pinned and adhered tiles.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

#### Thermal Blanket

For the ships that flew on the first four flight tests, SpaceX applied a white thermal blanket in the open area beneath all of the pinned tiles. This likely served as backup thermal protection, but Musk has stated that this configuration was not resilient to the loss of a single heat shield tile.

By being placed behind the tiles, the blanket, often referred to as the "felt", filled the gaps between the tiles that otherwise would have been exposed steel. As such, it likely protected the steel from the heating it would experience from these small gaps.

> Right now, we are not resilient to loss of a single tile in most places, as the secondary containment material will probably not survive.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, [Twitter Post](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/1796049014938357932), 30 May 2024

A mesh material was also placed overtop of this, which likely served as a method of keeping the felt held in place. While the felt would compress upon installation of the tiles, the additional mesh likely assisted with keeping everything tidy both before and after installation.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/4f60b395-f00e-4a79-a964-5c2d92190a1e-medium.jpg)Application of the felt and mesh on a barrel section.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Nic Ansuini](https://twitter.com/NicAnsuini) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/362a9cbc-ca77-493c-a38b-145cf2e5bd25-medium.jpg)A closeup of the felt and mesh layers.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Starship Gazer](https://twitter.com/StarshipGazer)

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/7cff6189-d244-4e67-900b-5f60dceaa94a-medium.jpg)A nosecone with the felt and mesh layers applied.  \|  Photo courtesy of [BocaChicaGal](https://twitter.com/BocaChicaGal) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

#### Tile Gap Filler

In some areas of the heat shield with exaggerated curves, SpaceX uses the white felt as a gap filler to bridge the gaps between the heat tiles. Ship 20, infamous for its crude heat shield, had gap filler in very few places, which led to some rather atrocious tile gaps.

On what would have been Ship 21, SpaceX added the filler material to the tip of the nosecone, and on Ship 24, it was added to the top of the flap aerocovers. The images below show how noticeable the gaps were prior to the introduction of the filler material.

### A New Shield

With atmospheric reentry on the fourth flight test not quite going to plan, SpaceX quickly began preparing the upgraded heat shield for the fifth flight. Unfortunately, this meant that extensive work would need to be done on the ship, but ultimately, SpaceX's iterative design approach often requires extensive retrofits to be made.

![TileTest.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/6e804220-60fe-418d-930b-5a65d66e8a5b-large.jpg)Starship tiles undergoing thermal testing in 2019.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Elon Musk](https://twitter.com/elonmusk)

A few days after the fourth flight, Musk announced that Ship 30's entire heat shield would be replaced, and upgraded materials would be added beneath it. While the exact details of the upgrades were unclear at the time, the community collectively began watching Ship 30 in the Starbase Highbay.

In true SpaceX fashion, it took just over a month to complete most of the heat shield replacement work, and below, we'll go over the upgrades that took place over June and the first half of July.

> We're going to replace the whole heat shield on the ship. So, the new heat shield tile is about twice as strong as the ones that were on the \[fourth\] flight. We want to put an ablative secondary structure, like basically ablative protection behind the tiles, so that if a tile cracks or comes loose, it doesn't cook the rocket.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [Twitter Broadcast @18:28](https://x.com/i/broadcasts/1YpJkwgbQQdJj)", 9 June 2024![S29Flap.png](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/2371e546-fa4b-437b-ac58-2c881fe08b0c-large.png)Atmospheric reentry with a tile provided for scale.  \|  Photo courtesy of [SpaceX](https://twitter.com/SpaceX)

#### Ablative Protection Layer

It didn't take long to remove most of the tiles from Ship 30, and once the attachment pins for the tiles were exposed, crews began preparing to apply a new sublayer on the heat shield. The new layer would be placed in the open gap beneath the pinned tiles that was previously occupied by the white felt.

What isn't as easily visible is that there are a couple of different configurations of materials beneath Ship 30's new heat shield, and in fact, some areas still have the exact same white felt. Let's explain.

![TimeComingOff30.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/df80ac4d-783c-4389-8475-7bbcd9ece607-large.jpg)Ship 30's original heat shield being removed.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

When SpaceX removed Ship 30's heat shield tiles, they also removed the original felt, but they didn't remove all of it. A region of felt approximately 13 tiles wide was not removed on the left and right sides of the heat shield. This resulted in essentially a large strip being removed down the center of the Ship.

In the image below, we've highlighted the region of felt that was not removed, and you can see the open space to the right where the new materials would be placed.

![BoundaryBODY30.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/aa2e9da2-7cf1-4b17-8eaf-655f3cf2edc3-large.jpg)Ship 30's common dome section, with a strip of the original white felt remaining.  \|  Photo courtesy of [BocaChicaGal](https://twitter.com/BocaChicaGal) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

The areas where the felt wasn't removed would simply have new tiles installed. These areas are specifically where tiles trail towards the backside of the Ship, which means that they likely experience far lower heating, and as a result, likely aren't areas where a missing tile would cause a breach.

![SideTileReinstall30.png](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/d9043c35-77cb-4c8c-bd30-f2fbf65021f8-large.png)Tiles being installed on the side portion of Ship 30 where the original felt remained.  \|  Photo courtesy of [BocaChicaGal](https://twitter.com/BocaChicaGal) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

The new ablative layer that Musk mentioned is a sheet of black material with holes to slot over the tile attachment pins. This material was applied down the central portion of the ship, the faces of the ship's flaps, and the areas that bridge the gap between the flaps and the vehicle's body.

The thing is, there is actually even more material added on top of the new ablative.

The black ablative layer is considerably thinner than the original felt, which is key because the thickness of any materials occupying the area under the tiles must be a similar thickness to what was previously installed.

SpaceX installed an additional thin layer of felt overtop of every surface where the black ablative material was applied, which means that not only do the flap areas and central portion of the Ship have the new ablative material, but they actually have an additional layer of felt to provide some protection.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/e062af8e-899d-4fc3-bca6-ede2cc556689-medium.jpg)Extra layer of felt on top of the black ablative.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Sean Doherty](https://twitter.com/SeanKD_Photos) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

On the fourth flight test, SpaceX tested different configurations of ablative materials on the aft section of Ship 29. Following the flight test, Musk noted that their "two-layer" solution survived reentry and sufficiently protected the Ship. This black material and thin felt combination is likely what he was referring to.

> Of the two tiles that we put on the rear \[of Ship 29\], where we took the tiles off, we had actually put some backup ablative there. One was two layers of ablative, one was one layer of ablative. The two-layer of ablative did not burn through, the one-layer of ablative did. I'm not sure if it actually burned through the steel too, but it did erode the single layer of ablative. But, it just says that if you have two layers of our ablative, it does not actually burn through.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [See Starship and the Orbital Launch Pad w/ Elon Musk](https://youtu.be/InJOlT6WdHc?si=ebcsc-ptXnxUoplr&t=1793)", 6 June 2024  ![S29TestTiles.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/d2de191b-33bf-42aa-b860-fa93dcd8e69d-large.jpg)Three different configurations of ablative test patches on Ship 29.  \|  Photo courtesy of [SpaceX](https://twitter.com/SpaceX)

During Tim Dodd's tour of the Starfactory prior to the fourth flight test, the nosecone with heat tiles already had the black ablative material and thin white felt layers installed, which suggests that this entire upgrade is backported from the Block 2 ship. This likely explains why the upgrade was deployed so quickly after the flight of Ship 29.

![B2Ablat.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/b63cdac9-8b97-47e1-ac54-e01a25135098-large.jpg)A nosecone for the upgraded Starship with the ablative and thin felt layers visible beneath the heat tiles.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Everyday Astronaut](https://twitter.com/Erdayastronaut)

#### New Heat Tiles

In addition to the updated configuration for the heat shield's sublayer, SpaceX replaced the vast majority of the ship's heat tiles. For the most part, they replaced the tiles that use the three attachment pins. According to Musk, the new tiles are stronger, although it isn't super clear what he meant by this.

He may have been referring to the tile's ability to protect the vehicle, the tile's resiliency to damage, or something else. The latter seems likely, but we're not sure exactly how they would go about doing this.

> We're going to replace the whole heat shield on the Ship. So, the new heat shield tile is about twice as strong as the ones that were on the \[fourth\] flight.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [Twitter Broadcast @18:28](https://x.com/i/broadcasts/1YpJkwgbQQdJj)", 9 June 2024

Looking at one of the new heat shield tiles, we can notice that externally it looks... exactly the same. We can't be sure of what exactly changed on these heat shield tiles, but we know that the shape, size, and core structure all appear to be very similar.

There may be changes to the composition, changes to the black layer on the front, changes to how it is constructed, changes to the internals, or a multitude of other things. The reality is that it's just hard to be sure without an official comment.

![TileBacking.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/fd46cc9a-2011-4c4f-bfae-af82d1ec0880-large.jpg)The backside of a new heat tile.  \|  Photo courtesy of [Starship Gazer](https://twitter.com/StarshipGazer)

Either way, Ship 30's tiles are almost all brand new. They specifically did not touch most of the adhered tiles, such as the barrel section seams, the flap aerocovers, the edges of the flaps, and others, but they may have determined that the tiles in these areas are sufficient.

However, this does mean that the hot gas seals on Ship 30's flaps are essentially unchanged. This likely suggests that the seal between the flap and the ship was not the issue SpaceX observed, but was rather the tolerances and gaps that we'll discuss in a moment.

![TileNotRemove.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/c5556d60-7719-4c86-91cb-2c29f8fde537-large.jpg)Regions of tiles on the aerocovers and flaps that were not removed.  \|  Photo courtesy of [BocaChicaGal](https://twitter.com/BocaChicaGal) for [NASASpaceflight](https://twitter.com/NASASpaceflight)

#### Adhesive Upgrade

Similar to [Ship 29](https://ringwatchers.com/article/s29-b11-updates#ship-thermal-protection-system), the adhered tiles on the two bands of external stringers and the tip of the nosecone were replaced and received a new adhesive. This upgrade isn't actually from the fourth flight test, but rather, was a lesson learned from the third flight test.

Even though this isn't necessarily a new change from Ship 29, it was still a modification that Ship 30 underwent during its heat shield work.

#### Additional Gap Filler

According to Musk, Ship 29's right forward flap was likely doomed due to inconsistent tolerances and gaps during the production process, resulting in larger-than-expected gaps that allowed plasma to breach the area.

> The right forward flap was the one that had the \[worst damage\], 'cause there's tolerance variations. There's a little bit of just stochastic luck... But it looks like, and I'm just guessing here, this is speculative, that the gaps in the tiles were a little wider on the right than the left side.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [See Starship and the Orbital Launch Pad w/ Elon Musk](https://youtu.be/InJOlT6WdHc?si=4I_ABbub2rYc3DP-&t=1467)", 6 June 2024

Similar to what SpaceX did to the tip of the nosecone and the flap aerocovers on previous vehicles, the tried-and-true solution for poor tile gaps is just to add a bunch of filler material in the areas of concern. In this case, the areas of concern seem to be the adhered tiles and the forward flap areas.

As mentioned earlier, the gap filler for these heat shield tiles essentially involves adding felt between the gaps, which is often adhered in place. The image below shows some gap filler prior to being trimmed.

![GapFiller30.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/d52d5db2-4f98-4f7d-9ebf-616ee335900b-large.jpg)Gap filler on Starship heat tiles.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

Technicians added additional gap filler to the edges of all four body flaps and the static aeros that bridge the flap and the Ship's body, however, the latter was only done for the forward flaps.

> We need to make the flap hinge area far more robust. We'll really go crazy hardcore on that... There are just things you can do to increase the tile precision and decrease the gap, improve the gap filler, thicken the tiles... We'll just go to town.

\-\- Elon Musk, CEO, SpaceX, " [See Starship and the Orbital Launch Pad w/ Elon Musk](https://youtu.be/InJOlT6WdHc?si=MYlIU_ZYI5o62H_x&t=1295)", 6 June 2024

Gap filler was also added to several areas on the tank section of the Ship. You'll now notice it on the horizontal seams where the ship was stacked and also on the bands of external stringers. It's important to note that these areas all used adhered tiles.

Interestingly, you may notice that the gap filler appears to be either a blue or a red colour in some areas, which is actually the adhesive that they use to affix the gap filler in place. There's a lot of residue, but this is likely just a cosmetic issue.

![](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/6f7d1582-858d-49a4-9ecd-edc451fd6ec9-medium.jpg)Gap filler on one of the barrel seams.  \|  Photo courtesy of [John Cargile](https://twitter.com/GroundTruthPics/) for [WAI Media](https://twitter.com/FelixSchlang)

Due to the inability to place felt behind the adhered tiles, SpaceX may have observed higher-than-expected heating in the gaps. Remember, the felt behind the normal tiles likely protects the steel from any tile gaps, but this isn't present on the adhered ones. By gluing felt to the sides of the adhered tiles, SpaceX may be attempting to mitigate this.

There are also various instances of individual tiles around the Ship having gap filler added, which may be heat tiles with embedded sensors or areas that are extra sensitive to heating.

Our observations have indicated that at least some of these are tiles that had to be adhered in place, likely due to damaged tile attachment pins. So, this would further suggest that the gap filler is directly related to the adhesive.

### Survive

The fifth flight test of Starship is approaching, and as SpaceX finally prepares to return Super Heavy to the launch complex after a flight, Starship's aspirations of reusability get ever closer.

Starship isn't a fully reusable launch vehicle yet, but step by step, they are making moves in that direction. Reliability upgrades on Ship 30's thermal protection system almost certainly provide some sort of mass penalty, but as it stands, SpaceX is likely prioritizing a working Ship over an optimized one. The latter can come eventually, but for the time being, it's time to get this vehicle into a working state.

![RHEntry.jpg](https://api.ringwatchers.com/images/09f01dcb-6b4b-4166-89d6-12f2aac856f5-large.jpg)A rendered depiction of Starship's atmospheric reentry.  \|  Render courtesy of [Ryan Hansen Space](https://twitter.com/RyanHansenSpace)

This added resiliency to tile loss should make it far less likely to lose a Ship during reentry, but with one issue fixed, there is always room for another to uncover itself. It seems that the issues during the previous flight were more a tolerance issue rather than a design issue with the seal, but we'll learn more as they keep flying.

It's very unlikely that this will be the last form of Starship's heat shield, but SpaceX has proven time and time again that they can keep working away on something until it performs as intended. It's tough to say how long it will take and how much work will go into it, but hopefully, they will eventually reach a point where the Ship can safely return to the pad.

If anything, excitement is, as always, guaranteed.
