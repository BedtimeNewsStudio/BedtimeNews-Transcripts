**The BM-25 Musudan** (Hwasong-10) is a North Korean intermediate-range ballistic missile (IRBM). It is speculated to have a range of 2,500 – 4,000 km with a 500 – 1,200 kg payload—enough to hold bases in Guam and Japan at risk.

## BM-25 Musudan Development

The BM-25 Musudan is derived from the Soviet [R-27 (NATO: SS-N-6 ‘Serb’)](https://missilethreat.csis.org/missile/ss-n-6-r-27-serb/) submarine-launched ballistic missile. North Korea reportedly began developing the Musudan missile in 1992, when it formalized a contract with Russia’s Makeyev Engineering Design Office to produce an R-27-derived space launch vehicle. North Korea allegedly completed Musudan prototypes in 2000, and in September 2003, U.S. officials observed the first deployments of Musudan launchers at Mirim airbase near Pyongyang. The Musudan system began to enter North Korean service at some point between 2003 and 2008.[1](#easy-footnote-bottom-1-268 "Jeffrey Lewis, &#8220;Origins of the Musudan IRBM,&#8221; <em>Arms Control Wonk, </em>June 11, 2012, https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/205337/origins-of-the-musudan-irbm/; &#8220;North Korea Missile Capabilities,&#8221; <em>Nuclear Threat Initiative, </em>May 1, 2010, https://www.nti.org/analysis/articles/north-korea-missile-capabilities/.")

### Testing

North Korea first publicly displayed the Musudan missile on parade in 2010. Despite concerns of an impending test, North Korea did not test launch the Musudan until April 15, 2016. The test was unsuccessful. According to South Korean and Pentagon officials, the test concluded with a “fiery, catastrophic attempt at a launch,” destroying the missile.[2](#easy-footnote-bottom-2-268 "“North Korean missile test &#8216;fiery, catastrophic&#8217; failure: Pentagon,” Reuters, April 15, 2016, http://www.reuters.com/article/us-northkorea-missile-usa-pentagon-idUSKCN0XC28N; Anne Fifield, “North Korea’s missile launch has failed, South’s military says,” The Washington Post, April 15, 2016, https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/asia_pacific/north-koreas-missile-has-failed-officials-from-south-say/2016/04/14/8eb2ce53-bc38-40d0-9013-5655bed26764_story.html") Two successive tests—on April 27 and 28—also failed.[3](#easy-footnote-bottom-3-268 "U.S. Strategic Command Public Affairs, &#8220;USSTRATCOM Detects, Tracks Attempted North Korean Missile Launches,&#8221; U.S. Strategic Command, April 28, 2016, https://www.stratcom.mil/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/983700/update-1-usstratcom-detects-tracks-attempted-north-korean-missile-launches/.") On May 30, North Korea conducted its fourth unsuccessful test of the Musudan, with the missile exploding shortly after launch.[4](#easy-footnote-bottom-4-268 "Ju-min Park, &#8220;Attempted North Korea missile launch fails: South Korea,&#8221; <em>Reuters, </em>May 30, 2016, https://www.reuters.com/article/us-northkorea-missile-fail/attempted-north-korea-missile-launch-fails-south-korea-idUSKCN0YM015.")

On June 21, 2016, North Korea flight-tested two additional missiles, achieving its first successful flight of the system. Japanese and South Korean officials stated that the first missile traveled approximately 150 km from a launch point near Wonsan before breaking up in flight.[5](#easy-footnote-bottom-5-268 "Gabriel Dominguez, Chieko Tsuneoka, and Karl Dewey, “North Korean ballistic missile fires 400 km,” IHS Jane’s Defence Weekly, June 22, 2016, http://www.janes.com/article/61684/north-korean-ballistic-missile-flies-400-km") In state media releases, North Korea claimed that testers intentionally destroyed the missile mid-flight.[6](#easy-footnote-bottom-6-268 "Julian Ryall, “North Korea claims mid-air explosion of Musudan was ‘deliberate,’” IHS Jane’s Defence Weekly, July 7, 2016, http://www.janes.com/article/62058/north-korea-claims-mid-air-explosion-of-musudan-was-deliberate") North Korea conducted its second, successful launch two hours later. During this test, the Musudan traveled 400 km while reaching a maximum altitude of over 1,000 km.The Department of Defense confirmed that STRATCOM detected and tracked both missile launches and that each fell into the Sea of Japan.[7](#easy-footnote-bottom-7-268 "“Stratcom Detects, Tracks North Korean Missile Launches,” Department of Defense, June 22, 2016.") In a statement following the test, North Korea claimed the “sure capability to attack in an overall and practical way the Americans in the Pacific operation theater.”[8](#easy-footnote-bottom-8-268 "Jack Kim, “North Korea leader says missile launch shows ability to attack U.S. in Pacific,” Reuters, June 23, 2016, http://www.reuters.com/article/us-northkorea-missile-kim-idUSKCN0Z82PB?feedType=RSS&amp;feedName=topNews&amp;utm_source=twitter&amp;utm_medium=Social")

Given the steep, “lofted” trajectory of the successful test, analysts estimate that the Musudan could range 2,500 to 4,000 km, placing U.S. bases in South Korea, Japan, and Guam at risk.[9](#easy-footnote-bottom-9-268 "Ankit Panda, &#8220;North Korea Just Test Launched 2 More Musudan Missiles. Here&#8217;s Why That Matters,&#8221; <em>The Diplomat, </em>June 22, 2016, https://thediplomat.com/2016/06/north-korea-just-test-launched-2-more-musudan-missiles-heres-why-that-matters/.") Additionally, the test’s lofted trajectory could demonstrate an alternative employment mode for the Musudan system. The increased reentry speed gained from a lofted missile could potentially represent an effort to stress U.S. and South Korean missile defenses. [10](#easy-footnote-bottom-10-268 "John Schilling, “A Partial Success for the Musudan,” 38 North, June 23, 2016, http://38north.org/2016/06/jschilling062316/")

On October 9 and 19, 2016, North Korea conducted two unconfirmed tests of the Musudan missile from Kusong in the country’s northwest. Both tests reportedly have failed catastrophically. While the U.S. Strategic Command and most analysts suspected the tests involved the Musudan missile, some speculated at the time that the test may have involved the [Hwasong-13 (KN-08)](https://missilethreat.csis.org/missile/kn-08/), a developmental North Korean ICBM.[11](#easy-footnote-bottom-11-268 "Anna Fifield, &#8220;Did North Korea just test missiles capable of hitting the U.S.? Maybe,&#8221; <em>The Washington Post, </em>October 26, 2016, https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/asia_pacific/did-north-korea-just-test-missiles-capable-of-hitting-the-us-maybe/2016/10/26/984e8a21-e6a7-4689-81e0-21d7d25c302f_story.html; Amanda Macias, &#8220;A North Korean nuclear attack &#8216;will be met with an overwhelming and effective response&#8217;, Business Insider, October 20, 2016, http://www.businessinsider.com/pentagon-north-korean-nuclear-attack-overwhelming-response-2016-10.")

## Specifications

The Musudan IRBM is a single-stage missile with an estimated length of 12 – 19 m and a diameter of 1.5 – 2.0 m. The missile is thought to possess a launch weight of 19,000 – 26,000 kg and carries a warhead of approximately 1,200 kg. Missile guidance is inertial with an accuracy of 1,600 m circular error probable (CEP).

The missile uses a North Korean variant of the Russian 4D10 liquid-fueled rocket engine found on the [R-27](https://missilethreat.csis.org/missile/ss-n-6-r-27-serb/) SLBM. Fueled by more energetic unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH) and nitrogen tetroxide (NTO) propellants, the 4D10 offers enhanced performance over the kerosene-powered Scud and Nodong missile engines. This allows the Musudan to achieve ranges of 2,500 to 4,000 km without significantly increasing the missile’s size.[12](#easy-footnote-bottom-12-268 "Jeffrey Lewis, “New DPRK ICBM Engine,” Arms Control Wonk, April 9, 2016, http://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/1201278/north-korea-tests-a-fancy-new-rocket-engine/.")

## Service History

Despite its poor performance in testing, the U.S. intelligence in 2020 still classified the Musudan as deployed with fewer than 50 launchers.

North Korea reportedly sold 18 Musudan missile sets to Iran in 2005. According to unofficial reports, Iran may have flight-tested a Musudan missile on behalf of North Korea in May 2007.[13](#easy-footnote-bottom-13-268 "Ibid.") In 2009, additional reports surfaced linking North Korea and [Iran’](https://missilethreat.csis.org/iran/) s missile efforts, indicating that Pyongyang had transferred Musudan parts to [Iran](https://missilethreat.csis.org/iran/).[14](#easy-footnote-bottom-14-268 "John Pomfret and Walter Pincus “Experts question North Korea-Iran missile link from WikiLeaks document release,” The Washington Post, December 1, 2010, http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/content/article/2010/11/30/AR2010113006781.html")
