# India seizes $725 million of Xiaomi assets over illegal remittances

NEW DELHI (Reuters) -India said on Saturday it had seized $725 million from the local bank accounts of China's Xiaomi Corp after a probe found the smartphone maker had made illegal remittances to foreign entities by passing them off as royalty payments.

The Enforcement Directorate had been investigating the Chinese company's business practices over suspected violations of Indian foreign exchange laws.

The financial crime fighting agency said on Saturday it had seized the bank account assets from Xiaomi Technology India Private Limited after finding the firm had remitted the foreign currency equivalent of 55.5 billion rupees to three foreign-based entities, including one Xiaomi group entity, "in the guise of royalty" payments.

The remittance to two other unidentified and unrelated U.S.-based entities was also for "the ultimate benefit of the Xiaomi group entities", the agency added in a statement.

"Such huge amounts in the name of royalties were remitted on the instructions of their Chinese parent group entities," the directorate said.

Xiaomi said in a statement issued later on Saturday that it complies with Indian laws and believed its "royalty payments and statements to the bank are all legit and truthful".

"These royalty payments that Xiaomi India made were for the in-licensed technologies and IPs used in our Indian version products ... we are committed to working closely with government authorities to clarify any misunderstandings," it added.

The directorate's actions against Xiaomi signal widening scrutiny of the Chinese smartphone maker, whose India office was raided in December in a separate investigation over alleged income tax evasion. Some other Chinese smartphone markers were also raided at the time.

Reuters reported on April 12 that Xiaomi's former India head, Manu Kumar Jain, had been summoned for questioning as part of the directorate's investigation.

Jain, who is now a global vice president at Xiaomi based in Dubai, appeared before investigators earlier this month, said a source with direct knowledge of the probe, asking not to be named due to the sensitivity of the matter.

The Enforcement Directorate also asked the company for details of foreign funding, shareholding and funding patterns, financial statements and information of key executives running the business.

Xiaomi was India's leading smartphone seller in 2021, with a 24% market share, according to Counterpoint Research. South Korea's Samsung was the No. 2 brand with a 19% share.

Many Chinese companies have struggled to do business in India due to political tensions following a border clash in 2020. India has cited security concerns in banning more than 300 Chinese apps since then, including popular ones like TikTok, and also tightened norms for Chinese companies investing in India.

# Here’s where Canadians can build wealth over the next decade

Serah Louis

Tue, November 10, 2026 at 10:18 a.m. EST6 min read

![Canadian money dollar bills standing up isolated cutout on white background with copy space](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/0e8e06a8b04906aed0149ae91a0a22e4419f9edefced59b4590a8fae1ba1c374/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Ffinancial_post_articles_610%2F930d332432c66ff54a7336d3165a5e5d.jpg)

Here's the top spots where Canadians should be looking to build wealth over the next decade. (JulieAlexK/Getty Images)

_In recent years, Canadians have enjoyed a sharp growth in financial assets, bolstered by the tech and artificial intelligence boom, while housing wealth has hit a plateau. "Canadian wealth has really shifted from the driveway to the brokerage," said Theresa Shutt, chief investment officer at Harbourfront Wealth Management. "Wealth is increasingly coming from markets, not paychecks, not assets." But as concerns about market conditions grow, that may be changing. We asked Shutt and other wealth advisers for their insights on where Canadians can build wealth over the next decade_.

## **Stocks**

Investing in stocks has been the main strategy for wealth building in recent years, and it's no secret that the technology sector has dominated the market.

"There's probably going to be a lot of progress and good investment opportunities within the technology sector, which is now the largest sector on a global basis," said Josh Sheluk, portfolio manager and chief investment officer at Verecan Capital Management Inc., but added that a diversified tech portfolio is key. "It's important for investors not to just chase … everything that is at the forefront of this AI revolution."

As an example, Sheluk suggested someone invested in memory chip stocks could also invest in software companies, which still can thrive if the AI movement doesn't live up to the hype. Shutt suggested investors consider AI investments outside of the U.S., such as more modestly valued entry points in Asia, and related infrastructure, such as cooling, grid equipment and memory companies' stocks.

Outside of tech, Shutt called banks and utilities the "bright spots" in Canada's wealth-building picture.

"These are longer-term holds," she said. "They're dividend-paying and they will benefit from the infrastructure build-up that we're hopefully going to see in Canada."

## **Real estate**

Real estate is not necessarily a top contender for wealth building over the next decade, especially compared with stock returns, Sheluk said.

"I wouldn't expect real estate to have some tremendous recovery and deliver the returns that it had through the 2010s in Canada, for example, because that's not the norm," he said.

Jason Heath, managing director at Objective Financial Partners, said a savvy renter can save and contribute to an investment account on a monthly basis, building wealth over the long run even without owning property.

Though financial markets have outperformed the housing sector, Shutt said it is still a good idea to own a home.

"It's certainly a different market than it was in the '80s, '90s, even early 2000s, where your house was your No. 1 asset, and continued to appreciate," said Shutt. However, "the home you live in still has real value: it's forced savings."

That said, the type of property you purchase matters, too. The condo market is no longer an attractive proposition, Shutt said. "Condos used to be the entry point for small investors, but that thesis has broken due to higher borrowing costs and a glut of supply," she said.

## **Private equity**

There has been a push for private equity, private credit, private real estate and private infrastructure, creating more opportunities for investors in recent years, said Sheluk. And while many private investments are only available to accredited investors and institutions, more are becoming available to ordinary investors, especially through funds.

With more private companies than public ones, these investments can offer a more diverse representation of the economy, said Shutt.

However, the downsides of private investments include less liquidity due to investments being locked up for years, a lack of transparency and higher fees and risk, said Sheluk.

"Today, private companies are trading at a discount compared to public companies where the valuations are at peak highs so there is an opportunity for attractive returns going forward," said Shutt.

But though Sheluk expects private equity "should do reasonably well" over the long term, he said higher interest rates are a concern, as they make private investments' often leveraged business models less attractive. He added that potential higher valuations leave less room for companies to grow and that greater investor interest today could possibly mean less upside in the future.

## **Commodities**

Commodities have performed well over the past year. Critical minerals that have industrial uses, such as copper and silver, are promising, while lithium is integral to the AI data centre buildout, said Shutt.

"But don't discount other commodities such as copper, which sits at the heart of electrification, natural gas which is critical for food exports, and uranium as nuclear power emerges as the preferred clean energy source for data centres," she added.

IG Wealth Management's chief investment strategist Philip Petursson said that though IG Wealth exited its gold position in March due to limited near-termupside potential, he believes the precious metal could be an attractive long-term hold.

Peturrson said IG Wealth measures gold against the M2 money supply, or the liquid and near-liquid currency and assets circulating in the economy, which he expects will continue to move higher over the next decade. "You assume that … gold will move higher in line with that," he said, though he added that he doesn't anticipate investors will see the same growth in gold that they did in the past couple of years.

As for energy, Peturrson said that while the war in Iran has driven the price of oil higher this year, energy growth may not just be dependent on current geopolitical conditions.

"Even if the war in Iran ended tomorrow, I think oil prices would remain higher than where we were a year ago," because of damage to oil infrastructure and the significant drawdown of the reserves in petroleum and petroleum products, he said.

While futures provide the purest exposure to the commodities market, they can also present significant volatility and complexity, Shutt said. "Funds and ETFs might provide the most attractive access point by providing exposure to a more diversified basket of commodities and producers at a lower cost."

Ultimately, given that commodities can experience big swings, along with exposure to nonmarket risks such as regulation and politics, Shutt said it is important to size these positions prudently in a diversified portfolio.

## **Side hustles**

Starting a side hustle is a great way to supplement your main income and help grow your wealth, said Objective's Heath.

Heath said he has seen this strategy across age groups — younger Canadians who want to monetize a hobby or make ends meet amid cost-of-living issues and older Canadians who are transitioning into retirement but don't want to completely stop working.

# Houston resists calls for legislation as AI data centre backlash grows

Lyndsay Armstrong

October 30, 20268 min read

![](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/cc4a8d113aa0afcd1bb9d5cdeaa4051b7b70b10504b1325076b3f3a32bd5f126/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fcp_finance_889%2Ff470427413f5b53ea86cc530f0da2ee0.jpg)

Nova Scotia Premier Tim Houston speaks during a funding announcement at EverWind Fuels in Point Tupper, N.S., on Friday, Oct. 16, 2026. THE CANADIAN PRESS/Steve Wadden - The Canadian Press

HALIFAX — Julissa Stewart says she panicked when she learned about plans for an 18,600-square-metre AI data centre at an industrial site about 20 minutes away from her home in southern Cape Breton.

Earlier this month, EverWind Fuels announced it intends to set up a $10-billion AI data centre in Point Tupper, N.S., that would come online by the end of 2027.

The American AI company Anthropic has signed a 20-year lease for the centre, which would draw 95 megawatts of electricity during its first phase. The company says a 325-megawatt expansion is expected in mid-2028, which would bring the development's total energy demand to 420 megawatts.

"I've been paying attention to what's happening with AI data centres in other places in Canada and in the States … then hearing that it's coming here, so close to where we live, I have serious concerns. Not because I'm against economic development, but because of how this is being rolled out," Stewart said in a recent interview.

The project by EverWind and Anthropic has thrust Nova Scotia into a debate that is playing out across Canada and the United States, as technology companies scramble to find locations for data centres needed to deliver the power for artificial intelligence and other computing infrastructure.

In many cases, it has placed politicians eager for major industrial investments at odds with neighbourhoods filled with suspicion and anxiety about what's planned in their backyards.

Stewart said she's been shocked by the speed of EverWind's plan and lack of consultation with the community.

EverWind's chief financial officer said Tuesday the company was unable to start community consultation ahead of the Oct. 16 announcement because of a strict non-disclosure agreement with Anthropic. Information sessions hosted by EverWind are scheduled to begin in the Richmond County area next week.

Stewart, who plans to attend, is also skeptical about how Premier Tim Houston appears to have shifted his views about data centres over the past few months.

"The way this has been rolling out has been very upsetting to people," Stewart said.

On June 18, Houston said it was too soon to turn his government's attention to the matter, and a discussion about data centres would happen "far out into the future."

By late September, he softened that stance, opening a door to consider proposals from the sector in a post on social media.

"Then all of a sudden, we're finding out this is coming to our backyard," Stewart said.

She said her biggest concern is the lack of environmental assessment for the project and a shortage of regulations.Houston told reporters Wednesday it's not clear to him if any new legislation is needed.

Story Continues

"Do we want to do a piece of legislation for every business that sets up in Nova Scotia? Probably not. I mean, we've got to be realistic about these things. We have guidelines. We have approval processes. And they'll be followed," the premier said.

Lora MacEachern, deputy minister of environment and climate change, said Tuesday the data centre doesn't need to go through an environmental assessment and the industrial approval process is sufficient.

AI data centres are not on the list of projects that would automatically trigger an environmental assessment in Nova Scotia. This list includes quarries, mines, energy generation projects and other industrial facilities.

The premier suggested AI data centres do not need to be on the list.

"We have guidelines that have to be met around water usage … around power rates. The government shouldn't care what type of company it is, the government should care that the requirements of the province are met, and we'll make sure that's the case," he said.

Stewart said legally binding regulations could help reassure residents about EverWind's promise to behave responsibly.

"We can't just rely on a company's word. It's not good enough," Stewart said. "What if this project gets built and it's not what was advertised?"

Less than two weeks after EverWind's announcement, a petition to stop the development of AI data centres in Nova Scotia on Change.org garnered more than 24,000 signatures.

Opposition parties have also called for legislated regulations for AI data centres and for an environmental assessment to be conducted.

NDP Leader Claudia Chender said legally binding regulations are essential given the "long history of large industrial projects in Nova Scotia leaving us with … decades of pollution, huge environmental implications, huge social implications."

EverWind's chief financial officer, Matthew Tinari, told a Nova Scotia legislature committee Tuesday that residents deserve clear and factual answers to their questions and the company is committed to addressing concerns.

Tinari said this includes a commitment to prevent rate hikes for residential electricity customers as well as a promise to protect the water supply and minimize water use. He also said the project would follow federal and provincial guidelines for data centres.

"What we've heard from community is (that) industry should have binding commitments. So we were proposing to the province some type of agreement like that," Tinari said.

Houston would not say if his Progressive Conservative government would require a legally binding agreement.

Vaibhav Bahadur, a professor in mechanical engineering at the University of Texas at Austin, specializes in AI data centres in a state that has welcomed massive data centre development. He said "industries don't like regulations, but in my opinion, you do need to balance what industry wants with environmental concerns and obviously concerns from the general public."

The University of Texas maintains a database of all data centres in the state. Bahadur says there are about 300 of them running and another 300 or so in various stages of planning or development.

While America is home to far larger data centres than the one EverWind is proposing in Cape Breton, Bahadur said the 95-megawatt facility is significant.

"There's bound to be concerns when you're talking about that amount of power going into one facility," he said in a recent interview.

EverWind says it will cover the cost of all electricity infrastructure, which is why the project will not increase power rates for customers.

The company also says the project will include a closed-loop cooling system using recycled water as well as an external cooling system that would require the use of additional water on very hot days.

On average, EverWind estimates the system would use an average of 100,000 litres of water per day from the nearby Landrie Lake in the first phase and an average of 500,000 litres per day when the expansion is complete. Tinari said the peak demand represents 1.5 per cent of the lake's estimated sustainable daily yield.

Bahadur said based on the location of the planned data centre in Point Tupper, he expects it would use far less water compared to AI data centres in hotter climates, such as Texas.

Paris Marx, a Newfoundland and Labrador-based tech critic and author of "Hyperscale: The Ambition and Excess of Big Tech's Data Empires," says AI companies have learned from the opposition they've encountered in the United States over the past couple of years and are trying to head off environmental criticism before it starts.

He said companies are aware of concerns around water use, which is why they're pivoting from evaporative cooling systems and constant water use in favour of closed-loop systems.

Marx said those closed-loop systems have only seriously been contemplated over the last six to eight months.

"Right now it sounds good because it's just like, 'Oh, we're using less water,'" Marx said in a recent interview.

"But the companies aren't coming out and being completely transparent about what the trade-offs of that are."

He said the trade-offs could include the need for using substances such as coolants and the need for more energy to keep the system running.

Bahadur said closed-loop cooling systems have been around for a few years in the United States, and they are a more recent development in Canada. He also agreed that closed-loop systems require more electricity than other cooling options and that there could be broader concerns about pollution, which are not limited to data centres.

The electricity required for a closed-loop system will result in additional water use in Nova Scotia, Bahadur said, because the province still relies on fossil fuels like coal to produce electricity.

"My overall opinion is that under cold climate conditions like in Nova Scotia, water use shouldn't be much of a concern, especially if the data centre is using state-of-art technologies, and being responsible — which can require regulations in place," Bahadur said in an email.

Stewart said she's worried about what may happen to Richmond County's environment and water supply if the project is not held to high environmental standards that extend into the future.

"I have a six-year-old daughter, and I have concerns about this down the road. If we don't put proper regulations in place, what is that going to look like for her? … What are the health risks? We don't have enough information," she said.

A recent United Nations University report says the environmental footprint of data centres globally already rivals some of the world's largest countries. The report also predicts their water and energy use and pollution will double in just four years as use of artificial intelligence grows.

# Boon or boondoggle? N.S. weighs $10B data centre's promised economic benefits

Devin Stevens

October 23, 20267 min read

![](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/1bd5619ee6c98318d3b15514f0335e2e0cdb78d338ef9d5a47e7e047342014e1/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fcp_finance_889%2F08ae482ca1e51f0b31f3bd6dc17dd3fe.jpg)

Workers applaud EverWind CEO Trent Vichie's remarks during a funding announcement at EverWind Fuels site in Point Tupper, N.S., on Friday, Oct. 16, 2026. THE CANADIAN PRESS/Steve Wadden - The Canadian Press

HALIFAX — Living more than 200 kilometres from the site of a major artificial intelligence data centre proposal isn't stopping Michael McCluskey from voicing his opposition to the project.

The retired military veteran and training professional from Dartmouth, N.S., has added his name to a Change.org petition that has already collected more than 18,000 signatures since the project was announced last Friday.

Anthropic, an American AI company, plans to lease the Point Tupper, N.S., site for its first Canadian data centre, the first major AI facility to be publicly proposed in Nova Scotia.

However, McCluskey said he just views it as the latest in a long history of Nova Scotia chasing grand economic development projects that never seem to deliver.

He said the site won't be able to serve any other purpose if Anthropic walks away, and he's worried the province will be left holding the bag when economic benefits don't appear.

"They don't put (data centres) anywhere that's prospering," McCluskey said in an interview.

"They don't put them in San Francisco. They don't put them in Los Angeles …. What they're looking for are disadvantaged, economically desperate areas?"

EverWind plans to build the 18,600-square-metre data centre by late 2027 and lease it for 20 years to Anthropic under undisclosed financial terms. San Francisco-based Anthropic says Canada is the sixth-largest worldwide user of its Claude AI model, which is driving the project.

EverWind calls the data centre the biggest private investment in provincial history. It originally planned to build a wind-powered hydrogen plant exporting energy from its Cape Breton marine terminal. The company said it's still pursuing this hydrogen project.

The proposal has thrust Nova Scotia into a debate that has exploded across Canada in recent months as massive AI corporations race to build computing capacity in areas with enough electricity and water to feed the resource-gobbling sector.

The facilities can range in size from a micro data centre about the size of a refrigerator to a hyperscale campus that consumes a level of power comparable to a small city.

A recent York University report found that Canada's existing 194 data centres have a combined capacity to draw 1.6 gigawatts of electricity.More than 14-times that amount — about 22.7 gigawatts — are either under construction or in the announcement stage across 213 sites.

The proposals are supersized. Existing data centres have an average capacity of about 11 megawatts and cover an average of 0.05 square-kilometres, about the size of six Canadian football fields. New proposals average about 122 megawatts spread over more than eight square kilometres, almost 1,000 football fields.

York researchers say a 122-megawatt operation will use the same amount of power annually as 49,000 to 78,000 Canadian homes.

EverWind's data centre will be 95 megawatts in the first phase, and the company has plans for a 325-megawatt expansion by mid-2028.

Paris Marx, a Newfoundland and Labrador-based tech critic and author of "Hyperscale: The Ambition and Excess of Big Tech's Data Empires," said data centre opposition is forming quickly in Canada because communities have seen U.S. headlines about skyrocketing power rates and water consumption over the last 18 to 24 months.

He said if the EverWind project moves ahead, he expects it will follow a global trend where the costs to communities are higher than first projected.

"The benefits just aren't there to the degree that we expect," he said in an interview.

"There are not a lot of jobs that are associated with data centres beyond the construction phase. Obviously, we need to employ people to build them. They don't employ a ton of people to actually run them."

EverWind expects to create 2,000 jobs during construction and 215 permanent jobs between the data centre and the four wind farms that will eventually power it.

The salaries for the permanent jobs would range between $135,000 and $185,000 per year, the company said, with about half of those jobs in the data centre and half in the wind farms.

Sean Mullin, with the University of Toronto's Munk School of Global Affairs and Public Policy, said data centres may not create the same amount of jobs as something like an auto factory, but even a couple hundred positions can make a big difference in a small community.

"We'd rather have that economic activity here than somewhere else. And then that also benefits the country in the sense that we have more compute capacity to serve the rest of the country," he said in an interview.

![](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/02a55a37ff85004bcbe7d47ed3cc60136f6c6d13bce8661f7f01d08b2371024b/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fcp_finance_889%2F747c852842f7bafd2ac7543c27871413.jpg)

Parliamentary secretary Kody Blois speaks during a funding announcement at EverWind Fuels site in Point Tupper, N.S., on Friday, Oct. 16, 2026. THE CANADIAN PRESS/Steve Wadden

Kody Blois, a Nova Scotia MP and parliamentary secretary to the prime minister, said at EverWind's announcement last week that AI is transforming the global economy and Ottawa wants to seize the opportunity.

"These are really important jobs right here in the region. They matter to families, they matter to communities," he said.

EverWind CEO Trent Vichie said there were already more than 20 Nova Scotian contractors working on the site and economic spinoffs will multiply the direct benefits.

"More people in restaurants, more people buying from local businesses," he said last Friday. "It's really providing an anchor to strengthening the community that's already here today."

Marx said he doubts the project will actually proceed, saying data centres are seen as a backup at the moment when other projects, such as hydrogen, fail to gain traction. He noted EverWind has already hit roadblocksa planned hydrogen project in Newfoundland.

EverWind said in an email that it's participating in an auction to sell hydrogen to Europe and that the data centre adds a major industrial use for its renewable energy portfolio and infrastructure while the international hydrogen market develops.

"The data centre helps maintain investment momentum in wind, transmission and shared infrastructure that can also support hydrogen and ammonia over time," the company said.

"While these investments complement each other, they are not dependent on one another."

Anthropic lost more than US$80 billion during the first half of 2026, according to Reuters. That's up from a US$13-billion loss in the first half of 2025. The company is planning an initial public offering of shares as early as November that could value the company at US$1.5 trillion.

The firm declined an interview request this week but has told investors that its plan to spend $518 billion on AI infrastructure over the next decade is needed because computing power is the key constraint on AI development. It has said future demand for AI systems is expected to exceed available supply, according to Reuters.

![IBM Corporation logo.](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/f8c54c253e28973b1354195870337237ad026f98ef196fd2100470d2f46a489c/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fcnw.ca%2F7994525386398455984c6272d3af946b.jpg)

Nanostack is the industry's first known three-dimensional, nanosheet-based design, vertically stacking and staggering transistors to pack more transistors onto a chip, with nearly 100 billion transistors in a space the size of a fingernail at its current 7 angstrom node. The design also unlocks the use of different material combinations within each stacked layer to optimize performance and power efficiency of each transistor independent of the other. These innovations power substantial leaps in energy efficiency, performance, and SRAM scaling.

Earlier this year, IBM demonstrated the viability of the nanostack architecture. Now, additional advances are needed to prepare the technology for manufacturing and production at scale.

Through this collaboration, IBM and Siemens will work to advance the design, simulation, and lithography techniques needed for this and future nanostack generations beyond the current 7 angstrom node. The collaboration includes developing advanced virtual models of nanostack in order to model performance, optimize designs, and address fabrication challenges before chips are built.

"Nanostack is a scalable transistor platform designed to shift device scaling from 2D to 3D, representing a new paradigm for the semiconductor industry and a path forward for the logic scaling needed to support AI and other emerging technologies over the coming decade," said **Huiming Bu, Vice President of Silicon Technology R&D, IBM**. "We are thrilled to work with Siemens as we continue to advance this technology and explore new approaches to extend angstrom-level scaling with nanostack."

"Scaling semiconductors into the angstrom era demands tightly coupled co-optimization between device physics, EDA design and manufacturing software, and manufacturing. As physical AI places extraordinary demands on silicon architecture, IBM's 3D nanostack design represents a pivotal leap forward," said **Ankur Gupta, Executive Vice President, EDA IC Portfolio, Siemens**. "Siemens' collaboration with IBM to advance virtual modeling, simulation, lithography, and design software optimizations needed to address physical limitations early and accelerate the development of these complex architectures is truly pioneering."

# Data Centres Need Power, but Higher Rates Change the Math: I’d Watch This TSX Stock

![](https://s.yimg.com/lo/mysterio/api/84aacee05452c52107971f203d6abd258dc2c8c78756de9a1f1a8476907d154a/lightyear_networkapi/resizefit_w960%3Bquality_80%3Bformat_webp/https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.zenfs.com%2Fen%2Fthe_motley_fool_261%2F2d392a540b8d9abf0b29e48b4b3eda1f.jpg)

Written by [Amy Legate-Wolfe](https://www.fool.ca/author/alegatewolfe/) at The Motley Fool Canada

Artificial intelligence (AI) may live in the cloud, but its electricity bill is very much on the ground.

The International Energy Agency expects global data-centre electricity consumption to roughly double to 945 terawatt-hours (TWh) by 2030. From 2024 through 2030, demand could grow about 15% annually, more than four times faster than electricity consumption from everything else.

That sounds like a gift for power companies, but there's one catch. Somebody has to pay for all those generators, transmission lines, substations, and cooling systems. Borrowing that money just became more expensive.

## **Power meets** costs

The Bank of Canada's overnight rate remains 2.25%, yet longer-term borrowing costs have climbed as global bond markets sell off. The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield briefly hit 5.3% on October 1, its highest level since 2002. That changes the data-centre investment calculation.

A proposed power project might look fantastic with cheap debt. Raise the financing cost, and the same project needs better contracts, higher electricity prices or stronger returns to justify construction.

Investors therefore shouldn't simply buy every company promising exposure to AI electricity demand. I'd favour businesses that can turn existing generation into contracted cash flow before spending billions on speculative capacity. That brings **Capital Power** ( [TSX: CPX](https://www.fool.ca/company/tsx-cpx-capital-power/342813/)) onto my watchlist.

## **AI finds Alberta**

Capital Power owns natural-gas, renewable and energy-storage generation across Canada and the United States. Its portfolio increasingly provides the reliable, dispatchable electricity that enormous data centres need when the sun isn't shining and the wind has decided to take the afternoon off.

The most important development came in July. Capital Power signed a greater-than-10-year energy-supply agreement with **Meta** **Platforms** for 250 megawatts (MW) of power supporting its new Sturgeon County, Alta., data centre. The load should begin during the second half of 2028.

Here's the attractive part. The agreement is backed by Capital Power's existing Alberta generation portfolio. Rather than betting entirely on a giant new build, Capital Power is converting current assets into longer-term contracted revenue. Higher financing costs make that distinction increasingly useful.

## **Cash flow** grows

The broader business is already expanding. Second-quarter adjusted funds from operations (AFFO), a useful measure of cash available from the business, climbed to $328 million from $235 million a year earlier. Adjusted EBITDA increased to $351 million from $322 million.

Story Continues

Some of that growth came from the Hummel Station and Rolling Hills plants acquired in 2025, which expanded Capital Power's U.S. natural-gas portfolio. Plus, shareholders are getting paid along the way. Capital Power raised its dividend another 2% this summer, its 13th consecutive annual increase. At the October 1 close of $61.43, the new $0.70 quarterly dividend provides a yield of roughly 4.6%.

## **Considerations**

Higher rates remain the obvious complication. Capital Power spent about $3 billion buying Hummel and Rolling Hills last year, and Q2 finance expenses increased as the company carried acquisition debt. Continued expansion will require careful capital allocation if borrowing costs remain elevated.

Natural-gas generation also brings commodity, emissions and regulatory risks. Meanwhile, the Meta contract doesn't begin contributing until 2028, so investors are still paying today for some growth arriving tomorrow.

That's why I'd watch CPX rather than treat AI power demand as permission to chase it. Anyone [buying stocks in Canada](https://www.fool.ca/investing/how-to-buy-stocks-in-canada/) still needs the numbers to work after the excitement wears off.
