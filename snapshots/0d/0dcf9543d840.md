# Pneumatic tube mail in New York City

Pneumatic tube mail in New York City

Pneumatic tube messaging system

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Pneumatic-tube-mail.png/500px-Pneumatic-tube-mail.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pneumatic-tube-mail.png) Terminals of the Tube[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Pneumatic-tube-mail-apparatus.png/500px-Pneumatic-tube-mail-apparatus.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pneumatic-tube-mail-apparatus.png) Receiving and Sending Apparatus in the Sub-Postoffice

The **pneumatic tube mail** was a postal system operating in New York City from 1897 to 1953 using [pneumatic tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatic_tubes "Pneumatic tubes"). Similar systems had arisen in the mid-19th century in London, via the [London Pneumatic Despatch Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Pneumatic_Despatch_Company "London Pneumatic Despatch Company"); in [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester "Manchester") and other British cities; and in Paris via the [Paris pneumatic post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_pneumatic_post "Paris pneumatic post"). Following the creation of the first American pneumatic mail system in Philadelphia in 1893, New York City's system was begun, initially only between the old [General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Hall_Post_Office_and_Courthouse_(New_York_City) "City Hall Post Office and Courthouse (New York City)") on [Park Row](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Row_(Manhattan) "Park Row (Manhattan)") and the [Produce Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Produce_Exchange "New York Produce Exchange") on [Bowling Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Green_(New_York_City) "Bowling Green (New York City)"), a distance of 3,750 feet (1,140m).[&#8202;](#ref-back-Untapped-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-Untapped)[&#8202;](#ref-back-US_Congress-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-US_Congress)

Eventually the network stretched up both sides of [Manhattan Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Island "Manhattan Island") all the way to [Manhattanville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattanville "Manhattanville") on the West Side and "Triborough" in [East Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Harlem "East Harlem"), forming a loop running a few feet below street level. Travel time from the General Post Office to Harlem was 20 minutes. A crosstown line connected the two parallel lines between the [new General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Farley_Post_Office "James Farley Post Office") on the West Side and [Grand Central Terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Central_Terminal "Grand Central Terminal") on the east, and took four minutes for mail to traverse. Using the [Brooklyn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Bridge "Brooklyn Bridge"), a spur line also ran from [Church Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Street_(Manhattan) "Church Street (Manhattan)") in [lower Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Manhattan "Lower Manhattan") to the [General Post Office in Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Building_and_Post_Office_(Brooklyn) "Federal Building and Post Office (Brooklyn)"), taking four minutes.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Untapped-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-Untapped) Operators of the system were called "Rocketeers".[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3)

Though 10 cities were funded for pneumatic mail, the New York operation was the most developed. By 1907 contracts were issued in five other cities (Boston, Brooklyn, Chicago, Philadelphia, St. Louis), but not in four cities (Baltimore, Cincinnati, Kansas City, San Francisco).[&#8202;](#ref-back-US_Congress-1)[\[2\]](#refnote-US_Congress) The system was discontinued in 1953.

## Inauguration

The system was inaugurated on 7 October 1897 and presided over by Senator [Chauncey M. Depew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauncey_M._Depew "Chauncey M. Depew"), who declared,

> This is the age of speed. Everything that makes for speed contributes to happiness and is a distinct gain to civilization. We are ahead of the old countries in almost every respect, but we have been behind in methods of communication within our cities. In New York this condition of communication has hitherto been barbarous. If the [Greater New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Greater_New_York "City of Greater New York") is to be a success, quick communication is absolutely necessary. I hope this system we have seen tried here to-day will soon be extended over all the Greater New York."[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYTimes-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-NYTimes)

The first dispatch was sent by Depew from the [General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Hall_Post_Office_and_Courthouse_(New_York_City) "City Hall Post Office and Courthouse (New York City)") to the Produce Exchange Post Office and included a Bible wrapped in an American flag, a copy of [the Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution "United States Constitution"), a copy of President [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley "William McKinley")'s inaugural speech and several other papers. The Bible was included in order to reference [Job](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Job "Book of Job") 9:25, "Now my days are swifter than a post" ( _[KJV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version "King James Version")_).[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYTimes-1)[\[4\]](#refnote-NYTimes) The return delivery contained a bouquet of [violets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet_(plant) "Violet (plant)") and, as reported the following day in _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_, the round trip took less than three minutes, most of which was taken in unloading and reloading the canister at the other end.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYTimes-2)[\[4\]](#refnote-NYTimes) Subsequent deliveries included a variety of amusing items including a large artificial peach (a reference to Depew's nickname), clothing, a candlestick and a live black [cat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat "Cat").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Untapped-2)[\[1\]](#refnote-Untapped)[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYTimes-3)[\[4\]](#refnote-NYTimes)[&#8202;](#ref-back-The_French_Hatching_Cat-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-The_French_Hatching_Cat) In his autobiography, postal supervisor Howard Wallace Connelly recalled,

> How it could live after being shot at terrific speed from Station P in the Produce Exchange Building, making several turns before reaching Broadway and Park Row, I cannot conceive, but it did. It seemed to be dazed for a minute or two but started to run and was quickly secured and placed in a basket that had been provided for that purpose. A suit of clothes was the third arrival and then came letters, papers, and other ordinary mail matter.[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-6)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Stuff-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-Stuff)

The installation in the Borough of Manhattan was constructed by the Tubular Dispatch Company. This company was purchased by the New York Pneumatic Service Company, who continued to operate the tubes under contract to the postal service. Construction after 1902, starting with the line between the New York and the Brooklyn general post offices, was completed by the New York Mail and Newspaper Transportation Company. Stock in these companies was owned entirely by the American Pneumatic Service Company.[&#8202;](#ref-back-60thCongress-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-60thCongress)

## Issues

Government studies later argued the mail could be handled at less cost and more expeditiously by other means. The growing volume of mail, limited system capacities, and the advent of the automobile made the tubes "practically obsolete," and actually hindered the efficient operation of the postal service. The pneumatic tube companies vehemently attacked the studies, but subsequent investigation identified that any errors actually favored the tubes. It was further argued that, if the tubes were truly successful, they would have seen wider adoption by the commercial industry.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Veto-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-Veto)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Postmaster-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-Postmaster)

Breakdowns often required digging up streets to retrieve clogged mail and restore the system to operation. At least one death was reported when a test run caused a tube to rupture violently, injuring the repair crew who had dug out a section under 4th Avenue to mend a break in the line.[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)

## Closure

The service was suspended during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") to conserve funding for the war effort.[&#8202;](#ref-back-National_Postal_Museum-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-National_Postal_Museum) The annual rental payment of $17,000 per mile was considered exorbitant, particularly when compared to the cost of delivery by automobile.[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13)

The Brooklyn section alone cost $14,000 ($529,144in 2024) in rent per year and $6,200 ($234,335in 2024) in labor.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Pope-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-Pope) After successful lobbying by contractors the service was restored in 1922. Service was again halted between Brooklyn and Manhattan in April 1950 for repairs on the Brooklyn side and was never restored. In 1953 service was halted for the rest of the system, pending review, and was not restored.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Pope-1)[\[14\]](#refnote-Pope)

## Statistics

Each canister could hold 600 letters and would travel up to 35 miles per hour.[&#8202;](#ref-back-National_Postal_Museum-1)[\[12\]](#refnote-National_Postal_Museum) At the peak, the system carried 95,000 letters a day, representing 30% of all mail in the city.[&#8202;](#ref-back-The_French_Hatching_Cat-1)[\[5\]](#refnote-The_French_Hatching_Cat) The total system comprised 27 miles (43km) of tubes, connecting 23 post offices.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Untapped-3)[\[1\]](#refnote-Untapped) The canisters used were 25-pound steel cylinders that were either 21 inches long and 7 inches in diameter[&#8202;](#ref-back-The_French_Hatching_Cat-2)[\[5\]](#refnote-The_French_Hatching_Cat) or 24 inches long and 8 inches in diameter.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Untapped-4)[\[1\]](#refnote-Untapped)
