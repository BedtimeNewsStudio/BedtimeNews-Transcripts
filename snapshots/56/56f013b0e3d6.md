# 2025 Kingdom of Denmark Investment Climate Statement

## Executive Summary

The Kingdom of Denmark includes Denmark, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands. Under the Kingdom of Denmark’s constitutional framework, the government of Denmark retains authority over foreign, defense, and security policy for Greenland and the Faroe Islands, but Copenhagen has increasingly consulted with them on these issues, including through a regularly occurring “Contact Committee” among the three prime ministers. The Faroe Islands and Greenland have the exclusive competence for most economic and trade related matters, including but not limited to, taxes, duties, supplies, production, distribution, price controls, import and export controls, the labor market, natural resource management (including fisheries and minerals), energy policy, health, and education.

Many independent observers regard Denmark as one of the world’s most attractive business environments and the country ranks highly in indices measuring political, economic, and regulatory stability. Denmark is a member of the European Union (EU); Greenland and the Faroe Islands are not. Greenland, however, is an Overseas Country and Territory (OCT) associated with the EU after having withdrawn from the European Community in 1985. Danish legislation and regulations conform to EU standards on virtually all issues. It maintains a fixed exchange rate policy, with the Danish krone pegged to the euro since 1999. Denmark is a social welfare state with a thoroughly modern market economy heavily driven by trade in goods and services. Given that exports accounted for about 70 percent of GDP in 2024, the economic conditions of its major trading partners – the United States, Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands, Norway, and the United Kingdom – have a substantial impact on Danish national accounts, as do the trade policies of the United States and other non-EU major trading members.

Denmark is a world leader in “green technology” industries, such as offshore wind and energy efficiency, and in sectors such as shipping, pharmaceuticals, and life sciences. Denmark is a net exporter of food. Its manufacturing sector depends on raw material imports. Within the EU,

Denmark is among the strongest supporters of liberal trade policy. Transparency International regularly ranks Denmark as the least corrupt country in the world. Transport and communications infrastructures are efficient.

The Danish economy has surpassed its pre-pandemic levels, with the IMF noting a robust expansion in the economy driven by an exceptional surge in the pharmaceutical sector. Denmark recorded a real GDP growth of 2.9 percent in 2025 compared to the EU average of 1.2 percent, according to the IMF. Denmark's underlying macroeconomic conditions are balanced and the investment climate is sound, while companies across sectors cite labor shortages as a key challenge. Legislation establishing a foreign investment screening mechanism to prevent threats to national security and public order came into effect in Denmark on July 1, 2021. The mechanism requires mandatory notification for investments in the following five sectors: defense, IT security and processing of classified information, companies producing dual-use items, critical technology, and critical infrastructure. ## Section 1. Openness to, and Restrictions upon, Foreign Investment

### Policies toward Foreign Direct Investment

As a small country with an open economy, Denmark is highly dependent on foreign trade and investment. Exports comprise the most significant component (70 percent) of GDP in 2024, with the pharmaceutical sector and maritime transport as important drivers. The Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) ranks Denmark second globally and first in Western Europe in its latest Business Environment Rankings. The EIU characterizes Denmark’s business environment as

reflecting solid macroeconomic fundamentals, a stable political and regulatory environment, fiscal space, a friendly policy toward private enterprise and foreign investment, a skilled labor force, a strong innovation capacity, and a well-developed digital and transport infrastructure. Principal concerns include a relatively high personal tax burden, a constrained supply of skilled labor, potential financial risks in the banking sector due to leveraged real estate markets in the Nordic-Baltic region, a fairly high private-sector indebtedness, and obstacles to competition in parts of domestic-oriented services sectors. Overall, however, operating conditions for companies are broadly favorable. Denmark ranks highly in multiple categories, including its political and institutional environment, macroeconomic stability, foreign trade, exchange controls, foreign investment policy, private enterprise policy, and infrastructure.

As of February 2025, the EIU rated Denmark seventh on its 2024 Democracy Index. Denmark has an AAA rating from Standard & Poor’s, Moody’s, Fitch Group, DBRS Rating, and Scope Ratings. <u>Invest in Denmark</u>, an agency of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and part of the Danish Trade Council, provides detailed information to potential investors. It released a new strategy for investment promotion in June 2024, which lays the framework for attracting, retaining, and developing foreign investments. The strategy focuses on attracting foreign investments that contribute to solving societal challenges and creating good jobs to the benefit of the Danish economy. It also notes four priority sectors: clean tech, tech, life sciences, and food.

### Limits on Foreign Control and Right to Private Ownership and Establishment

As an EU member state, Denmark is bound by EU rules on the free movement of goods, capital, persons, and certain services. Denmark welcomes foreign investment and does not discriminate between EU and other investors.

Denmark’s central and regional governments actively encourage foreign investment on a national-treatment basis, with relatively few foreign control limits and no reported bias against foreign companies from municipal or national authorities when compared to domestic investors. A foreign investment screening mechanism came into force in July 2021 to prevent threats to Danish national security and public order from foreign direct investment, but aside from the screening mechanism there are no additional permits required by foreign investors.

The mechanism requires mandatory notification for five high-risk sectors and allows for voluntary notification for all sectors. The sectors requiring mandatory notification are defense, IT security and processing of classified information, companies producing dual-use items, critical technology, and critical infrastructure. In addition to investments within these sensitive sectors, a foreign investor must apply for pre-approval from the Danish Business Authority (DBA) (mandatory notification) if the investment acquires a “qualified share” of a Danish company, which is defined as 10 percent ownership, voting rights, or control. Voluntary notification can be made for investments where the company reaches 25 percent ownership or control and where the investment may pose a threat to national security or public order. A pre-screening process exists to determine if the investment is in the critical technology or critical infrastructure sector, followed by a two-phased administrative procedure. In the first phase, the authorities have 45 calendar days to process the case. If the authority finds that a transaction may pose a threat to national security or public order, the authorities initiate an in- depth phase two examination that entails a new 125-calendar-day review period. Notification and guidance all take place online, handled by the <u>Danish Business Authority (DBA)</u>.

The DBA released an overview detailing the processing of cases from July 1, 2021, to June 30,

2023. During this period, the DBA handled a total of 358 cases. These included 231 mandatory applications for authorization to carry out a foreign investment or special financial agreement, 10 voluntary notifications, and 117 requests for pre-screening. The DBA and the Minister of Industry, Business and Financial Affairs have only intervened in two cases. In one case, approval was granted under specific agreed conditions. In the other case, the investment was denied initially but subsequently approved by the Danish authorities. Therefore, the latest data indicate that no foreign investment has been banned under the Danish screening regime. A foreign or domestic private entity may freely establish, own, and dispose of a business enterprise in Denmark. The capital requirement for establishing a corporation (Aktieselskab A/S) or Limited Partnership (Partnerselskab P/S) is DKK 400,000 (approx. $60,660) and for establishing a private limited liability company (Anpartsselskab ApS) it is DKK 40,000 (approx. $6,056). In 2025, the government lowered the capital requirements to set up a private limited liability company, making it easier for entrepreneurs to launch businesses and bringing Denmark more in line with other Nordic countries. No restrictions apply regarding the residency of directors and managers.

Since October 2004, any private entity may establish a European public limited company (SE company) in Denmark. The legal framework of an SE company is subject to Danish corporate law, but it is possible to change the nationality of the company without liquidation and re-founding. An SE company must be registered at the Danish Business Authority if its official address is in Denmark. The minimum capital requirement is €120,000 (approx. $136,200).

Danish professional certification and/or local Danish experience are required to provide professional services in Denmark. In some instances, Denmark may accept equivalent professional certification from other EU or Nordic countries on a reciprocal basis. EU-wide residency requirements apply to the provision of legal and accountancy services.

In addition to investment screening cases, ownership restrictions apply to the following sectors:

- **Oil and Gas**: Requires 20 percent Danish government participation on a "non-carried interest" basis.
- **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)**: Requires 20 percent Danish government participation on a "non-carried interest" basis for storage.
- **Offshore Wind**: As of May 2023, the framework conditions for offshore wind tenders require 20 percent government co-ownership.
- **Maritime Services**: There are foreign (non-EU resident) ownership restrictions on Danish- flagged vessels other than those owned by an enterprise incorporated in Denmark. Ships owned by Danish citizens, Danish partnerships, or Danish limited liability companies are eligible for registration in the Danish International Ships Register (DIS). Vessels owned by EU or European Economic Area (EEA) entities with a genuine, demonstrable link to Denmark are also eligible for registration. Foreign companies with a significant Danish interest can register a ship in the DIS.
- **Civil Aviation**: For an airline to be established in Denmark, it must have majority ownership and be effectively controlled by an EU state or a national of an EU state, unless otherwise provided for through an international agreement to which the EU is a signatory.

- **Financial Services**: Non-resident financial institutions may engage in securities trading on the Copenhagen Stock Exchange only through subsidiaries incorporated in Denmark.
- **Real Estate**: Ownership of holiday homes, also known as summer houses, is restricted to Danish citizens. Such homes are generally located along the Danish coastline and may not be used as full-year residences. On a case-by-case basis, the Ministry of Justice may waive the citizenship requirement for those with close familial, linguistic, cultural, or other close connections to Denmark or the specific property. In general, EU and EEA citizens may purchase full-year residential property or real estate that supports self-employment without obtaining prior authorization from the Ministry of Justice. Companies domiciled in an EU or an EEA Member State that have set up or will set up subsidiaries or agencies or will provide services in Denmark may, in general, also purchase real property in Denmark without prior authorization. Non-EU/EEA citizens must obtain authorization from the Ministry of Justice to purchase real estate in Denmark, which is generally granted to those with permanent residence in Denmark or who have lived in Denmark for a consecutive period of five years.
### Other Investment Policy Reviews

- There is no specific mention of Denmark in the latest WTO Trade Policy Review of the European Union from April 2023.
- The EU Commission publishes <u>European Semester documents for Denmark</u>.

### Business Facilitation

The Danish Business Authority (DBA) is responsible for business registrations in Denmark. As a part of the DBA, "Business in Denmark" provides information on relevant Danish rules and online

registrations to foreign companies in English. The <u>Danish business registration website, Virk</u>**,** is the principal digital tool for licensing and registering companies in Denmark and offers a business registration process that is clear and complete.

Registration of sole proprietorships and partnerships is free of charge. For other types of businesses, online registration costs DKK 670 (approx. $102). Registration by non-digital means costs DKK 2,150 (approx. $327).

The process for establishing a new business is distinct from that of registration. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs' "Invest in Denmark" program provides a <u>step-by-step guide to establishing a</u> <u>business</u>, along with <u>other relevant resources</u>. The services are free of charge and available to all investors, regardless of country of origin.

The processing time for establishing a new business varies depending on the chosen business entity. Establishing a Danish private limited liability company (ApS), for example, generally takes four to six weeks for a standard application. Establishing a sole proprietorship (Enkeltmandsvirksomhed) is more straightforward, with processing generally taking about one week.

Those providing temporary services in Denmark must provide their company details to the Registry of Foreign Service Providers (RUT). The <u>Danish business registration website, Virk</u>, provides English guidance on registering a service with RUT. A public digital signature, referred to as a MitID, is required for those wishing to register a foreign company in Denmark. A CPR number (a 10-digit personal identification number) and valid identification are needed to obtain a MitID. Danish citizenship is not a requirement.

Under Denmark's Årsregnskabslov (Financial Statements Act), a small enterprise is defined using a three-pronged test over two consecutive years. A company classified as “small” meets at least two of these criteria: fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees, annual net revenue under DKK 89 million (approx. $13.5 million), or a balance sheet total under DKK 44 million (approx. $6.7 million). Medium-sized enterprises are those that do not exceed 250 employees. Limits on annual revenue or the yearly balance sheet total are DKK 313 million (approx. $47.6 million) or DKK 156 million (approx. $23.7 million).

Denmark is a signatory to the WTO Investment Facilitation for Development Agreement.

### Outward Investment

Danish companies are not restricted from investing abroad, and Danish outward investment has exceeded inward investment for more than a decade. Several large Danish companies have significant operations abroad, including A.P. Moller-Maersk, Coloplast, DSV, LEGO, Novo Nordisk, Novozymes, Ørsted, Pandora, and Vestas. The United States is the country in which Denmark invests the most. Danish investments in the United States increased over 225 percent between 2013 and 2023, at which point Danish direct investment reached nearly $40 billion (DKK 273.7 billion). In the third quarter of 2024, Danish investments in the United States were equivalent to 94 percent of Danish GDP while United States investments in Denmark that same period were equivalent to 74 percent of Danish GNP, according to a March 2025 report from the Central Bank and Statistics Denmark.

The Trade Council, a part of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, assists Danish and international companies with export and investment promotion services. Denmark’s Export and Investment Fund (EIFO), which was established in January 2023 through a merger of three state funds (Vaekstfonden, EKF Denmark’s Export Credit Agency, and the Danish Green Investment Fund), provides a single point of access for all Danish companies in need of risk-tolerant government co-financing, both for capital to be used domestically or abroad.

## Section 2. Bilateral Investment and Taxation Treaties

The United States and Denmark have had a Friendship, Commerce, and Navigation Treaty since 1961 that, among other things, ensures National Treatment, Most-Favored Nation status, transparency of the regulatory process, and competitive equality with state-owned enterprises. According to UNCTAD, Denmark has <u>39 bilateral investment treaties (BITs) signed and in force</u>.

There has been little change to the status of Denmark’s pre-existing bilateral investment treaties since the EU’s Lisbon Treaty came into force in 2009, which transferred competence for foreign direct investment to the EU, but some older BITs remain in force under grandfathering and transitional arrangements.

## Section 3. Legal Regime

### Transparency of the Regulatory System

Denmark's judicial system is highly regarded and considered fair. Its legal system is independent of the government's legislative branch and includes written and consistently applied commercial and bankruptcy laws. Secured interests in property are recognized and enforced. The World Justice Project (WJP) Rule of Law Index rates Denmark first among 142 countries.

To facilitate business administration, Denmark maintains two "legislative days" per year— January 1 and July 1—as the only days when new laws and regulations affecting the business sector can come into effect. Danish laws and policies granting national treatment to foreign investments are designed to increase FDI in Denmark. Denmark consistently applies high standards to health, environment, safety, and labor laws. Danish corporate law is generally in conformity with current EU legislation. The legal, regulatory, and accounting systems are relatively transparent and follow international standards.

Bureaucratic procedures are streamlined and transparent; proposed laws and regulations are published in draft form for public comment. Public finances and debt obligations are transparent.

The government uses transparent policies and effective laws to foster competition and establish clear rules of the game, consistent with international norms and applicable equally to Danish and foreign entities. The Danish Competition and Consumer Authority (CCA) works for well-functioning markets and consumer welfare. The CCA is a government agency under the Ministry of Industry, Business, and Financial Affairs. It enforces the Danish Competition Act. This Act, along with Danish consumer legislation, aims to promote efficient resource allocation in society, promote efficient competition, create a level playing field for enterprises, and protect consumers.

Corporate tax records of all companies, associations, and foundations that pay taxes in Denmark are published by the tax authorities according to public law since December 2012 and

are updated annually. The corporate tax rate is 22 percent. Greenland and the Faroe Islands retain autonomy for their respective tax policies.

Publicly listed companies in Denmark must adhere to the Danish Financial Statements Act when preparing their annual reports. The accounting principles are International Accounting Standards (IAS), International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), and Danish Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). Financial statements must be prepared annually. The Danish Financial Statements Act covers all businesses.

Private limited companies, public limited companies, and corporate funds are obliged to prepare financial statements under accounting classes determined by company size. There are four different accounting classes: A, B, C and D. Accounting class B is further divided into micro and standard B, and C is divided into medium-sized and large C. The smallest companies belong to accounting class A, while the largest belong to accounting class D. The classification is based on the assessed size of the company based on two out of three parameters (net revenue, balance sheet, and number of employees):

- **Personal companies as well as companies with limited liability (class A):** Fewer than an annual average of 10 full-time employees and total assets not exceeding DKK 7 million (approx. $1.2 million) or net revenue not exceeding DKK 14 million (approx. $2.3 million) during the fiscal year. According to the Danish Financial Statements Act, personally owned businesses, personally owned general partnerships (multiple owners), and general funds are characterized as class A; there is no requirement to prepare financial statements unless the owner voluntarily chooses to do so.
- **Private limited liability companies, commercial funds, and companies with limited liability** **(class B):** o Micro businesses (class micro B): Fewer than an annual average of 10 full-time employees and total assets not exceeding DKK 2.7 million (approx. $411,000) or net revenue not exceeding DKK 5.4 million (approx. $822,000) during the fiscal year.

o Small businesses (class B): Fewer than an annual average of 50 full-time employees and total assets not exceeding DKK 44 million (approx. $6.7 million) or net revenue not exceeding DKK 89 million (approx. $13.5 million) during the fiscal year.

- **Medium-sized enterprises (class C medium):** Fewer than an annual average of 250 full-time employees and total assets not exceeding DKK 156 million (approx. $23.7 million) or net revenue not exceeding DKK 313 million (approx. $47.6 million) during the fiscal year.
- **Large companies (class C large):** Companies that are neither small nor medium companies, have an annual average of at least 250 full-time employees, total assets in excess of DKK 156 million (approx. $23.7 million), or net revenue in excess of DKK 313 million (approx. $47.6 million) during the fiscal year, but are not a class D company.
- **Accounting class D:** Publicly traded companies and certain state-owned joint-stock companies. <u>All government draft proposed regulations are published at Høringsportalen</u> and are available for comment from interested parties. Following the comment period, the government may revise draft regulations before publication on the <u>Danish parliament's website</u>. Final regulations are published on that site and on the <u>official gazette of Denmark</u>. All ministries and agencies are required to publish proposed regulations. According to the World Bank’s <u>2019</u> <u>Global Indicators of Regulatory Governance</u>, Denmark has a composite score of 4.75 out of 5.
### International Regulatory Considerations

Denmark is a member of the European Union and is an active supporter of the internal market. As a result, many aspects of business regulation are dictated by the EU and hence aligned with other EU Member States.

Denmark adheres to the WTO Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures (TRIMs); no inconsistencies have been reported.

### Legal System and Judicial Independence

Denmark's decision-making power is divided into the legislative, executive, and judicial branches. The principles of separation of power and an independent judiciary help ensure democracy and Danish citizens' legal rights. The district courts, the high courts, and the Supreme Court represent the Danish legal system's three basic levels. The <u>Danish legal system</u> also comprises other institutions with special functions (e.g., the Maritime and Commercial Court).

### Laws and Regulations on Foreign Direct Investment

As an EU member state, Denmark is bound by EU rules on the free movement of goods, capital, persons, and certain services. <u>Invest in Denmark</u> provides detailed information to potential investors.

### Competition and Antitrust Laws

The Danish Competition and Consumer Authority (CCA) reviews transactions for competition-related concerns. A merger or takeover is subject to approval by the CCA. Large-scale mergers also require approval from EU competition authorities. According to the Danish Competition Act, the CCA requires notification of mergers and takeovers if the aggregate annual revenue in Denmark of all undertakings involved is more than DKK 900 million (approx. $137 million) and the aggregate yearly revenue in Denmark of each of at least two of the undertakings concerned is more than DKK 100 million (approx. $15.2 million), or if the aggregate annual revenue in Denmark of at least one of the undertakings involved is more than DKK 3.8 billion (approx. $579 million) and the aggregate yearly worldwide revenue of at least one of the other undertakings concerned is more than DKK 3.8 billion (approx. $579 million). When a merger results from the acquisition of parts of one or more undertakings, the calculation of the revenue referred to shall only comprise the share of the revenue of the seller or sellers that relate to the assets acquired. Even mergers below the traditional thresholds now may be called in by the Danish Competition and Consumer Authority (DCCA) if the combined turnover in Denmark is at least DKK 50 million (approx. $7.6 million), and the DCCA assesses a significant risk to competition. Merger control provisions are contained in Part 4 of the Danish Competition Act and further

elaborated in the Executive Order on the Notification of Mergers. Revenue is calculated under the Executive Order on the Calculation of Turnover in the Competition Act.

A full notification of a merger must include the information and documents specified in the full notification form, <u>Annex 1 – Information for Full Notification of Mergers</u>. A simplified notification of a merger must include the information and documents specified in the simplified notification form, <u>Annex 2 – Information for Simplified Notification of Mergers</u>. The fee for a simplified notification amounts to DKK 50,000 (approx. $7,612). The fee for a full notification amounts to 0.015 percent of the aggregate annual turnover in Denmark of the undertakings involved; this fee is capped at DKK 1,500,000 (approx. $228,400).

Additional information concerning notification of mergers is available in the <u>Guidelines to the</u> <u>Executive Order on Notification of Mergers and on Merger Fees</u>. More general information on <u>Danish merger control can be found in the Merger Guidelines</u> (currently under revision).

### Expropriation and Compensation

By law, private property can only be expropriated for public purposes, in a non-discriminatory manner, with reasonable compensation, and under established principles of international law. There have been no recent expropriations of significance in Denmark.

### Dispute Settlement

#### ICSID Convention and New York Convention

There have been no significant investment disputes in Denmark in recent years. Denmark has been a member of the World Bank-based International Center for the Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) since 1968 and extended the application of the Convention to the Faroe Islands as of 1969. Denmark is a party to the 1958 (New York) UN Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, meaning local courts must enforce international arbitration awards that meet specific criteria. Subsequent Danish legislation makes international arbitration of investment disputes binding in Denmark.

#### Investor-State Dispute Settlement

There have been no significant investment disputes in Denmark in recent years.

#### International Commercial Arbitration and Foreign Courts

Denmark is a party to the 1961 European Convention on International Commercial Arbitration and to the 1962 Agreement relating to the application of this Convention. Denmark adopted the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration in 1985.

### Bankruptcy/Insolvency Regulations

Monetary judgments under the bankruptcy law are made in freely convertible Danish kroner. The bankruptcy law addresses creditors’ claims in the following order:

(1) costs and debt accrued during the treatment of the bankruptcy;
(2) costs, including the court tax, relating to attempts to find a solution other than bankruptcy;
(3) wage claims and holiday pay;
(4) excise taxes owed to the government; and
(5) all other claims
## Section 4. Industrial Policies

### Investment Incentives

Performance incentives are available to both foreign and domestic investors. Examples include grants or preferential financing in designated regional development areas. Foreign subsidiaries located in Denmark can participate in government-financed or subsidized research programs on a national-treatment basis.

Denmark is recognized as a global leader in green and renewable energy. The government provides a multitude of support programs to private households and companies for energy efficiency renovations. In May 2025, the Ministry of Climate, Energy, and Utilities announced Denmark would launch offshore wind tenders with a capacity of three gigawatts (GW), with the government providing support that could reach DKK 55.2 billion (approx. $8.3 billion) over 20 years through a “contract for difference” model to ensure a stable price for electricity produced. Similarly, several programs exist for maturation and tech commercialization of green technologies. In December 2021, the Danish parliament reached a political framework agreement for a total of DKK 16 billion (approx. $2.4 billion) support for carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) between 2022 and 2030. The Energy Technology Development and Demonstration Program (EUDP) supports private companies and universities to develop and demonstrate new energy technologies, in support of Denmark’s goal of a 70 percent carbon reduction by 2030 and climate neutrality by 2050. The <u>EUDP has contributed DKK 6</u> <u>billion (approx. $913 million) to more than 1,200 projects</u> since its inception in 2007.

### Foreign Trade Zones/Free Ports/Trade Facilitation

The only officially designated free zone in Denmark is the Copenhagen Free Port area, which retains certain customs-free facilities under Danish law, though its significance has declined since Denmark’s integration into the EU Single Market. The Port of Copenhagen and the Port of Malmö (Sweden) merged their commercial operations in 2001, including the free port activities, in a joint company named CMP. CMP is one of the largest port and terminal operators in the Nordic Region and one of the largest Northern European cruise ship ports; it occupies a key position in the Baltic Sea region for the distribution of cars and transit of oil. The facilities in the Free Port are mainly used for tax-free warehousing of imported goods, for exports, and for in- transit trade. Customs and duties are not payable until cargo leaves the Free Port for release into the EU market. The processing of cargo and the preparation and finishing of imported automobiles for sale can freely be set up in the Free Port. Manufacturing operations can be established with permission of the customs authorities, which is granted if special reasons exist for having the facility in the Free Port area. The Copenhagen Free Port welcomes foreign companies establishing warehouse and storage facilities.

### Performance and Data Localization Requirements

Danish law mandates performance requirements in connection with investments in hydrocarbon exploration, where concession terms typically require a fixed work program, including seismic surveys, and in some cases, exploratory drilling, consistent with applicable EU directives. Performance requirements are primarily designed to protect the environment, mainly by encouraging reduced energy and water use. Several environmental and energy requirements are universally applied to households as well as businesses in Denmark, both foreign and domestic. For instance, Denmark was the first of the EU countries, in January 1993, to introduce a carbon dioxide (CO2) tax on business and industry. This includes specific reimbursement schemes and subsidy measures to reduce the costs for businesses, thereby safeguarding competitiveness.

Performance requirements are governed by Danish legislation and EU regulations and are applied uniformly to domestic and foreign investors. Potential violations of the rules governing this area are punishable by fines or imprisonment.

The Danish government does not follow “forced localization” policies, nor does it require foreign IT providers to turn over source code or provide access to surveillance. The Danish Data Protection Agency, the Ministry of Justice, and the Ministry of Digital Affairs are the entities involved with data storage.

Cloud usage for data storage is governed by Danish legislation and EU regulations. Since the annulment of Privacy Shield, the transfer of personal information to third countries has become more burdensome. The Danish Data Protection Agency published a <u>Guide on the use of Cloud</u> <u>Services</u> to give guidance on cloud usage in March 2022.

## Section 5. Protection of Property Rights

### Real Property

Property rights in Denmark are well protected by law and in practice. Real estate is chiefly financed through the well-established Danish mortgage bond credit system, the security of

which compares to that of government bonds. In compliance with the covered bond definition in the EU Capital Requirements Directive (CRD), the Danish mortgage banking regulation allows for commercial banks to have the same opportunities as mortgage banks and ship-financing institutions to issue covered bonds. Only issuers that have been granted a license from the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority (FSA) are permitted to issue Danish covered bonds. Secured interests in property are recognized and enforced in Denmark. All mortgage credits in real estate are recorded in local public registers of mortgages. Except for interests in real estate, cars, and commercial ships, other property interests are generally unrecorded. The local public registers are a reliable system of recording security interests. Denmark ranked third globally and second in Europe in the Property Rights Alliance’s International Property Rights Index 2024.

Denmark is a contracting party to the 2001 Cape Town Convention on Mobile Equipment (CTC) and the Protocol on Matters Specific to Aircraft Equipment (Aircraft Protocol).

### Intellectual Property

Denmark is not listed in USTR’s Special 301 Report or the Notorious Market’s List. Intellectual property rights (IPR) in Denmark are well protected and enforced. Denmark has ratified and adheres to key international conventions and treaties concerning protection of IPR, including the WTO Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) and several treaties administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), including the Berne Convention, the Paris Convention, the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT), the WIPO Copyright Treaty, and the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty. For additional information about national laws and points of contact at local IPR offices, please see <u>WIPO’s country profiles</u>.

The U.S. Embassy in Copenhagen publishes <u>a list of attorneys and law firms in Denmark known</u> <u>to accept foreign clients</u>. This list is as a convenience to U.S. citizens and is not intended to be a comprehensive list of attorneys in Denmark, and the absence of an attorney from the list is in no way a reflection on competence. A complete list of attorneys in Denmark, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands may be found at the <u>Danish Bar Association website</u>.

## Section 6. Financial Sector

### Capital Markets and Portfolio Investment

Denmark has fully liberalized foreign exchange flows, including those for direct and portfolio investment purposes. Credit is allocated on market terms and is freely available. Denmark adheres to its IMF Article VIII obligations. The Danish banking system is under the regulatory oversight of the Financial Supervisory Authority. Differentiated voting rights – A and B stocks – are used to some extent, and nearly 1,000 Danish companies (including some of the country’s most prominent corporations) are controlled by foundations, which can restrict potential hostile takeovers, including foreign takeovers.

The Danish stock market functions efficiently. In 2005, the Copenhagen Stock Exchange became part of the integrated Nordic and Baltic marketplace, OMX Exchanges, which is headquartered in Stockholm. Besides Stockholm and Copenhagen, OMX also includes the stock exchanges in Helsinki, Tallinn, Riga, and Vilnius. To increase the access to capital for primarily small companies, the OMX in December 2005 opened a Nordic alternative marketplace – “First North” – in Denmark. In February 2008, the NASDAQ-OMX Group acquired the exchanges.

The Danish stock market is divided into four segments:

- The **OMXC25** index includes the 25 most valuable companies in Denmark.
- **Large Cap** companies have a market value exceeding €1 billion (approx. $1.1 billion).
- **Mid Cap** companies have a market value between €150 million (approx. $170 million) and €1 billion (approx. $1.1 billion).
- **Small Cap** companies have a market value of less than €150 million (approx. $170 million).
### Money and Banking System

The major Danish banks are rated by international agencies, and their creditworthiness is rated high by international standards. The European Central Bank and the Danish Central Bank (Nationalbanken) conduct annual stress tests of both systemic and larger banks, and the Danish

Central Bank reported in November 2023 that Denmark’s tested banks have all passed stress tests, most by considerable margins. In 2023, the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority developed and implemented cyber stress testing to strengthen the financial sector’s ability to manage extensive, long-term Information and Communication Technology (ICT) disruptions.

The IMF notes that Denmark’s financial sector is six times the size of its GDP. The three largest Danish banks – Danske Bank, Jyske Bank, and Nykredit – hold approximately 75 percent of the total assets in the Danish banking sector. As of April 2025, foreign investors held 57.4 percent of the shares in Danish banks, while Danish investors held 42.6 percent, including 12.8 percent held by Danish households and 1.9 percent held by the government.

The primary goal of the Central Bank is to maintain the peg of the Danish currency to the euro. The Central Bank followed the European Central Bank’s four interest rate cuts of one percentage point in total since October 2024. The Central Bank also functions as the general lender to Danish commercial banks and controls the money supply in the economy.

As occurred in many countries, Danish banks experienced significant turbulence in 2008–2009. The Danish parliament subsequently passed a series of measures to establish a “safety net” program, provide government lending to financial institutions in need of capital to uphold their solvency requirements, and ensure the orderly winding down of failed banks. The parliament passed an additional measure, named Bank Package 4, in 2011, which sought to identify systemically important financial institutions, ensure the liquidity of banks that assume control of a troubled bank, support banks acquiring troubled banks by allowing them to write off obligations of the troubled bank to the government, and change the funding mechanism for the sector-funded guarantee fund to a premiums-based, pay-as-you-go system. According to the Danish government, Bank Package 4 provides mechanisms for a sector solution to troubled banks without senior debt holder losses but does not supersede earlier legislation. As such, senior debt holder losses are still a possibility in the event of a bank failure.

The Danish Financial Supervisory Authority identifies specific financial institutions as “systemically important” (SIFI) annually, based on the most recent financial statements. There are nine SIFI in Denmark as of June 2024, the most recent designation, which are: Danske Bank A/S, Nykredit Realkredit A/S, Jyske Bank A/S, Nordea Kredit Realkreditaktieselskab, Sydbank

A/S, DLR Kredit A/S, Spar Nord Bank A/S, Arbejdernes Landsbank A/S, and Saxo Bank A/S. The government identified these institutions based on quantitative measures. Effective January 2022, the government’s model for calculating which banks are systemically important includes 12 indicators that can be summarized in four categories: size, importance, complexity, and interconnectedness. The new model aligns the methodology with the recommendations of the European Banking Authority. The Faroese SIFIs are P/F BankNordik and Betri Banki P/F, while Grønlandsbanken is the only SIFI in Greenland.

Denmark’s GDP grew by an estimated 3.5 percent in 2024, according to Statistic Denmark. Inflation in Denmark has been less than 2 percent since June 2024, with a rate of 1.6 percent in May 2025. Analysts assess that the effects of the shocks to the Danish economy that caused inflation to rise in 2022 (mainly coronavirus-related supply and demand issues and increase in energy prices following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine) have subsided. The Central Bank announced in February 2025 that for the fourth consecutive year, a surplus in government finances has led to a decrease in Denmark’s central government debt to a historic low of DKK 217 billion (approx. $31.7 billion), equivalent to 7.4 percent of GDP in 2024.

### Foreign Exchange and Remittances

#### Foreign Exchange

Exchange rate conversions throughout this document are based on the 2025 average exchange rate where 1 Danish krone (DKK) = 0.1459 USD.

Denmark has not adopted the euro. According to the Stability and Growth Pact, member states using the euro are expected to maintain a government debt-to-GDP ratio below 60 percent and a budget deficit-to-GDP ratio below 3 percent. The country meets the EU’s economic convergence criteria for membership and can join if it wishes to do so. Denmark conducts a fixed exchange rate policy with the Danish krone linked closely to the euro within the Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) II framework. The Danish krone (DKK; plural: kroner, in English, “the crown”) has a fluctuation band of +/- 2.25 percent of the central rate of DKK 746.038 per 100 euro. By treaty, Denmark is not required to adopt the euro as its currency or to join the European banking union.

The Danish political reservation concerning euro participation can only be abolished by national referendum, and Danish voters have twice (in 1992 and 2000) voted it down. The government has stated that it supports adopting the euro in principle, but regular polling on this issue shows a majority of public opinion remains in favor of keeping the krone.

#### Remittance Policies

There are no restrictions on converting or transferring funds associated with an investment into or out of Denmark. Policies in place are intended to facilitate the free flow of capital and to support the flow of resources in the product and services markets. Foreign investors can obtain credit in the local market at normal market terms, and a wide range of credit instruments are available.

## Section 8. Corruption

Denmark ranks as the least corrupt country in the world according to the 2024 Corruption Perceptions Index by Transparency International, which has local representation in Denmark. The Ministry of Justice is responsible for combating corruption, which is covered under the Danish Penal Code. Penalties for violations range from fines to imprisonment of up to four years for a private individual’s involvement and up to six years for a public employee’s involvement. Danish businesses cannot claim a tax deduction for the cost of bribes paid to officials abroad.

Denmark is a signatory to the OECD Convention on Combating Bribery, the UN Anticorruption Convention, and a participating member of the OECD Working Group on Bribery. In the Working Group’s 2023 Phase 4 monitoring report on Denmark, the Working Group acknowledged Denmark’s efforts to implement the Convention and related instruments, but concluded that Denmark gives insufficient priority to preventing, detecting, and penalizing foreign bribery.

## Section 9. Political and Security Environment

Denmark is a politically stable country. Incidents involving politically motivated damage to projects or installations are very rare. The EIU assessed Denmark’s political stability and security risks as very low as of March 2025.

## Section 10. Labor Policies and Practices

The Danish labor force is generally well-educated and efficient. English-language skills are good, and English is considered a natural second language among a very high proportion of Danes. The labor market is tight and flexible. U.S. companies operating in Denmark have indicated that Danish rules on hiring and firing employees generally enable employers to adjust the workforce quickly to changing market conditions. The Danish Agency for Labor Market and Recruitment describes this “flexicurity model” as one that combines high mobility between jobs, a comprehensive income safety net for the unemployed, and an active labor market policy.

The Danish labor force totaled approximately 3.1 million people in December 2024. Approximately 29 percent are employed in the public sector, which includes education and health services. Denmark’s OECD-harmonized unemployment rate was 6.2 percent in 2024, close to the EU-27's average rate of 5.9 percent but above the OECD average of 4.9 percent. Denmark faces labor shortages in certain sectors, which may present a challenge for foreign investors.

The labor force participation rate is among the highest in the world. In the fourth quarter of 2024, 76.9 percent of the working-age population participated in the labor force, while the unemployment rate was approximately 6.2 percent. The Central Bank predicts that by the end of 2027, 35,000 more people are expected to be employed than at the beginning of 2025.

The Danish labor force is highly organized, with a trade union density of 67 percent of wage earners belonging to a union in 2019, the second highest in the OECD. Labor disputes and strikes occur only sporadically. In general, private sector labor/management relations are excellent, based on dialogue and consensus rather than confrontation. Working conditions are

established through a complex system of legislation and organizational agreements, where most aspects of wage and working conditions are determined through collective bargaining rather than legislation.

The contractual work week for most wage earners is 37.5 hours. By law, employees are entitled to five weeks of paid annual leave. In practice, most of the labor force has the right to six weeks of paid annual leave, gained through labor market accords.

Denmark has well-functioning unemployment insurance and sick-pay schemes, self-financed or financed by the state. Employees have a right to take maternity or paternity leave, which in Denmark is a total of 52 weeks. As a general rule, each parent is entitled to 24 weeks of leave after birth with maternity/paternity benefits if the parents live together when the child is born. For a salaried employee, up to 13 weeks of the leave can be transferred to the other parent. The mother may in general take: pregnancy leave four weeks before the expected date of birth, ten weeks of leave immediately after the birth of the child, and the remaining 14 weeks of leave before the child is one year old. The father or co-mother may take: two weeks of leave after the birth and 22 weeks of leave before the child is one year old.

Danish wages are high by international standards and have prompted the use of capital- intensive technologies in many sectors. Some investors report that the high average wage level is detrimental to Danish competitiveness. Although high wages and generous benefits, including time off, may reduce relative competitiveness, high productivity and low direct costs to employers can result in per employee costs that are lower than in other industrialized countries. Under Denmark’s “flexicurity model,” both employers and employees in Denmark make minimal contributions to social security; Denmark’s comprehensive welfare system is largely funded through general taxation. Private sector real wages increased on average by 3 percent from Q4 2023 to Q4 2024. The general private sector labor accord that was finalized in spring 2023 resulted in wage increases in 2023 and 2024 of approximately 5 percent in 2023 and 4-4.5 percent in 2024. The Central Bank reports that new collective agreements in the private labor market include significantly lower wage increases over the next three years due to reduced pressure on the labor market.

Generally, personal income tax rates in Denmark are among the highest in the world. In 2025, an individual who is fully tax resident in Denmark will generally pay tax according to the ordinary tax scheme of up to 52 percent (or 55.9 percent including alternative minimum tax, which is also income tax for double taxation treaty purposes). However, foreign employees making more than an amount specified annually by the Danish Immigration Service and certain researchers may choose to be subject to a 27 percent income tax rate, plus a labor market contribution tax amounting to 32.84 percent income tax in the first seven years of working in Denmark. Certain conditions must be fulfilled for key employees to be eligible for the 27 percent tax rate: for example, since January 1, 2025, wages must total at least DKK 78,000 (approx. $11,900) per month before the deduction of labor market contributions and after Danish labor market supplementary pension contributions. There are also limits based on an individual’s previous work history in the Danish labor market. These measures are intended to act as an incentive compared with the general Danish progressive income tax system. Further information can be obtained from Danish embassies or from the <u>Danish Immigration Service</u>.

Danish work permits are not required for citizens of EU countries. U.S. companies have reported that in general, work permits for foreign managerial staff may be readily obtained. However, permits for non-managerial workers from countries outside the EU and the Nordic countries are granted only if substantial professional or labor-related conditions warrant. Special rules detailed by the Danish Immigration Service in its "Positive List Scheme" apply to certain professional fields experiencing a shortage of qualified manpower. The list is updated twice annually. Foreigners who have been hired in the designated fields will be immediately eligible for residence and work permits. The minimum educational level required for a position on the Positive List is an occupational bachelor's degree, a series of education usually 3.5 years in length combining theoretical and practical experience. In some cases, a Danish authorization must be obtained, which the Positive List will explicitly state if applicable (e.g., non-Danish- trained doctors must be authorized by the Danish Patient Safety Authority). Professions covered by the Positive List Scheme include engineers, scientists, doctors, nurses, IT specialists, marine biologists, lawyers, accountants, and a wide range of other master's or bachelor's degree positions.

Persons who have been offered a highly paid job have particularly easy access to the Danish labor market through the Pay Limit Scheme. As of 2025, the Pay Limit Scheme extends to positions with an annual pay of no less than DKK 514,000 (approx. $75,000), regardless of the field or specific nature of the job. The length of work and residence permits granted under the Pay Limit Scheme depend on the length of the employment contract in Denmark. For permanent employment contracts, work permits are granted for an initial period of four years. After this period, the permit can be extended if the same job is held. There are several other schemes meant to make it easier for certified companies to bring employees with special skills or qualifications to Denmark. These schemes vary in duration and requirements.

Danish immigration law also allows issuance of residency permits of up to 18 months’ duration based on an individual evaluation, using a point system based on education, language skills, and adaptability.

Denmark has ratified all eight ILO Core Conventions and been an ILO member since 1919.

## Section 12. Other Areas in the Kingdom of Denmark

### The Faroe Islands

The Faroe Islands have an open economy and multiple trade agreements with other countries. The Government of the Faroe Islands retains control over most internal affairs, including the conservation and management of living marine resources within the 200 nautical mile fisheries zone, natural resources, financial regulation and supervision, and transport. Denmark

continues to exercise control over foreign affairs, security, and defense, in consultation with the Faroese government.

For more than two centuries, the Faroese economy has relied on fisheries and related industries. Fish and fish products account for between 90 and 95 percent of the total export value, reaching a recent peak of 97 percent in September 2024. The fishing industry accounts for around 20 percent of the GDP and employs 15 percent of the labor force. The fisheries industry includes aquaculture, focused on salmon farming, and fishing activities targeting pelagic and demersal fish (such as cod, mackerel, herring, and haddock), as well as their processing. The Faroe Islands exported DKK 12.3 billion (approx. $1.78 billion) worth of goods in 2024, 93.5 percent of which were fish products. Salmon alone accounted for 46 percent of goods exports between April 2024 and March 2025. Among the Faroe Islands' other important economic activities are financial services, energy-related businesses, shipping, manufacturing for the maritime sector, IT and telecommunication, tourism, and creative industries.

As a non-EU member, the Faroe Islands had open access to the Russian market post-2014, despite Russia’s retaliatory trade embargo on certain food imports from the EU. The Faroese government announced in February 2022 that it supports Western sanctions in response to Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine and the major Faroese aquaculture companies voluntarily withdrew from exporting salmon to the Russian market. In May 2022, the Faroese parliament passed legislation to enable it to implement sanctions against Russia and Belarus. In November 2024, the Faroese government introduced additional sanctions against goods not covered by previous sanctions, such as seafood. These sanctions also cover dual use items and entities on the EU’s list, thereby integrating Faroese sanctions fully with those of the EU and include provisions against sanctions evasion. In December 2024, the Faroe Islands renewed its bilateral fisheries agreement with Russia for 2025. Exports to Russia accounted for 9.2 percent of total exports in 2024.

In 2024, the United States became the Faroe Islands’ top destination for goods exports, receiving 12.8 percent of Faroe Islands’ exports. Denmark was the Faroe Islands’ primary goods export destination in 2022 and 2023; Denmark received 10.3 percent of 2024 exports. Other top export destinations in 2024 were:

- United Kingdom (10.2 percent),
- Russia (9.2 percent),
- Norway (7.8 percent), and
- China (6.3 percent).
Goods imports totaled DKK 10.3 billion (approx. $1.49 billion) in 2024, with the vast majority from Europe (with 48.9 percent of imports to the Faroe Islands from the EU in 2024). Of total Faroes' goods imports in 2024, 23.8 percent came from Denmark, followed by:

- Norway (22.7 percent),
- Germany (6.8 percent),
- China (6.2 percent), and
- the Netherlands (4 percent).
Direct imports from the United States were 2.7 percent of total imports. Major import categories were:

- machinery and transport equipment (23.5 percent),
- food and live animals (22.4 percent),
- mineral fuels, lubricants and related materials (14.6 percent), and
- manufactured goods (13.8 percent).
Official statistics list 2023 as the most recent year available for GDP figures, at DKK 26.9 billion (approx. $3.9 billion). According to Statistics Faroe Islands, real GDP rose:

- 2.5 percent in 2023,
- 3.6 percent in 2022,
- 5.5 percent in 2021, and
- contracted by -1.6 percent in 2020, mainly due to COVID-19 and related price effects on fish prices.

The average real growth rate from 2013 to 2023 was 3.2 percent. According to the Danish Central Bank, the Faroese fishing industry has benefited in recent years from high fish prices, which have contributed to a sharp increase in exports. The favorable conditions for export-oriented fishing and aquaculture industries have driven overall growth in the Faroese economy. However, the growth in Faroese exports of fish and fish products has slowed, increasing by 2 percent (in value terms) after a few years of annual growth in export value reaching over 20 percent. Denmark’s Central Bank attributed this slowdown to a decline in the export value of farmed salmon.

Following a sharp rise in the consumer price index (CPI) from the spring of 2021 to a peak in November 2022 of 10.1 percent annual inflation, inflation has since retreated to a nadir of 0.8 percent in November 2024. The average annual inflation for 2024 was 2.0 percent. The Faroe Islands' small, open, but non-diversified economy makes it vulnerable to changes in international markets. The Faroe Islands have full autonomy to set tax rates and fees, and to set levels of spending on the services the government provides. Denmark provides an annual block grant that remained at DKK 641.8 million (approx. $93.1 million) annually from 2015 to

2022. In 2023, the Faroese government announced a plan to reduce the block grant from Denmark by DKK 100 million (approx. $14.5 million) over the following four years, starting with a reduction of DKK 25 million (approx. $3.63 million) in 2023. The 2023 block grant of DKK
616.8 million (approx. $89.5 million) corresponded to 2.4 percent of GDP. The 2024 block grant was DKK 591.8 million (approx. $85.8 million). Statistics Faroe Islands notes the population of the Faroe Islands as 54,903 in May 2025, with population growth now following a period of decline. Analysts attribute this reversal to significant economic growth in the Faroe Islands since 2011. However in the longer term, the aging Faroese population will strain the sustainability of public finances, according to numerous reports from the Economic Council for the Faroe Islands, first reported in 2015. According to a December 2024 report from the Danish Central Bank, the Faroe Islands is undergoing a demographic shift, with the ratio of people of working age to those over state pension age steadily decreasing and putting increasing pressure on public finances, even though public debt is currently low. The Economic Council of the Faroe Islands’ most recent sustainability

calculation estimates that a permanent fiscal tightening of 9 percent of GDP will be required to bring public finances into balance in the long term.

The Faroe Islands have a well-established financial sector comprised of four banks, two insurance companies, two life insurance companies, and some public funds. Faroese banks are under the supervision of the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority. The currency of the Faroe Islands is the Faroese króna, issued by the Danish National Bank, and holding the same value as the Danish krone. The Faroe Islands Governmental Bank, established in 2017, is the financial institution of the Faroese government and manages the country’s liquidity and loan facilities. The Faroe Islands opened its own securities exchange in 2000; active trading of shares followed in 2005. The exchange is a collaboration with the VMF Icelandic exchange on the Nasdaq OMX Nordic Exchange Iceland.

In September 2024, credit agency Moody's maintained its Aa2 long-term issuer rating of the Government of the Faroe Islands, a rating it has held since 2019. The Aa2 rating is the third-highest rating on Moody’s 21-tier scale. The outlook remains stable and Moody’s concludes that the Faroe Islands’ economic policies will continue to support macroeconomic stability which, along with a continuation of strong institutions and effective policy making, will result in strong financial health. Moody’s notes the Faroe Islands’ “small and undiversified economy” – notably a high reliance on revenues from fishing and fish farming – poses constraints to its credit profile.

Faroese foreign liabilities of DKK 2.5 billion (approx. $353 million) in 2022 turned into $740 million (DKK 5.1 billion) in net assets by 2023. Rising share prices combined with a decrease in foreign securities holdings in Faroese companies contributed to this significant DKK 7.6 billion increase in net assets from 2022 to 2023. The Faroese government has indicated an interest in attracting further foreign investment. <u>Invest in the Faroes</u> is the Faroese government unit promoting Faroese trade. There are also business development schemes, including several managed by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Labor, that aim to promote entrepreneurship, innovation, and exports.

The Faroe Islands have, over the years, been involved in several disputes with the EU over fishing quotas, and the EU adopted measures that allowed it to impose sanctions on the Faroe

Islands in 2012. In March 2013, the Faroe Islands unilaterally increased its quota for herring and mackerel. EU member states responded by voting in favor of imposing sanctions, which went into force in August 2013. The EU lifted sanctions in 2014 after reaching a political understanding with the Faroe Islands on herring catches. Subsequently, the Faroe Islands and the other coastal states in the North Atlantic signed a five-year agreement on mackerel quotas, reducing uncertainty for fisheries and improving profitability since the agreement allows for more sustainable harvesting. The Faroe Islands negotiates reciprocal exchanges of fishing opportunities with the EU, Norway, Russia, and the United Kingdom annually.

Employment has risen notably, and the labor market participation rate is high at 85 percent in 2024, which has pushed down unemployment from 7 percent in 2011 to record lows. Unemployment was around 1 percent throughout 2024 and was exactly 1 percent in April 2025. The labor force comprised 33,102 people in 2024. The need for labor has increasingly been met via high net immigration, which has prolonged the economic upswing. According to the Danish Central Bank, the Faroese economy is in a “boom period” with effectively full employment. High inflation and widespread labor market pressures in recent years have resulted in significant upward pay adjustments for employees in a number of recent collective agreements. The increase in employment in recent years is a result of an expanding labor force, including many foreigners entering the Faroese labor market. Denmark’s Central Bank further reports around half of the increase in employment since 2019 has been driven by foreigners. The many new inhabitants along with rising disposable income, however, have put the housing market under pressure, especially in and around the capital Tórshavn. The housing market seems to have stabilized in 2023 after a period of annual double-digit growth in prices; housing prices even decreased by 1.8 percent from 2023 to 2024.

In many areas, the Faroese labor market model resembles other Nordic countries, with high standards of living, well-established welfare schemes, and independent labor unions. Most people in the Faroe Islands are bilingual or multilingual, with Danish and English being the most widely spoken languages after Faroese. The Faroe Islands boast well-developed physical and telecommunications infrastructure and have well-established political, legal, and social structures. The standard of living for the population of 54,903 is high by global standards, with

GDP per capita in 2023 of DKK 496,100 (approx. $72,003), surpassing Denmark’s 2023 GDP per capita of DKK 468,238 (approx. $67,959).

### Greenland

The Greenland government continues to explore ways to increase trade and diversify its international relationships for sustainable economic growth. The vast majority of Greenland’s exports and imports pass through Denmark to and from the rest of the world and are reported as trade between the two.

Greenland is a self-governing area within the Kingdom of Denmark. Greenland originally joined the EU with Denmark in 1973 but left in 1985 after the territory gained Home Rule and is today one of the EU’s Overseas Countries and Territories. Greenland’s status within the Kingdom of Denmark is outlined in the Self Rule Act (SRA) of 2009, which details the Greenland government’s right to assume in the future a number of additional responsibilities from Denmark’s government, including the administration of justice, labor, aviation, immigration and border control, and financial regulation and supervision, beyond what it had after the introduction of Home Rule in 1979. The Self Government Act gave the Greenland government authority over its underground resources, including minerals and hydrocarbons. By 2009, Greenland had already acquired control over taxation, fisheries, internal labor negotiations, natural resources, and oversight of offshore labor, environment, and safety regulations. Denmark continues to have control over foreign affairs, security, and defense policy, in consultation with Greenland and the Faroe Islands. Denmark also retains authority over border control issues, including immigration into Greenland.

Two-thirds of Greenland lies above the Arctic Circle, its northern tip is less than 500 miles from the North Pole, and 81 percent of its landmass is covered by the ice sheet. Greenland can be reached by air or freighter from Denmark, Iceland, and the United States (seasonal flights between Newark and Nuuk planned to start June 2025). Greenland has no road network connecting population centers; air and sea connections are the only options. A new international airport opened in November 2024 in Nuuk; new airports in Ilulissat and Qaqortoq will open in 2026, joining an existing international airport at Kangerlussuaq.

Greenland’s GDP was estimated at DKK 22.9 billion (approx. $3.32 billion) in 2023, according to Greenland Statistics. GDP per capita in 2023 was DKK 405,200 (approx. $58,810). The 2009 Self-Government Act set an annual Danish grant to Greenland of DKK 3.4 billion (approx. $515.8 million), though that figure is adjusted annually “in accordance with the increase in the general price and wage index.” In 2023, the block grant amounted to about DKK 4.14 billion (approx. $627.8 million). While the block grant constitutes up to 51 percent of the Greenland government’s revenue, the grant’s relative share of GDP has been declining, falling below 19 percent in 2023, according to Greenland’s Ministry of Finance and Taxes. Beyond the block grant, the Danish government’s additional expenses related to Greenland (public order and safety, police services, military and defense, prisons, courts, economic affairs, research, and environmental protection among other areas) amounted to €204 million ($188.5 million) in

2023. Greenland also receives about 4 percent of its treasury income from the European Union. The Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) elevated Greenland’s ratings for both security and political stability risk to “A” in May 2025. Public service and administration accounts for more than 45 percent of jobs, according to Greenland’s statistics authority. This figure does not include government-owned enterprise jobs, of which one major company alone accounts for over 5 percent of employment. Greenland’s five largest government-owned enterprises comprise 55 percent of its GDP. Fishing is Greenland’s single most important commercial industry, accounting for more than 90 percent of exports in 2024. The sector is dominated by two companies, the Government of Greenland-owned Royal Greenland and the privately-owned Polar Seafood. Greenland imports goods from all over the world, primarily through Denmark, and to a lesser extent, via Iceland. Royal Greenland’s largest single market is China, and one-third of its revenues are generated in Asia, half in Europe, and 10 percent in North America, which the company views as a growth market. Polar Seafood has its main markets in Scandinavia, China, and Japan. Over the past decade, rising prices for fish and shellfish, Greenland’s predominant exports, have generated solid earnings for large parts of the fisheries sector, though they were negatively impacted by COVID-19 border closures in China. The Greenland government directed government-owned enterprises to stop trading with Russia in February 2022. Greenland has joined EU sanctions

against Russia. Greenland’s parliament adopted legislation in May 2022 providing the framework for the government to impose sanctions against Russia.

The Greenland government seeks to increase economic growth and government revenues by promoting the further development of fisheries, mineral resources, renewable energy production, and tourism. Key initiatives include improving access to local financing for new businesses and enhancing Greenland’s corporate tax competitiveness. The government anticipates launching tenders for two large hydropower and associated off-take projects in coming years. Greenland owns and has disposal rights over all mineral resources, including oil and gas resources, although since 2021 there is a moratorium on oil/gas exploration licenses. To meet anticipated demand and spur growth in the tourism sector, the Government of Greenland has started construction of three new airports in Nuuk, the capital, Ilulissat, the primary tourist destination, and Qaqortoq in south Greenland. Private partnerships are underway to expand adventure tourism from the shoreline to the polar ice cap and to increase access to Greenland’s UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Other efforts to develop tourism include increases in hotel capacity and a focus on promoting foreign language education to create a more multilingual workforce. The government is calling for stricter safety requirements for navigation in Greenlandic waters.

Greenland is aiming to significantly boost its tourism sector through tourism legislation (passed in November 2024) and the development of a 10-year tourism strategy. This strategy aspires for tourism to account for 40 percent of Greenland’s export value by 2035 and to double tourism-related employment from the 2023 figure of around 1,000 jobs. The 2024 tourism law introduces several elements related to investors, including a requirement that tourism businesses have two-thirds local ownership (defined as a business which has been registered in Greenland for at least five years), a process for municipal governments to restrict tourism activities in certain geographic areas, and a requirement for tour operators to have a safety plan. More than 140,000 tourists visited Greenland by cruise ships and air in 2023 – a record season for tourism. Greenland experienced a 24.5 percent increase in the number of cruise tourists in 2024 compared to 2023, with 98,185 cruise passenger arrivals registered in 2024.

#### Establishing a Company in Greenland

Information on establishing a business in Greenland can be found on tradeinvest.gl.

Danish <u>business (CVR) registration through Virk</u> is required for conducting business in Greenland. Furthermore, companies planning to have employees must register as an employer with the employer register <u>Sulinal</u>. Foreign companies may start their businesses in Greenland either through a subsidiary (both ApS and A/S type companies) or via a registered branch office.

- **ApS and A/S:** An ApS (private limited company) or A/S (public limited company) is a separate legal entity with limited liability for its shareholders. The main difference between a private (ApS) and a public (A/S) limited company is that the shares of a private limited company cannot be issued publicly. Therefore, an ApS cannot be subject to listing or otherwise issue shares to the public to secure more capital. In addition, there are a few differences concerning capital and management requirements. Under the Companies Act, the minimum share capital requirement for an ApS is DKK 40,000 (approx. $6,060). The minimum share capital requirement for an A/S is DKK 400,000 (approx. $60,560). However, under the Danish Companies Act, it is possible to incorporate an A/S and only pay 25 percent of this amount (DKK 100,000, or approximately $15,140), leaving the company with a receivable on the shareholders for the outstanding amount (DKK 300,000, or approximately $45,420). A founder of a company may be foreign or Greenlandic individuals or corporate entities. Both ApS and A/S companies can be registered via the Danish Business Authority’s (DBA) online system. No registration fees are required.
- **Registered Branch Office:** A foreign company may typically establish a registered branch office in Greenland instead of establishing a Greenlandic company. A branch of a foreign company may be created through an application with the DBA. Companies within the EU and European Economic Area (EEA) may set up a branch in Greenland and Denmark without further approval from the DBA. However, companies outside of the EU/EEA must obtain approval before registering. A foreign company can do business in Greenland in a consecutive or non-consecutive 90-day period over 12 months without being required to register as a business.

#### Greenland Tax

Greenland’s tax system is based on flat-rate taxation of business profits for both resident and non-resident corporations. Greenland operates with a net income principle, where the taxable income is calculated as a total net amount after deductions. The net income principle means that all income is treated equally, regardless of whether the income comes from employment, self-employment, investment income, or pensions, etc. As the rules of taxation for businesses can be complicated, potential investors may seek to retain guidance from the <u>Greenlandic Tax</u> <u>Agency</u> or professional consultants.

The corporate income tax rate is 25 percent; an additional surcharge of 6 percent of the tax payable brings the total corporate tax rate to 26.5 percent.

The taxation of royalty payments is 30 percent. Greenland has no value-added tax (VAT) system, property tax, sales tax, or similar taxes. There are, however, some payable duties, such as taxes for cruise liners, ports duties, etc. There are four types of depreciation in Greenland's tax law. Buildings can be depreciated 5 percent annually. Ships, planes, and hydrocarbon prospecting can be depreciated 10 percent annually. Mineral licenses can be depreciated 25 percent each year for four years, and operating equipment can be depreciated at a rate of 30 percent annually. Assets with a cost of less than DKK 100,000 (approx. $15,140) may be depreciated in the year of acquisition.

Permanent establishments of foreign companies in Greenland are taxed under the same rules and rates as companies resident in Greenland. There is no branch profits remittance tax or other similar tax on branch profits. If a foreign company has more than one location or permanent establishment in Greenland, these are treated as separate taxable entities with no possibility of consolidation.

#### Greenland Labor

Greenland’s labor force was 29,049 persons in 2021. More than 40 percent of all jobs are found in the public sector. Average unemployment for 2023 was 2.9 percent according to Statistics Greenland. The Central Bank estimates around 80 percent of the population aged

between 25 and 65 participate in the labor market, which is slightly below the OECD average and well below the Nordic countries. Unemployment has decreased significantly, especially in Nuuk (population 20,000). Nuuk has a multilingual workforce. In Greenland, residents pay 42-

44.5 percent tax from personal income, depending on the municipality. According to Statistics Greenland, 54 percent of the Greenlandic population age 16 and above had an education beyond primary school in 2023. Of these, 27 percent had vocational education, while 14 percent had some form of tertiary education. In 2020, education expenses in Greenland accounted for just over 10 percent of the country’s GDP, owing to housing and transportation expenses since there are only four high schools and a limited number of upper primary schools in Greenland. By comparison, Denmark spent 5.8 percent, while the OECD average was 5.1 percent. The official language of Greenland is Greenlandic. Residents of Greenland often speak Greenlandic, Danish, or both. English use is growing but might be more limited outside of large towns. English is part of Greenland’s primary education curriculum. In December 2012, Greenland passed legislation known as the “Large Scale Act,” which allows companies to use foreign labor during the construction phase of development when project costs exceed DKK 5 billion (approx. $725 million) and when workforce requirements exceed the local labor supply. The Act is intended for potential mining or infrastructure projects in Greenland. The Act lays out the framework for politically negotiated Impact Benefit Agreements (IBA) for the Government of Greenland and the employer to agree on the exact conditions of employment for foreign labor. The scale of Greenlandic labor utilized will be negotiated for each project and will vary depending on local capacity and the negotiated agreement for each project. In 2021, Greenland implemented the Fast Track Scheme. This arrangement allows businesses in industries facing labor shortages to hire foreign workers who can begin working before they are approved for a work permit. Denmark’s Agency for International Recruitment and Integration (SIRI) manages labor visas and permits for Greenland. SIRI plans to open an office in Nuuk in October 2025 to process residence permits for employment and education. Foreign workers enjoy the same legal protections as Greenlandic workers, including the same per hour minimum wage and retention of the right to strike.

#### Investment in Natural Resources

Greenland possesses sizable discovered and undiscovered mineral resources, including some of the world’s largest deposits. These include iron and ferroalloys (iron, nickel, molybdenum, tungsten), base metals (copper, zinc, lead), specialty metals (rare earth elements—including the world’s largest hard rock-hosted REEs—uranium, niobium, tantalum, beryllium, cobalt, antimony), precious metals (platinum group elements and gold), gemstones (diamonds, rubies, sapphires), and other critical minerals (fluorite, graphite, titanium, vanadium). These resources are essential to advanced technology and industrial base applications. This resource base gives Greenland the potential for large-scale critical mineral production; however, mining projects involve long timelines and sizeable investments, and Greenland’s high operating costs and a lack of critical infrastructure and labor present further challenges in the near-term. At present, 47 companies – mostly small exploration firms from Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom – hold 90 exploration permits and exploitation licenses for acreage along Greenland’s ice-free coasts, a zone roughly equivalent in area to California.

With the 2009 Self Government Act, Greenland gained rights to its mineral and hydrocarbon resources, and it acquired the regulatory authority over these on January 1, 2010. The Act also created a revenue mechanism: if Greenland’s natural resources’ exploitation becomes commercially viable, Greenland will keep the first DKK 75 million (approx. $11.4 million) in annual revenues derived from these resources. Additional revenues will be split equally between the governments of Denmark and Greenland, with Denmark’s share transferred by deducting the equivalent amount from the annual block grant to Greenland of DKK 4 billion (approx. $606 million). Once the block grant’s total value is reached, any additional revenue will be subject to negotiations between the Danish and Greenlandic governments.

Mining industry experts anticipate that Greenland’s retreating ice will make the island’s stores of raw materials more easily accessible. However, exploration and exploitation projects will still face higher costs because of remote locations, lack of infrastructure, harsh climate, and distance to world markets. The Greenland government also adopted into law the “Uranium Act” in 2021, which prohibits the exploration and exploitation of mineral deposits with radioactive elements above 100 parts per million.

Most of Greenland’s identified rare earth deposits are licensed by the Mineral License and Safety Authority, and some have reached advanced stages of exploration. In 2023, Greenland ranked 60th out of 86 jurisdictions in the Canadian Fraser Institute’s Investment Attractiveness Index and was the worst performer among all European jurisdictions included in the study. All respondents for Greenland cited concerns about the country's infrastructure, with 88 percent expressing uncertainty concerning environmental regulations, and 83 percent highlighting issues with the availability of skilled labor.

#### Greenland General Business Information

The <u>Trade and Invest in Greenland</u> website helps foreign companies search for information about trade with and investment in Greenland. The website contains relevant information for companies, such as requirements and regulations for import and export between Greenland and the United States. There are descriptions of the various sectors in Greenland such as fishing, raw materials and mining, tourism, ice and water, and research. The <u>Mineral Resources</u> <u>Authority</u> provides information on natural resource exploration and extraction. Information about doing business in Greenland can be found at <u>Trade and Invest in Greenland</u>. <u>Statistics</u> <u>Greenland</u>, the central statistical organization in Greenland, provides statistics on Greenland. <u>Greenland’s official public procurement portal, Udbudsportalen</u>, provides information on public tenders.

Greenland does not allow private ownership of land, but a right of use may be sold for an area (i.e., if you buy property, you own the building but not the land on which it sits). By law, private property can only be expropriated for public purposes in areas under Greenlandic government jurisdiction, in a non-discriminatory manner, and with reasonable compensation. There have been no recent expropriations of significance in Greenland.

There had been no significant disputes over foreign investment in Greenland in recent years, but in March 2022 Australia-based Greenland Minerals requested international arbitration regarding a dispute with the Governments of Greenland and the Kingdom of Denmark over the future of its Kvanefjeld rare earths project. While it is common that disputes are settled in Greenland’s courts, which Denmark administers, Denmark’s Supreme Court remains the

highest appeals court for disputes in Greenland. If a dispute is very specialized and within the purview of the Danish Administration of Justice Act, the parties involved may choose the Danish Maritime and Commercial Court as a court of first instance.
