New Oxford American Dictionary

The _**New Oxford American Dictionary**_ ( _**NOAD**_) is a single-volume [dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary "Dictionary") of [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English") compiled by American editors at the [Oxford University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University_Press "Oxford University Press").

_NOAD_ is based upon the _[New Oxford Dictionary of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Oxford_Dictionary_of_English "New Oxford Dictionary of English")_ ( _NODE_), published in the United Kingdom in 1998, although with substantial editing, additional entries, and the inclusion of illustrations. It is based on a [corpus linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_linguistics "Corpus linguistics") analysis of Oxford's 200 million word database of contemporary [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English").

_NOAD_ includes a [diacritical respelling scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation_respelling_for_English "Pronunciation respelling for English") to convey pronunciations, as opposed to the [Gimson phonemic IPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation_respelling_for_English#International_Phonetic_Alphabet "Pronunciation respelling for English") system that is used in _NODE_.

## Editions

### First edition

Published in September 2001, the first edition was edited by Elizabeth J. Jewell and Frank Abate.

### Second edition

Published in May 2005, the second edition was edited by [Erin McKean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erin_McKean "Erin McKean"). The edition added nearly 3,000 new words, senses, and phrases. It was in a large format, with 2096 pages, and was 8½" by 11" in size. It included a CD-ROM with the full text of the dictionary for [Palm OS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_OS "Palm OS") devices.

Since 2005 [Apple Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc.")'s [Mac OS X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_X "Mac OS X") operating system has come bundled with a [dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_(software) "Dictionary (software)") application and [widget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widget_(computing) "Widget (computing)") which credits as its source "Oxford American Dictionaries", and contains the full text of _NOAD2_. The [Amazon Kindle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Kindle "Amazon Kindle") reading device also uses _NOAD_ as its built-in dictionary, along with a choice for the _[Oxford Dictionary of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_English "Oxford Dictionary of English")_.

Oxford University Press published _NOAD2_ in electronic form in 2006 at the OxfordAmericanDictionary.com,[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1) and in 2010, along with the _Oxford Dictionary of English_, as part of [Oxford Dictionaries Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OxfordDictionaries.com "OxfordDictionaries.com").[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)

### Third edition

Published in August 2010, the third edition was edited by [Angus Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Stevenson "Angus Stevenson") and Christine A. Lindberg. This edition includes over 2,000 new words, senses, and phrases, and over 1,000 illustrations; hundreds of new and revised explanatory notes, new "Word Trends" feature charts usage for rapidly changing words and phrases.

### Abridged edition

Based on the NODE, the _Concise Oxford American Dictionary_ was published in 2006.

## Fictitious entry

The dictionary includes an entry for the word "esquivalience", which it defines as meaning "the willful avoidance of one's official responsibilities". This is a [fictitious entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_entry "Fictitious entry"), intended to protect the copyright of the publication. The entry was invented by Christine Lindberg, one of the editors of the _NOAD_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3)

With the publication of the second edition, a rumor circulated that the dictionary contained a fictitious entry in the letter 'e'. _[New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")_ contributing editor Henry Alford combed the section, and discussed several unusual entries he found with a group of American lexicographers. Most found "esquivalience" to be the most likely candidate, and when Alford approached _NOAD_ editor in chief [Erin McKean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erin_McKean "Erin McKean") she confirmed it was a fake entry, which had been present since the first edition, in order to protect the copyright of the [CD-ROM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD-ROM "CD-ROM") edition. Of the word, she said "its inherent fakeitude is fairly obvious".[&#8202;](#ref-back-alford-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-alford)[&#8202;](#ref-back-curzan-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-curzan)

The fake entry apparently ensnared [Dictionary.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary.com "Dictionary.com"), which included an entry for it (that has since been removed) which it attributed to _[Webster's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webster's "Webster's") New Millennium Dictionary_, both of which are owned by the private company [Lexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference.com "Reference.com").[&#8202;](#ref-back-alford-1)[\[4\]](#refnote-alford) Possibly due to its licensing of Oxford dictionaries, [Google Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Dictionary "Google Dictionary") included the word, listing three meanings and giving usage examples.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]
