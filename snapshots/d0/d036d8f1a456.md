# Supporting NOCs and their athletes every step of the way to Paris 2024 and beyond

2024 was truly an Olympic year – a momentous time when years of dedication and 
preparation culminated in unforgettable 
experiences. It was a year when dreams 
were realised or challenged, and new stars 
emerged on the world stage. Once again, 
the Olympic Games powerfully showcased 
the spirit that unites us all: humanity coming 
together in celebration of sport and shared 
values.

The National Olympic Committees (NOCs) 
were indispensable in readying their delegations for this groundbreaking occasion 
– from long months of preparation to the 
intense weeks of competition. For the 
International Olympic Committee (IOC) 
teams who work with NOCs, it was also a 
pivotal period. After years of collaboration 
and meticulous planning alongside the 
Organising Committee for the Olympic 
Games (OCOG), the NOC Relations team 
witnessed the tangible results of their efforts 
firsthand, including the successful participation of athletes from the territories of all 
206 NOCs. The Governance Services team 
ensured that all NOCs strictly adhered to the 
principles of the Olympic Charter and were 
in good standing to be able to carry out their 
mission and operate properly. Meanwhile, 
Olympic Solidarity saw its mission fulfilled 
through programmes supporting elite athletes in their preparation for and participation 
in the Games.

While the Olympic Games shine as the 
pinnacle of athletic achievement, they represent only the tip of the iceberg – or, if you 
prefer, the summit of a pyramid. Beneath 
that visible peak lies a strong and essential

foundation: grassroots sport, community 
engagement and youth athlete development. 
This report highlights the vital work that 
the NOC Relations, Olympic Solidarity and 
Olympism365 teams undertake to ensure 
this foundation remains solid and continues 
to grow stronger year after year, and that 
sport contributes to achieving the United 
Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals.

2024 was also a landmark year for the next 
generation of winter sports athletes. The 
Winter Youth Olympic Games Gangwon 
2024 brought together young hopefuls for 12 
days of outstanding competition and enriching youth activities.

Looking ahead, challenges will undoubtedly 
arise. Yet NOCs can approach the future 
with optimism. The approved budget for 
the Olympic Solidarity 2025–2028 plan has 
reached a record USD 650 million, an overall 
increase of 10 per cent from the previous 
cycle, with the majority of the additional 
funds earmarked for continued support for 
athletes. This strengthened investment, 
combined with the ongoing and never-ending efforts to promote good governance, 
highlights a firm commitment to enhancing 
support for NOCs and their athletes in the 
years to come.

The extraordinary work and dedication 
demonstrated by NOCs and all the partners 
over the past few years deserves special 
recognition. The shared journey, fostering 
the Olympic values and empowering athletes 
worldwide, continues.

Happy reading!

PETER HARRIS

# Arshad Nadeem: Perseverance pays off as Pakistani javelin star strikes Olympic gold

Hard work, new equipment fuel record-breaking
throw at Paris 2024

When asked to sum up his accomplishments at the Olympic Games Paris 2024, 
Pakistani javelin ace Arshad Nadeem puts 
it simply: “I continue to be mesmerised by 
what I did.”

And who wouldn’t be? Nadeem crushed 
all competition at Paris 2024, securing 
gold with a colossal throw of 92.97m, an 
Olympic record.

The result was a full 3.52m ahead of runner-up and reigning Olympic champion 
Neeraj Chopra of India, a rival Nadeem had 
yet to vanquish in nine previous attempts, 
and an additional 0.91m better than that of 
two-time world champion Anderson Peters 
of Grenada.

Not that Nadeem, an Olympic Scholarshipholder for Paris 2024, paid any mind to 
the final rankings. “I’m indifferent to what 
everybody else is doing,” he admitted. “I 
do not compete against other athletes. My 
competition is with myself.”

In terms of personal growth, Nadeem’s 
performance in Paris was a striking 
improvement on his Olympic debut at

Tokyo 2020, where he placed fifth with a 
throw of 84.62m.

The 27-year-old credits his steady progress, which included a gold medal at 
the 2022 Commonwealth Games and a 
silver medal at the 2023 World Athletics 
Championships, to the lessons learnt at 
his first Games and the relentless training 
that followed.

“Tokyo 2020 was my first Olympics, and 
it was an introduction to what an Olympic 
competition was, so I had some shortcomings,” he said. “What the world saw [at Paris 
2024] was a result of what I had gained at 
the previous Olympics, what I took note of, 
and how I worked on these points during 
my training.”

Nadeem was also able to put his Olympic 
Solidarity scholarship funding to good use 
ahead of Paris. It included better training 
opportunities overseas and the purchase of 
some much-needed equipment.

“The Olympic Solidarity scholarship was 
very beneficial,” Nadeem said. “It aided 
especially in my training, as I used part of it 
to go to South Africa for training.”

“I also bought some equipment,” he added, 
before pausing to display his new purchases. “Look! Good graphite javelins that I 
did not have previously.”

As the story goes, Nadeem had been using 
the same javelin for nearly eight years 
prior to the upgrade. The episode is a stark 
reminder of the hurdles faced by some 
athletes around the world, and also makes 
Nadeem’s rise from humble beginnings in a 
rural farming community to all-conquering 
athletics champion all the more impressive.

In Pakistan, a country obsessed with team 
sports such as cricket and hockey, Nadeem 
is an anomaly on the sporting landscape. 
His Olympic victory was the nation's first 
ever in an individual sport, and the first 
Olympic gold for Pakistan in any sport in 40 
years.

His unexpected individual triumph at Paris 
2024 made Nadeem an overnight sensation back home, where thousands came

out to greet him upon his return from the 
Games. The adulation was enough to test 
the patience of even the most seasoned 
celebrity athlete, but, true to form, Nadeem 
has remained even-keeled throughout it all.

“I was really honoured by the love, affection 
and honours that the people of Pakistan 
gave me … but none of it has gone to my 
head. I’m still the same old Arshad,” the 
soft-spoken Olympic champion said. “I like 
to care for people, I like to be with my family.

“But it has given me renewed energy to 
work harder and improve my performances. 
You could say my success has fuelled my 
passion.”

Ominous words indeed for the rest of the 
javelin world heading into the next Olympiad.

# Midas touch: Track phenom Tebogo transforms Olympic scholarship into gold

From adversity to advocacy, Botswana’s first
Olympic champion comes full circle

It's fair to say that, having gone from 
struggling to make ends meet to Olympic 
gold medallist in just a few short years, 
Botswana's Letsile Tebogo has made the 
most of his Olympic Solidarity scholarship.

When Tebogo first began receiving 
Olympic Solidarity funding in 2022, he 
was a teenage sprinter with all the talent 
and ambition to one day become a champion. What he lacked was the wherewithal.

On the track, his star was rising. At 17, 
he won gold at the 2021 World U20 
Championships, setting a U20 world 
record in the process. A year later, he 
was crowned African champion, and the 
same year he defended his U20 World 
Championship title, became the first 
Botswanan to break the 10-second barrier 
(9.96) in the 100m, and lowered his U20 
world record even further, this time to 9.91 
seconds.

Off the track, however, he was barely 
getting by. It wasn’t until his Olympic 
Solidarity grant kicked in that his life took 
a dramatic turn for the better.

“Since receiving it, everything has become 
easier, because as an athlete it allows you 
to channel all your focus on training …

not worrying about what you are going to 
eat, what you are going to pay rent with,” 
Tebogo, now 21, said during a visit to 
Olympic House in Lausanne in 2024.

His coach, Kebonyemodisa “Dose” 
Mosimanyane, added: “Letsile was able 
to eat well, sleep well because he had 
accommodation and could buy groceries. 
He could also get to training every day and 
on time because he didn’t struggle much 
with petrol money or transport.”

Importantly, the scholarship also allowed 
Tebogo to attend any competition in 
Botswana or internationally that his coach 
deemed necessary for his progression.

The results quickly followed:

• In 2023: bronze in the 200m and 
silver in the 100m at the 2023 World 
Championships in Budapest.

• In 2024: a national record (9.86) in 
the 100m and gold in the 200m at the 
Olympic Games Paris 2024 – an African 
record (19.46) and the first gold medal 
ever for Botswana.

Tebogo also claimed silver in the 4x400m 
at Paris 2024 alongside teammates

Busang Kebinatshipi and fellow Olympic 
Scholarship-holders Bayapo Ndori and 
Anthony Pesela. Team Botswana lost out 
on gold by one-hundredth of a second 
to Team USA, Tebogo making it possible 
with his relentless pursuit of Rai Benjamin 
over the final leg to set up a sensational 
finish. Remarkably, the average age of the 
Botswana relay team was just 22 at Paris 
2024, meaning there is likely plenty more 
in store from the group on the world’s 
biggest stages.

“With a team comprising young talents, it’s 
promising that in the future we still have 
more chances of dominating in athletics,” 
said Ndori. “The experience gained from 
Paris 2024 combined with the ongoing 
development and support lays a strong 
foundation for the future.”

The same can also be said for Botswanan 
athletes more generally. During the 2021-
2024 period, the NOC of Botswana was a 
recipient of Olympic Solidarity funding as 
part of the Development of the National 
Sports Structure programme. With the 
support, the NOC has set out to strengthen 
and align long-term athlete and coaching development systems in the country 
for the sport of athletics. The main aims 
are to increase the number of athletes 
and qualified coaches, raise awareness 
of and cooperation in delivering athletics, and improve the standard of active 
athletes through increased competition 
opportunities. The hope is that this can 
create a sustainable pathway for all these 
stakeholders.

Not surprisingly, these goals received a 
tremendous boost thanks to the performances of Tebogo and his teammates at 
Paris 2024. “It helped a lot to unite the 
nation,” said Oabona Theetso, Athletics 
Team Manager for Botswana at Paris 2024.

“It has inspired many athletes – they now 
see that it is possible. The number of athletes who register for our track and field 
series has increased significantly to a point 
where we are sometimes forced to [cap the 
number of competitors].”

Tebogo and his teammates are helping put 
the country on the map in other ways, too. 
In 2026, Botswana will become the first 
African nation to host the World Relays, 
while Tebogo, who was named World 
Athletics Athlete of the Year for 2024, will 
also take up the role of World Athletics’ 
global ambassador for Kids’ Athletics.

It’s a fitting honour, as Tebogo has been 
vocal in his commitment to helping others, 
especially those looking to follow in
his footsteps.

“I believe in ‘blessed is the hand that 
gives’: the more you give out, the more you 
receive,” said Tebogo. “So for me, helping 
people is one of the things that’s on the top 
of my mind because I want to see the next 
Letsile, I want to see other athletes benefiting from Olympic Solidarity scholarships.”

## Refugee Athlete Support

The IOC Refugee Olympic Team Paris 
2024 represented more than 100 million 
forcibly displaced people worldwide. Made 
up of 37 athletes, hosted by 15 NOCs and 
competing across 12 sports, the team 
demonstrated to the world that refugees 
are an enrichment to society. The team 
was led by the Chef de Mission, Masomah 
Ali Zada, herself a member of the Refugee 
Olympic Team Tokyo 2020. In the lead-up 
to the Olympic Games Paris 2024, 74 
Refugee Athlete Scholarship-holders were 
on a programme managed by the Olympic 
Refuge Foundation and funded by Olympic 
Solidarity supporting refugee athletes to 
train and compete with the aim of being 
selected to represent the IOC Refugee 
Olympic Team Paris 2024. The most 
notable achievement was the first medal 
ever won by the team, with Cindy Ngamba 
winning bronze in the women’s boxing 
75 kg event.

## Team Support Grant

The Teams Support Grant progamme 
allows NOCs to provide technical and 
financial support for teams to train for and 
take part in regional, continental and world 
competitions, with a view to attempting 
to qualify for the Olympic Games. By the 
time of the Paris 2024 Games, 209 grants 
had been awarded to summer sport teams 
at various development levels. Of these 
teams, 26 qualified for the 2024 Games. 
Among the most notable achievements 
were the gold medals secured by the 
German women’s 3x3 basketball team, 
the Serbian men’s water polo team and 
the men’s handball team from Denmark.

In addition to the Team Support Grants for 
summer sports, 17 were allocated for winter 
sports (curling and ice-hockey) in the years 
prior to 2024, bringing the total of Team 
Support Grants to 226.

# Team players: Belgian Cats provide ultimate alley-oop at Paris 2024

Generational. Inspirational. Sensational.

The Belgium women’s national basketball 
team was all that and more at the Olympic 
Games Paris 2024 – despite not even 
reaching the podium.

The Belgian Cats came agonisingly close 
to medalling at their second Olympic 
appearance, finishing fourth following 
a narrow 85-81 loss to Australia in the 
bronze-medal match.

Influence of women’s basketball team extends
far beyond the court

Disappointing? Sure. But the result was 
also the latest reminder of just how far the 
Cats have progressed in recent years.

“We showed already for a couple of years 
that we really belong,” said Team MVP 
and tournament leading scorer Emma 
Meesseman ahead of the Games. “But to 
be a double Olympian, not just once? At the 
end of [Tokyo 2020], I talked with somebody who was telling me it was never going 
to happen again, that this was something 
that was never going to be repeated. And 
yet here we are four years later.”

In less than a decade, the team has transformed from rank outsiders into serious 
contenders at global competitions.

In 2023, they were crowned European 
champions for the first time after two 
bronze-medal performances in 2017

and 2021. At the 2018 and 2022 FIBA 
World Cups, they placed fourth and fifth, 
respectively.

A recipient of an Olympic Solidarity Team 
Support Grant for the 2021-2024 period, the 
Cats have clearly been putting the funding, 
and that from the Belgian Olympic and 
Interfederal Committee (BOIC), to good use.

The support enabled the team to hire a 
second head coach, ensure the players had 
access to medical staff year-round, and 
organise extra preparatory matches ahead 
of the Games.

Those were the tangibles, the direct benefits to the team. What the Cats gave back 
in return, the intangibles, had a much more 
profound impact: inspiring the Belgian 
Olympic team to its best performance in a 
century, raising the status of women’s basketball at home and around the world, and 
contributing to gender equity at the Games, 
to name but a few.

As one of three teams in the Belgian 
delegation for Paris 2024, the Cats had 
a galvanising effect on the entire squad, 
according to BOIC President Jean-Michel 
Saive. “I could see and feel how important 
it is to have team sports inside the delegation,” said Saive, adding that the country’s 
Olympians had historically excelled at individual sports.

“It brings more dynamic, more self-confidence for the whole team. It really lifts the 
team to another level.”

It certainly did. Team Belgium went on 
to claim its most medals at an Olympic 
Games in 100 years, securing 10 podium 
finishes at Paris 2024, second only to the 
13 it enjoyed at Paris 1924.

Saive added that it was important for the 
Belgian public and partners to see that the 
nation is strong in team sports, not just in 
individual ones.

And the fans are definitely taking notice. 
At a Paris 2024 qualifier in Antwerp, the 
Cats pulled in a crowd of 13,700 people, a 
record for a women’s sports event of any 
kind in Belgium. Following the Games, they 
became the first women’s national team 
to surpass the 10,000-spectator mark in 
EuroBasket qualifyer when they welcomed 
10,614 fans to a game against Lithuania in 
Antwerp.

“The Belgian Cats are a great example 
of what’s possible when talent, success 
and support come together at the right 
time,” said FIBA Europe Executive Director 
Kamil Novak, who cited the team’s historic 
EuroBasket triumph in 2023, a generational 
talent in Emma Meesseman, and a “funto-watch brand of basketball” as factors 
contributing to their popularity.

The Cats also contributed to Paris 2024 
becoming the first-ever gender-equal 
Olympic Games in terms of athletes, while 
helping Team Belgium close its own gender 
gap. The BOIC sent a delegation to Paris 
that was 49.4 percent women – 85 female 
athletes and 87 male athletes – an increase 
from 44.7 per cent at Tokyo 2020 and 35.2 
per cent at Rio 2016.

So, while the Belgian Cats may have fallen 
just shy of their own goals in Paris, they left 
the French capital with their heads held 
high, having delivered a slam dunk in terms 
of legacy in a number of important areas.

## IOC Subsidies for NOCs’ Participation in the Olympic Games and Youth Olympic Games

IOC Subsidies for NOCs’ Participation in 
Olympic Games and Youth Olympic Games 
support NOCs before, during and after each 
Games. For the Gangwon 2024 Winter 
YOG, Olympic Solidarity covered the full 
travel costs for each NOC delegation, along 
with accommodation in the Youth Olympic 
Village. This ensured broad and equitable 
global participation, particularly for smaller 
or less-resourced NOCs.

For the Olympic Games Paris 2024, 
each NOC received financial support 
amounting to USD 20,000 to cover their 
operational and logistical expenses. In 
addition, Olympic Solidarity covered the 
travel and accommodation expenses for 
NOC officials, easing the financial burden 
associated with sending teams to the 
Games. To further assist athlete preparation, each NOC could also receive up 
to USD 15,000 for participation in pre-
Games training camps held in France. 
Following the conclusion of the Games, a 
post-Games subsidy was provided to each 
NOC based on the number of athletes

# Pre-Games training camps give small nations big boost ahead of Paris 2024

French towns prove perfect springboards for ONOC, 
Panam athletes

For larger National Olympic Committees 
(NOCs), organising training camps ahead 
of the Olympic Games is a time-honoured 
tradition. For many smaller NOCs, however, 
the financial and logistical demands that 
such camps require make them largely 
unachievable.

Until recently, that is.

Olympic Solidarity offered pre-games training camp subsidy in its 2017-2020 plan 
under the IOC Subsidy for NOCs participation in the Olympic Games programme. The 
main objective is to prepare Olympians to 
be at their peak physically at the Games. But 
no less important is helping them acclimate 
to the host country, bond with other athletes 
and the local communities, and create a 
positive, lasting legacy for the Games.

Designed to support athletes from smaller 
NOCs, ONOC and Panam Sports pool 
the Pre-Games Training Camp subsidies 
provided by the Olympic Solidarity and 
organised multi-sport pre-games training

# Passing the torch: Athletes Games Services at the Games and the art of the Olympic knowledge transfer

From debriefs to secondments, the NOC Athletes Games 
Services team helps future hosts turn lessons into legacy

On the surface, Olympic Games debriefs 
appear to be a relatively straightforward 
process of inquiry and analysis: What 
worked? What didn’t? And how can things 
be improved for future editions?

But with an undertaking as large and 
complex as the Olympic Games, any such 
transfer of knowledge requires detailed 
planning and effective collaboration to be 
executed successfully.

Over the years, a number of measures have 
been introduced to help future OCOGs 
distil the vast amount of data and information they receive into manageable, 
actionable insights – all tailored to their 
specific editions with the best interests of 
the National Olympic Committees (NOCs) 
and their athletes in mind.

Chief among them are the post-Games 
debriefs. The Paris 2024 Debrief was jointly 
organised by the IOC and the Association 
of National Olympic Committees (ANOC) 
on 21 and 22 November in Lausanne. 
Featuring representatives from a wide 
cross-section of NOCs from different 
continents, the gathering allowed future 
OCOGs to learn not only from the experiences, best practices and issues of Paris 
2024, but also from the feedback the 
NOCs provided on how to rationalise and 
optimise service levels.

It also gave those future OCOGs the 
opportunity to pose vital questions to 
those who lived the experience: 26 NOCs 
representing all continents and a range of 
delegation sizes provided feedback on their 
Paris 2024 journey.

“The debriefs are a really great opportunity for future OCOGs to sit and listen to 
the previous host and IOC,” said Danielle 
Lopez, National Olympic and Paralympic 
Committee Services Director at LA28. “But 
we’re also there to say ‘Wait, when you said 
that, what did you mean by it? Why was 
it an issue? If you could do it again, what 
would you have done differently?’”

Only by probing deeper, Lopez suggests, 
can future OCOGs better assess whether 
a particular issue is relevant to them. 
Contextualisation is key, requiring the right 
questions to be asked at the right time “to 
really leverage the power of the feedback.”

Another transfer-of-knowledge initiative the IOC has implemented during 
Games’ time is the organisation of NOC 
focus groups. NOCs are invited to share 
feedback on topics such as planning, 
pre-Games communications, Games operations, and more. The sessions provide 
real-time insights that are then compiled 
and analysed before recommendations

are made at the conclusion of the final 
meeting.

Similarly, all Chefs de Mission are asked 
to fill in a questionnaire on the different services provided by NOC Athletes 
Games Services, jointly with ANOC, at the 
Games. Feedback is also received from the 
Olympians who participated in Paris via a 
separate questionnaire conducted by the 
IOC Athletes’ Department.

Since London 2012, the Athletes Games 
Services Unit has offered staff from future 
OCOGs the opportunity to work alongside 
the IOC as secondees. The programme 
provides staff from NOC/NPC Relations 
and Services and Village Management 
teams with a real-time opportunity to 
observe the day-to-day activities of the 
Olympic Village and the roles performed 
by an OCOG National Olympic and 
Paralympic Committees Services team.

The programme allows the Athletes Games 
Services Unit to show the secondees 
what is working well and what isn’t, and 
to explain to them how issues arose and 
how they were dealt with, all with an eye 
to avoiding similar occurrences at their 
edition of the Games.

“Working directly with the IOC behind the 
scenes gave me a much deeper understanding of how they operate and how they 
collaborate with NOCs,” said Antonella 
Cerasino, NOC Relations Manager Europe 
and Africa for the Milano Cortina 2026 
Organising Committee. “It was valuable 
because I previously only saw that from 
the outside – it helped me build stronger 
working relationships going forward.”

Familiar as they are with both Village 
operations and their OCOG colleagues, 
the secondees help manage tours with

members of future hosts and Olympic city 
authorities. This is an excellent opportunity 
for them to highlight areas they feel are 
most relevant or pose the greatest risk to 
their edition of the Games.

Cerasino was a secondee in Paris 2024 
for two weeks, where she assisted with 
everything from Opening Ceremony preparations to compiling statistics for the IOC. 
Meeting NOC representatives face-to-face 
was especially beneficial, she said, as the 
interactions gave her a much better understanding of their needs and perspectives.

“I could listen to them directly, understand 
their challenges, and think about how to 
respond in terms of our own edition of the 
Games,” she said. “That’s key to building 
trust and ensuring better service.”

Through debriefs, focus groups, questionnaires and on-the-ground experience, the 
Athletes Games Services Unit has developed a robust and multi-layered system not 
just for transferring Olympic knowledge but 
for transferring understanding as well.

And that’s a victory not only for future 
Olympic Games but for all NOCs and athletes that attend them.

# Empower state of mind: WISH pathway takes aim at gender gap in coaching

## Leadership programme paves way forward for elite female coaches

With an equal number of male and female 
athletes, the Olympic Games Paris 2024 
marked a historic milestone for gender 
equity in sport.

But while significant improvements have 
been made on the field of play, progress 
around it – specifically the athletes’ entourage – continues to lag. In leadership roles 
as well as coaching, a sizeable gender gap 
remains.

Seeking to address this imbalance, 
Olympic Solidarity introduced the Women 
In Sport High-Performance (WISH) 
pathway for the 2021-2024 cycle.

Supported by a USD 1 million budget 
over four years from Olympic Solidarity, 
the programme is designed to empower 
elite female coaches by giving them the 
tools needed to succeed at the highest 
levels of sport. Each programme runs for 
21 months and includes online sessions, 
mentoring activities and a residential week 
at the Institute of Sport at the University of 
Hertfordshire in the United Kingdom.

While participants receive instruction on 
leadership and sport-specific skills, they 
are also taught the importance of fundamentals such as mentoring, networking 
and confidence building. These latter elements, according to the instructors, have

been some of the most rewarding aspects 
of the course for many taking part.

“Sometimes all someone needs is for one 
person to believe in them. WISH gives 
people that belief,” said legendary UK 
rowing coach Rosie Mayglothling, one of 
the pioneers of the project that eventually evolved into the WISH programme. 
“We’re not just ticking boxes. We’re actually 
helping women see what they’re capable 
of and giving them the tools to make it 
happen.”

To date, 120 coaches from 22 sports and 
59 NOCs have benefitted from the WISH 
pathway. Ten WISH alumni had coaching 
roles at Paris 2024 in seven sports.

One of those graduates was Happiness 
Burutu, who was part of the coaching staff 
for the Nigerian wrestling team at Paris 
2024. Burutu said her time on the WISH 
programme had a profound effect on her 
ahead of the Games, instilling her with 
greater confidence that allowed her to 
better communicate with her athletes.

“It provided me with both the knowledge 
and the support I needed to perform at my 
best,” she said. “Leading up to Paris 2024, 
the programme helped in developing me 
not just physically but mentally and emotionally, too.”

Another WISH alumnus that coached at 
Paris 2024 was Ireland’s Noelle Morrissey. 
The high-performance athletics trainer 
says she was so moved by the stories of 
her peers on the programme that it inspired 
her do all she can to support women in elite 
coaching roles going forward.

“The WISH programme opened my eyes to 
the struggles of women in different contexts, 
making me realise that, despite my own challenges, I was relatively fortunate,” she said. 
“I’ve been called ‘lucky’ by male coaches 
instead of being recognised for my skills and 
experience. But after hearing the stories of 
my WISH peers, I’m motivated to become 
an advocate for women in elite coaching — 
something I hadn’t even considered before.”

One of the most powerful elements of 
the programme developed organically: a 
support network was formed between the 
different cohorts and the instructors that 
has quickly become a source of strength for 
the participants. According to WISH Project 
Director Elizabeth Pike, it has the potential to 
be a true game changer.

“What we talk about on the programme 
is for the participants to not only develop

themselves, but also to develop others,” 
said Pike, the Global Winner of the 2024 
IOC Gender Equality, Diversity and 
Inclusion (GEDI) Champions Award. 
“The collaboration has been key. We 
brought the initial four cohorts back together 
again for our Coach Connect programme 
and they’re saying they’ve got a massive 
support network now. They’re learning from 
each other, they’re supporting each other, 
they’re giving each other visibility.”

As in any field undergoing a cultural shift, 
seismic change within the elite coaching 
ranks isn’t expected to happen overnight. 
But with time, hard work, and an ever-expanding support network, progress is 
inevitable.

“Now that we’ve got these amazing graduates who are out there advocating for 
change and demonstrating the positive 
impacts,” Pike concluded, “we will absolutely see changes going forward.”

This all bodes well for an increase in the 
number of female coaches at upcoming 
editions of the Olympic Games as well as 
many other national, continental and international competitions worldwide.

## Advancing governance

With the Olympic Games in mind and 
universality as a core value, our support 
to NOCs implementing basic principles 
of good governance and applying them to 
their preparation for the Games was a key 
element ensuring athletes’ participation.

In 2024, the IOC Institutional Relations 
and Governance Services Unit provided 
such support in various areas. It reviewed 
and updated the statutes of 55 NOCs, 
leading to formal approval by the IOC 
of the statutes of 13 of them by the end 
of 2024. Updating the design of institutional emblems was also on the Unit’s 
list of services, delivered to 12 NOCs. In 
addition, during 2024, and particularly 
after the Olympic Games Paris 2024, 48 
NOCs held their elections, with the results 
being acknowledged by the IOC. The Unit 
monitors these processes closely and 
may have to intervene, when requested 
or when necessary, to make sure that the 
NOC elections take place in accordance 
with the Olympic Charter and the NOCs' 
own statutes. Unfortunately, the year of 
the Olympiad was no stranger to conflict, 
with most cases related to internal governance issues or disputes with government 
authorities.

Among the NOCs that the Unit helped 
to resolve a conflict was the NOC of

Afghanistan. Since the Taliban regime 
seized power in August 2021, women 
and young girls have been dramatically 
deprived of their rights, particularly in 
the areas of education, employment and 
freedom of movement and expression. 
The Taliban authorities have also banned 
them from participating in sports activities. 
This led the IOC to urge the Afghan NOC, 
which operates from exile, and the Afghan 
General Directorate of Physical Education 
and Sports to do everything in their power 
to convince the country’s highest authorities to lift these restrictions. Unfortunately, 
no progress was made. The IOC nonetheless continued to provide direct support 
to Afghan athletes, in particular through 
Olympic Solidarity scholarships for their 
preparations and participation in the 
Games. In view of the Games in Paris, the 
IOC reinforced its ongoing work with the 
NOC of Afghanistan to ensure that female 
athletes would be able to participate. As a 
result, Afghanistan fielded a gender-balanced team in Paris, composed of three 
women competing in athletics and cycling, 
and three men in athletics, swimming, 
and judo. Flagbearers Fariba Hashimi 
and Sha Mahmood Noor Zahi carried the 
pre-Taliban tricolour flag at the Opening 
Ceremony. No Taliban officials were 
allowed at the Games.

In India, the NOC has been facing internal governance issues and disputes for 
a number of years. To secure the participation of Indian athletes in Paris, the IOC 
Institutional Relations and Governance 
Services Unit maintained a constant dialogue with the NOC in the lead-up to the 
Games. The NOC entered a total of 112 
athletes, 46 women and 66 men, and won 
six medals - one silver and five bronze. 
Unfortunately, tensions in the NOC arose 
again soon after the Closing Ceremony 
and the governance issues could not

be resolved despite the IOC’s efforts. In 
October 2024, direct funding from the IOC 
and Olympic Solidarity to the NOC was 
frozen, except that directed specifically to 
the athletes (e.g. Olympic scholarships). 
This measure applies until further notice, 
with the hope that there will be more clarity 
within the NOC as soon as possible for 
the good of the athletes and the Olympic 
Movement in India.

# Adriana Ruano Oliva: Golden gun delivers defining moment for Guatemala at Paris 2024

Provisional lifting of NOC suspension allows
nation to celebrate in style

Standing on top of the medal podium at the 
Olympic Games Paris 2024, Guatemalan 
trap shooter Adriana Ruano Oliva admits to 
being overcome by a mix of emotions.

She had just won her nation’s first Olympic 
gold medal, and first of any colour by a 
Guatemalan woman. For added emphasis, 
she set a new Olympic record in the final to 
secure her medal.

As her country’s flag began to rise into the 
mid-afternoon sky, Ruano Oliva’s initial 
thoughts were of her father, whom she lost 
just three weeks before her first Olympic 
appearance at Tokyo 2020.

But when her national anthem began 
playing for the first time in the 128-year 
history of the modern Olympic Games and 
a small army of Guatemalans on hand to 
support her began belting out the opening 
words – "Guatemala feliz!" or "Happy 
Guatemala!" – Ruano Oliva was suddenly 
consumed by a feeling of gratitude.

“I felt really honoured to give this to my 
country,” the 29-year-old said. “It was an 
amazing feeling.”

Fortunately for Guatemalans the world over, 
the country was afforded the opportunity of

celebrating its greatest Olympic showing 
replete with all its national trappings – team 
colours, flag, anthem. Because things could 
have unfolded much differently.

Only a few months earlier, the NOC of 
Guatemala was mired in a year-and-a-halflong suspension, a measure taken by the 
IOC Executive Board to protect the NOC 
from government interference in its internal governance.

While the suspension was necessary to 
protect the Olympic Movement in the 
country, it nevertheless had a significant 
impact on Guatemalan athletes. They were 
now barred from representing their country 
at Olympic events, including the Games, 
and instead had to compete as neutral 
athletes. No national flag or anthem would 
accompany them on their Olympic journey.

The impact was also acutely felt throughout the NOC, with most employees having 
to cease work as salaries were no longer 
being paid.  “It was a very difficult time 
for our NOC and Guatemalan sport in 
general,” explained Lizza Alonzo, NOC 
Communications Director. “The atmosphere became one of fear, uncertainty and 
sadness. Many people lost their jobs.”

Standing by the NOC throughout, the IOC 
began by rejecting the outcome of the 
elections resulting from the government 
interference in October 2021. It advocated 
for a fair process to support the NOC’s 
efforts to review and approve its statutes 
(December 2021), and for fresh NOC elections to be held in March 2022 under the 
supervision of the IOC and Panam Sports.

In addition to clearly reiterating its position on numerous occasions, the IOC 
maintained an open dialogue with IOCrecognised office-bearers and government 
authorities, while closely monitoring the 
progress of appeals before the constitutional court in Guatemala.

After months of negotiations, including a 
crucial face-to-face meeting in Lausanne 
between the IOC President and Guatemalan

President Bernardo Arévalo, the IOCrecognised office bearers of the NOC 
elected were reinstated on 16 March 2024. 
They were given full authority to lead the NOC 
under IOC-approved NOC statutes from 
December 2021 and the Olympic Charter.

As a result, the suspension was provisionally lifted by the IOC Executive Board on 19 
March 2024, allowing for Guatemalan athletes to participate at Paris 2024 without 
restrictions.

The decision paved the way for Ruano 
Oliva – who began her Olympic journey as a 
volunteer at Rio 2016 and, impressively, also 
volunteered at Paris 2024 after winning her 
gold medal – to write her name into Olympic 
and Guatemalan history, a feat that earned 
her a hero’s welcome upon returning home.

To this day, she still gets stopped in public 
by appreciative strangers.

“ All the people tell me that I made them 
proud that day,” she said. “They just say, 
‘Hey, thank you. Thank you for what you did 
for the country. Thank you because you are 
an inspiration for my kids.’ For me that’s the 
most special thing. ”

# Tracking Triumph – Bahrain Olympic Committee launches National Sports Database

New digital hub poised to shape future
of Bahraini sport

Success for the Bahrain Olympic 
Committee (BOC) came in many forms in 
2024.

Topping the list was the country’s performance at the Olympic Games Paris 
2024, where Bahraini athletes doubled 
the nation’s overall medal tally to eight 
after winning two gold, one silver and one 
bronze.

Setting Bahrain up for future Olympic glory 
was the launch in spring 2024 of the BOC’s 
National Sports Database.

The cornerstone of a broader digital transformation currently under way at the BOC, 
the National Sports Database centralises 
data on athletes, coaches, and officials to 
enable stakeholders to track key metrics 
such as performance, attendance and 
health.

The long-term goal is for the National 
Sports Database to transform decision-making in areas such as training, 
development and the scouting of talent, 
while improving coordination between 
departments and national federations 
to aid in the long-term development of

athletes and preparation for the Olympic 
Games.

“For the National Olympic Committee, 
the database has streamlined athlete 
management, reporting and preparation 
for multi-sport events,” said Mohammed 
Rafiq Bhatti, Head of IT Infrastructure at the 
BOC. “For the athletes, it ensures they are 
visible, their progress is tracked, and their 
development plans are personalised, and 
data-informed. It has also improved communication between athletes, coaches, and 
administrators.”

The switch to a digital hub marks a sea 
change in the way the BOC operates. In the 
past, the NOC kept track of such data using 
a combination of spreadsheets, manual 
records and isolated files across different 
departments, according to Bhatti. “This 
decentralised approach,” he said,” led to 
inconsistencies, duplication of data, and 
difficulties in tracking athlete progress over 
time, especially across different competitions and Olympic cycles.”

The BOC concluded that an upgrade was 
necessary following an internal review of 
its processes in 2020 and a needs-assessment exercise conducted in early

2021. The findings led the BOC to apply 
for an Olympic Solidarity grant in 2021 
through the NOC Management Initiatives 
programme, which is a sub-programme 
of the NOC Administration Development 
programme.

While Olympic Solidarity encourages all 
NOCs to enhance their operations through 
the adoption of IT tools and infrastructure, 
it also cautions that upgrades be done in 
a manageable, sustainable way that corresponds to their individual needs. Rushing 
into such an endeavour can quickly 
become overwhelming in terms of workload, management and expense.

Supported by Olympic Solidarity funding, 
the BOC ensured it had a clear vision and a 
practical blueprint in place from the outset 
to guide the execution of the project. This 
gave the NOC a solid understanding of the 
work, costs and timeline required and provided the flexibility to switch gears when 
necessary, during the implementation 
phase.

Certain issues arose even with the 
roadmap in place. The initial data migration, for example, was hampered by the 
variety of sources and inconsistent formats 
previously used, while stakeholders also 
required time to adjust to the new digital 
workflow.

To address these challenges, the BOC 
implemented onboarding workshops, 
phased system rollouts and established a 
dedicated support team. End users were 
also actively involved to ensure the platform aligned with their practical needs.

From the planning stages to its launch 
on 30 April 2024, the National Sports 
Database project took almost three years 
to materialise. Still in progress are a

public-facing version of the National Sports 
Database portal as well as a fan-engagement 
app, both designed to create stronger 
connections between the athletes and 
public in Bahrain.

Thanks to a number of late nights by the 
in-house IT team and the external developers 
hired for the job, the BOC now has a powerful 
new tool with which to monitor the progress 
and results of athletes and officials over time.

“We’ve been highly successful in achieving 
our objectives” Bhatti said. “The database 
has become the central reference point for 
athlete information, significantly improving 
communication and planning. Our national 
federations now have access to updated 
athlete profiles, and decisions are more 
data-driven than ever before.”

With its National Sports Database now in 
operation, the BOC has laid the groundwork not just for future podium finishes 
but for a smarter, more connected sporting 
ecosystem that will serve Bahrain and its 
athletes for years to come.

# Safeguarding the Games: Welfare Officers play vital role at Paris 2024

Athletes’ well-being at heart of new protective 
measures spanning the globe

Amid the spectacle of over 200 national 
teams vying for gold at the Olympic Games 
Paris 2024, there was another team quietly 
driving the success of the Games behind 
the scenes: the NOC Welfare Officers.

In total, 154 NOC Welfare Officers worked 
in Paris to safeguard the physical, emotional, and mental well-being of athletes 
and staff, playing a vital role in the delivery 
of the Games.

Since Beijing 2022, Welfare Officer 
accreditation is offered to staff members 
of participating NOCs at each Olympic 
Games. To qualify, Welfare Officers must 
either be nationally certified mental health 
professionals or internationally certified 
safeguarding practitioners.

Many of the Welfare Officers in Paris 
were graduates of the IOC Certification: 
Safeguarding Officer in Sport programme 
funded by Olympic Solidarity’s Olympic 
Values programme. By the end of 2024, 
some 343 safeguarding officers from over 
99 countries had officially been certified 
through the programme.

One graduate who went on to play an 
important role in Paris was Sefanaia 
Takape, Sports Education and 
Safeguarding Officer at the Nauru Olympic 
Committee. As a Scholarship-holder for 
the 2021-2024 period, Takape sharpened

his competencies in the prevention of and 
response to harassment and abuse in sport 
during the seven-month course, which featured seven online workshops. Separately, 
Safe Sport workshops were held in Fiji in 
2023 and in Guam in 2024.

As part of the Oceania National Olympic 
Committees (ONOC)’s shared services 
impetus for Paris 2024, in which 15 Pacific 
Island nations under the ONOC umbrella 
benefitted from the communal use of 
expertise in different areas of the Olympic 
Games mission, Takape was one of three 
Welfare Officers tasked with overseeing 
the well-being of athletes and staff for the 
entire region in Paris.

At the Games, he interacted daily with 
Olympians and ONOC staff within the 
Athletes’ Lounge and on the sidelines of 
competitions. As a member of the safeguarding community of practice in Paris, 
he also attended numerous workshops and 
lectures facilitated by the IOC on topics 
selected to address current challenges and 
opportunities in safeguarding.

Takape said the most impactful moment for

to somehow remove that culture of silence 
and ensure that Oceania athletes compete 
at their best and have podium finishes 
more often at Games to come.”

The Welfare Officers were part of a larger 
suite of safeguarding initiatives in place 
at Paris 2024. Other measures included 
a mental health hotline and the first-ofits kind Athlete365 Mind Zone, a space 
of tranquility within the Olympic Village 
where athletes could relax, disconnect and 
get mentally prepared for competition. A 
Safe Sport Zone was integrated within the 
MindZone as a safe space where athletes 
could consult trained IOC staff for safeguarding assistance and mental health 
support. All of these services were rated 
highly by the athletes and Chefs de Mission 
at the conclusion of the Games.

Beyond the Games, global attention on 
athlete welfare is also on the rise, with 
NOCs, International Federations and other 
sports organisations showing a growing 
commitment to safeguarding. Eighty-four 
NOCs now have trained safeguarding 
officers, for example, and the establishment 
of safeguarding hubs worldwide further 
underscores this expanding priority.

As the Olympic Movement looks to the 
future, the presence of Welfare Officers at 
the Olympic Games and other safeguarding initiatives are no longer just a luxury, 
they are essential for the protection of athletes everywhere.

## Olympism365

In 2024, the IOC continued to advance 
its mission to use sport as a catalyst for 
sustainable development, aligned with the 
United Nationsʼ Sustainable Development 
Goals (SDGs). Through Olympism365, the 
IOC’s strategic framework for sport and 
social impact, the IOC supported over 550 
initiatives across 176 countries, engaging 
millions worldwide.

The Olympism365 strategy spans three 
pillars:

1. 	Thematic portfolios of sport for social 
development projects delivered in 
collaboration with global sport, health 
and social development organisations, 
including UN entities, public development banks, NGOs and others. 
To date 29 global collaborators and 
141 local implementing organisations 
are working with the IOC across 19 
multi-stakeholder consortia in 102 
countries;

2. 	Direct investment in NOCs through 
Olympic Solidarity’s Olympic Values 
programme. In 2024, 128 NOCs 
ran close to 300 projects to champion Olympic values. Notably the 
Campeonas en Movimiento project, 
led by the NOC of Paraguay, using 
football to empower girls with life 
skills, safeguarding and employability 
training. With the support of Olympic 
Solidarity, the project’s scope has 
been expanded to offer training workshops to 120 coaches and leaders on 
inclusive and gender equitable sports 
practices, as well as digital skills and 
entrepreneurship.

3. 	IOC-led signature initiatives including 
IOC Young Leaders, Olympic Values

Education Programme, Athletes 
Giving Back, P&G Athletes for Good, 
Olympafrica O!Yes programme and 
Let’s Move.

Olympism365 thematic portfolio areas 
include:

• Sport, Education and Livelihoods: 
Empowering 50,000+ youth in Latin 
America, the Caribbean, and Africa 
through skills training and access to education and jobs.

• Sport, Health and Active Communities: 
Promoting physical and mental well-being 
through community sport and physical 
activity programmes for up to one million 
people in 5 countries, and capacity 
building for 1,000 health and sport professionals and 250 organisations across 
50 countries.

• Sport, Equality and Inclusive 
Communities: Advancing gender equality and inclusion, including funding and 
technical support for 21 projects in 13 
countries across Oceania in 2024 through 
the Oceania Impact Network Play for 
Equity Fund.

• Sport, Peace and Safe Communities:  
Strengthening the role of sport in youth 
crime prevention strategies including 
through capacity building and demonstrative community projects in Indonesia, The 
Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam.

• Urban Development: Mainstreaming 
sport in urban planning processes as 
a catalyst for healthier, more inclusive 
and sustainable cities, including through 
training for 125 local government and 
community development organisations.

Innovation was a key focus in 2024, with a 
first set of sport and Olympism-based initiatives funded through the Olympism365 
Innovation Hub to deliver social impact 
in targeted communities. The IOC also 
continued to strengthen the global 
sport-for-development ecosystem through 
capacity building initiatives for practitioners and orgainsations including on impact 
measurement, and sport-for-development 
policy research.

# Championing change: IOC

# Young Leaders win over

# Olympic fans at Paris 2024

If bright ideas were awarded medals at the 
Olympic Games, the concept behind the 
creation of the Champions Park would have 
been a shoo-in for gold at Paris 2024.

Not far behind was the decision – another 
masterstroke – to place the IOC’s Young 
Leaders’ booth inside said park.

Youth-managed booth inside Champions Park gives 
new voice to the IOC’s Olympism365 strategy

Located at the Place du Trocadéro at the 
foot of the Eiffel Tower, the Champions 
Park was open free of charge to the general 
public, who came in droves during the 
Games to celebrate in person with Olympic 
medallists and to take in the day’s sporting 
action at boisterous public viewing parties.

With over a quarter of a million people filing 
through the gates of the Park during the 
nine days it was operational, there wasn’t 
a better location anywhere in France for 
the IOC Young Leaders to engage with the 
public.

Their mission? “Primarily to spread the 
message of Olympism365 – that sport 
is not just for a particular time of year 
or during sports events,” said Grace 
Towobola, IOC Young Leader from Nigeria. 
“Sport can be used for development at any 
point in time by anybody, every day.”

The choice to place the booth in a public 
space inside the Champions Park helped

deliver the IOC's highest-profile activation 
targeting Olympic fans at Paris 2024.

“I think we really benefitted from this 
atmosphere, being in the Park,” said Paul 
Bayet, IOC Young Leader from France and 
a Paris 2024 volunteer. “We were probably 
their only face-to-face touchpoint with the 
Olympics and the Olympic Movement, 
and they were likely to remember the 
interaction.”

Nine Young Leaders worked in shifts at 
the booth to promote the Olympic values 
and showcase the positive impact sport 
can have on society. Their work supported 
wider efforts throughout the Games to 
showcase the IOC as an organisation 
committed to youth empowerment and the 
contribution of sport to the UN SDGs.

Visitors took part in a range of activities 
designed to demonstrate how sport can 
be used as a tool for development in key 
areas such as equality, inclusion, health and 
well-being, sustainability and education.

They included interactive exercises, sustainable handcrafting workshops and Q&A 
sessions with Olympians, Paralympians 
and other notable figures, including 
Nobel Peace Prize Laureate Professor 
Muhammad Yunus.

## Olympic Refuge Foundation

The Olympic Refuge Foundation (ORF) is 
an independent international non-profit 
organisation that helps provide access to 
safe sport for young people affected by displacement, operating at the crossroads of 
sport, development, and humanitarian aid. 
Since its founding, the work of the ORF has 
contributed to almost one million young 
people affected by displacement experiencing safe sport. In 2024, the ORF ran 
programmes in nine countries: Bangladesh, 
Burkina Faso, Burundi, Colombia, France, 
Jordan, Kenya, Türkiye and Uganda.

One of its key initiatives is the Sport 
Coach+ programme, which is delivered in 
partnership with the Red Cross and Red 
Crescent Movement mental health and 
psychosocial support Hub and funded in 
collaboration with the IOC and Olympic 
Solidarity through a USD 569,000 
Ukraine emergency response fund. This

programme has been expanded across 
seven countries impacted by the Ukrainian 
displacement crisis and aims to train 2,500 
coaches to provide trauma-informed care 
and psychosocial support through sport, 
helping to rebuild lives and foster healing in 
affected communities.
