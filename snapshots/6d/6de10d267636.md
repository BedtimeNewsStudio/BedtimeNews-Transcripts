# Ramokgopa unveils R2.23trn electricity generation plan

Leaves door open to expand nuclear to 10 000MW.

[![Antoinette Slabbert](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Antoinette-Slabbert-e1429175781323-75x75.jpg)](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/author/antoinette-slabbert/ "View more articles by Antoinette Slabbert")[Antoinette Slabbert](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/author/antoinette-slabbert/ "View more articles by Antoinette Slabbert") · [20 Oct 2025](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/2025/10/20/ "View all articles from 20 Oct 2025 on Moneyweb"), 04:04

[![SA is not ‘abandoning coal’ says Kgosientsho Ramokgopa, while stressing the need to control emissions. Image: Department of Electricity and Energy Facebook page](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Ramokgopa-IRP-2025--555x370.jpg)](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Ramokgopa-IRP-2025--1024x683.jpg "SA is not ‘abandoning coal’ says Kgosientsho Ramokgopa, while stressing the need to control emissions. SA is not ‘abandoning coal’ says Kgosientsho Ramokgopa, while stressing the need to control emissions. Image: Department of Electricity and Energy Facebook page

Minister of Electricity and Energy Kgosientsho Ramokgopa announced an ambitious plan on Sunday to build 105 000MW of new generation capacity between now and 2039 to the value of R2.23 trillion.

This is more than double Eskom’s current generation capacity of 50 230MW.

Read: [SA unveils nuclear ambitions in new energy plan](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/south-africa/sa-unveils-nuclear-ambitions-in-new-energy-plan/)

The plan will ensure energy security to support economic growth of at least 3% from 2030, lay the foundations for a revival of the struggling construction industry, and be a lever for industrialisation, Ramokgopa said.

It will also map South Africa’s road towards net-zero emissions by 2050.

His announcement follows the approval of the country’s Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) by cabinet on 15 October. It is expected to be published in the Government Gazette on Friday 24 October.

> The plan sets out government policy in terms of the size and technology of planned new power stations.

It provides for a drastic increase in renewable energy, with the contribution of coal being reduced from the current 58% to 27% by 2039.

![](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Pic-1-1.png)

Source: Department of Electricity and Energy

The plan provides for the addition of 11 270MW of solar photovoltaic (PV) and 7 340MW of wind capacity by 2030, as well as 6 000MW of gas-to-power, 3 100MW of storage and 5 400MW of distributed energy (behind the meter).

Due to the long lead time, there is no provision for nuclear within this timeline and no new coal.

Towards 2039, the plan relies heavily on 16 000MW of gas-to-power and provides for an additional 5 200MW of nuclear generation to balance the intermittent renewables.

![](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Picture-1-1.png)

Source: Department of Electricity and Energy

The door has been left open to increase nuclear provision to 10 000MW, which will be further explored in a Nuclear Industrialisation Plan to determine the minimum capacity to support the development of the entire nuclear fuel cycle value chain within the country.

According to the plan, South Africa’s energy mix will be greatly transformed by 2039 to consist of: 27% coal generation, 24% wind, 18% solar PV, and 11% gas-to-power. Distributed generation, which is expected to be largely rooftop solar, will make up 8% of capacity, with nuclear contributing 5%, storage (mostly batteries) adding 4%, and pumped storage 3%. The rest will be made up of several small contributions from other energy sources.

> Ramokgopa said the plan will be funded by the private sector, off government’s balance sheet.

Government “will be the offtaker” of the electricity, and it will be a contingent liability for the state. He vowed that government procurement will not be stop-start in nature and mentioned the possibility of mega procurement rounds.

**Policy adjustments**

Government has made several policy adjustments to the least-cost scenario, which include:

- Reliance on an Eskom energy availability factor of more than 60%;
- Increasing the minimum load factor of the initial gas-to-power projects (6 000MW) to 50% to ensure security of supply and the viability of gas processing infrastructure – the load factor may be adjusted later;
- The construction by Eskom of a clean coal demonstration plant by 2030;
- Development of a Nuclear Industrialisation Plan to determine the minimum capacity to reach the economies of scale for the entire nuclear fuel cycle value chain within the country;
- Ensuring effective implementation and monitoring of the South African Renewable Energy Industrialisation Masterplan (SAREM); and
- Considering a “mega bid window” to fast-track industrialisation, localisation and job creation.

Ramokgopa emphasised that government is not “abandoning coal”. He said in developing the IRP, South Africa’s domestic conditions took precedence over international commitments. This includes the country’s rich coal endowments, “but the question is how to control emissions”.

Read: [Eskom seeks solar buyers in shift away from coal](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/south-africa/eskom-seeks-solar-buyers-in-shift-away-from-coal/ "Eskom seeks solar buyers in shift away from coal")

He added that the plan does not include green hydrogen, because “science still \[has\] to resolve the cost”. He said if the technology becomes a viable option, the IRP will be updated accordingly.

The minister acknowledged the risk of not having the 6 000MW of gas-to-power generation operational by 2030, especially in light of depleting regional gas resources and a lack of regasification infrastructure for imports. The lead time for getting new turbines is three to four years, he said.

Gas generation is however critical to close the baseload gap that will be left after the planned closure of 8 000MW of old coal generation plants. He said 2 000MW is already in the market but acknowledged the urgency of procurement decisions in this regard.

If need be, the country’s existing four open-cycle gas turbines that are currently running on diesel may be converted to use gas instead. Since these plants have been running for years, this could save time as opposed to building new plants right away, Ramokgopa said.

Regarding nuclear, he pointed to a worldwide move back to nuclear as a clean energy to replace fossil fuels and said 14 of the world’s top financing institutions, including the World Bank, have indicated willingness to fund nuclear programmes.

> While Ramokgopa stressed how important it is for consumers to be able to afford electricity, it was not within the scope of the IRP to model the electricity price path.

A second and third volume of the IRP will be published by the end of March next year to address the system operations and transmission capacity respectively.
