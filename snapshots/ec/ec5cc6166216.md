# The trouble with turning discontent into votes in Nigeria

**BY LEKAN OLAYIWOLA**

**In Nigeria, public discontent runs counter to electoral power. Anger fills conversations, surveys and protests, yet rarely alters the arithmetic of elections. Critics point to 2015, when Goodluck Jonathan lost, but that defeat owed less to sentiment than to elite defections and a unified opposition machine.**

The deeper puzzle is why dislike so seldom converts into ballots. Elections hinge on turnout, mobilisation, coordination and elite machinery, not mood alone. In 2023, fewer than three in ten registered voters cast a ballot, and barely one in eleven chose the president — a gap that defines Nigeria’s democracy.

**When anger that remains at home**

Advertisement

The 2023 presidential election illustrates Nigeria’s denominator problem. Out of 93.47 million registered voters, only 24.97 million cast ballots — a turnout of 26.72%, with INEC’s measure at 27.05%. Bola Tinubu secured 8.79 million votes, 36.6 per cent of valid ballots. Against the registered electorate, that is roughly 9.4%— about one in eleven Nigerians.

This exposes a structural feature of Nigerian elections where governments can be disliked by large majorities and still prevail because contests are decided not by the population as a whole but by those who turn up, are mobilised, and survive the arithmetic of plurality. National surveys capture a universe far larger than the one that counts. The three-quarters who stayed home expressed no preference at the booth. Anger that remains at home does not defeat an incumbent; only organised votes do.

**What happens when sentiment is accompanied by organisation?**

Advertisement

The 2015 election shows what happens when sentiment is matched by organisational realignment. In 2013, five governors defected from the ruling People’s Democratic Party to the newly formed All Progressives Congress. Two years later, Muhammadu Buhari defeated incumbent Goodluck Jonathan by 15.42 million votes to 12.85 million, the first time a Nigerian president lost reelection. It is tempting to treat defections as the cause, but one analysis found that even if those five states had voted differently, Buhari would still have won. Their significance lay in signalling coalition strength and mobilisation capacity.

Today the indicator points the other way. As of March 2026, the APC controlled 31 of 36 governorships, leaving the PDP with Oyo and Bauchi. Governors command structures that influence local mobilisation and logistics. While this does not mean they can mechanically deliver votes, it does shape conversion capacity. In 2015, machinery moved against the incumbent; in 2026, much of it sits behind him.

**The other side of the equation is opposition coordination**

INEC’s final list for 2027 contains 18 presidential candidates. Atiku Abubakar is running under the ADC with Rotimi Amaechi as his running mate, while Peter Obi contests on the NDC ticket with Rabiu Kwankwaso. The striking irony is that Amaechi and Kwankwaso were among the governors who defected from the PDP in 2013, helping to build the coalition that defeated Jonathan. In 2027, they are diverging with different tickets.

Advertisement

Each candidate pursues the best individual path. But collectively, fragmentation creates a mathematical problem. In a plurality system, the winner does not need majority support, only more votes than any rival. That distinction was decisive in 2023, when Tinubu won with 36%. For the opposition, dissatisfaction is raw material. It becomes politically valuable only when enough voters are persuaded to unite behind one candidate, turn out, and remain coordinated until the count.

**Why both sides resist the uncomfortable argument**

It is easier to attribute defeat entirely to manipulation than to confront organisational weakness. But neither explanation should be assumed in advance. The presidency can point to reforms — subsidy removal, foreign-exchange reform and the student-loan system — as evidence that difficult changes were undertaken for longer-term economic reasons. Opposition parties can point to household costs, insecurity and living conditions as evidence that reforms have imposed burdens without yet delivering sufficient relief.

The presidency has obvious reasons to reject the description of an “unpopular winner”. A mandate obtained from a relatively small proportion of the registered electorate is politically vulnerable to the suggestion that it rests more on fragmentation and mobilisation than broad enthusiasm. The opposition has an equally uncomfortable problem. If millions of Nigerians are dissatisfied with the government and the opposition nevertheless loses, then dissatisfaction alone cannot explain the outcome. The opposition must account for its own inability to convert sentiment into votes.

**Dislike is not a voting system**

The real question is not whether Nigerians dislike Tinubu. Surveys can measure that, although the quality of the answer depends on the sample, timing and question. The substantial issue is how much of that sentiment can be converted into an electoral majority. That requires at least four things to happen.

Advertisement

Dissatisfied citizens must be willing and able to vote. They must choose among competing alternatives. Opposition forces must prevent their votes from being dispersed beyond the reach of a plurality victory. And the electoral system must translate the ballots cast into an outcome that citizens broadly accept as credible. Failure at any stage weakens the relationship between public sentiment and political power.

This also explains why a credible poll of likely voters would be considerably more revealing than a general measure of presidential approval. A survey can tell us how Nigerians feel. It does not necessarily tell us who will overcome the considerable distance between feeling and voting.

**The larger problem is not the incumbent**

When turnout is low, political organisation is disproportionately valuable. When opposition parties are fragmented, dissatisfaction is divided among alternatives. When state-level political machinery is concentrated in the ruling party, mobilisation capacity is uneven. And when citizens become cynical about politics, dissatisfaction can produce withdrawal rather than participation.

Advertisement

Yet, electoral change is impossible. A strong opposition coalition could alter the equation just as a major increase in turnout could alter it. A substantial change in economic or security conditions could turn the tide as well as a political crisis within the ruling coalition. Even the most formidable political machine can fail if its assumptions about voters prove wrong. But discontent is an input, not an electoral outcome. It becomes an outcome only through conversion.

**The question for 2027**

Nigeria’s elections are not verdicts on national mood; they are contests over whether mood can be converted into participation. The decisive questions for 2027 are not about affection or dislike for the president, but about mobilisation in terms of who can bring citizens who usually stay home into the polling booth, who can coordinate a fragmented opposition, and who can turn state-level machinery into turnout. Until that conversion reflects more of the population, a government can be widely disliked and still prevail, not because Nigerians chose it, but because its rivals failed to organise discontent into power.

The clear policy challenge is that anger without ballots is politically impotent. To rally the millions who abstain, parties must invest in grassroots organisation, reduce barriers to access, and persuade citizens that participation is worth the effort and risk. Only when dissatisfaction is channelled into coordinated turnout will elections reflect the breadth of Nigeria’s democracy. Otherwise, silence remains indistinguishable from consent.
