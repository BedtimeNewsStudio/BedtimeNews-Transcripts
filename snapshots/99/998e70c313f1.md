**Hans Berger** (21 May 1873 – 1 June 1941) was a German psychiatrist. He is best known as the inventor of [electroencephalography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography "Electroencephalography") (EEG) in 1924, which is a method used for recording the electrical activity of the brain, commonly described in terms of [brainwaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_oscillation "Neural oscillation"), and as the discoverer of the [alpha wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_wave "Alpha wave") rhythm which is a type of brainwave.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3) Alpha waves have been eponymously referred to as the "Berger wave".[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4)

## Biography

Berger was born in [Neuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coburg "Coburg") (now part of [Coburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coburg "Coburg")), [Saxe-Coburg and Gotha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxe-Coburg_and_Gotha "Saxe-Coburg and Gotha"), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire "German Empire").

After attending [Casimirianum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimirianum_Coburg "Casimirianum Coburg"), where he gained his [abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur "Abitur") in 1892, Berger enrolled as a mathematics student at the [Friedrich Schiller University of Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schiller_University_of_Jena "Friedrich Schiller University of Jena") with the intention of becoming an astronomer. After one semester, he abandoned his studies and enlisted for a year of service in the cavalry. During a training exercise, his horse suddenly reared, and he landed in the path of a horse-drawn cannon. The driver of the artillery battery halted the horses in time, leaving the young Berger shaken but with no serious injuries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5) His sister, at home many kilometres away, had a feeling he was in danger and insisted their father telegram him. The incident made such an impression on Berger that, years later in 1940, he wrote: "It was a case of spontaneous telepathy in which at a time of mortal danger, and as I contemplated certain death, I transmitted my thoughts, while my sister, who was particularly close to me, acted as the receiver."[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-6)

On completion of his military service, and obsessed by the idea of how his mind could have carried a signal to his sister, Berger returned to Jena to study medicine with the goal of discovering the physiological basis of "psychic energy."[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-7) His central theme became "the search for the correlation between objective activity in the brain and subjective psychic phenomena."[&#8202;](#ref-back-8-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-8)

After obtaining his medical degree from Jena in 1897, Berger joined the staff of [Otto Ludwig Binswanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Binswanger "Otto Binswanger") (1852–1929), who held the chair in [psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry") and [neurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology "Neurology") at the Jena clinic. Habilitated in 1901, he qualified as a senior university lecturer in 1906 and physician-in-chief in 1912, eventually succeeding Binswanger in 1919.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Wiedemann_1994-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-Wiedemann_1994) He also collaborated with two famous scientists and physicians, [Oskar Vogt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Vogt "Oskar Vogt") (1870–1959) and [Korbinian Brodmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korbinian_Brodmann "Korbinian Brodmann") (1868–1918), in their research on [lateralization of brain function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateralization_of_brain_function "Lateralization of brain function"). Berger married his technical assistant, Baroness Ursula von Bülow, in 1911 and later served as an army psychiatrist on the Western front during World War I.[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10) He was elected Rector of Jena University in 1927.

In 1924, Berger succeeded in recording the first human [electroencephalogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalogram "Electroencephalogram") (EEG), a term he coined.[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11) Filled with doubt, he took five years to publish his first paper in 1929, which demonstrated the technique for "recording the electrical activity of the human brain from the surface of the head."[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12) His findings were met with incredulity and derision by the German medical and scientific establishments.[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13) Having visited the EEG laboratory at Jena in 1935, American [roboticist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roboticist "Roboticist") [William Grey Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Grey_Walter "William Grey Walter") noted that Berger:

> ... was not regarded by his associates as in the front rank of German psychiatrists, having rather the reputation of being a crank. He seemed to me to be a modest and dignified person, full of good humour, and as unperturbed by lack of recognition as he was later by the fame it eventually brought upon him. But he had one fatal weakness: he was completely ignorant of the technical and physical basis of his method. He knew nothing about mechanics or electricity.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14)

After British [electrophysiologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophysiologists "Electrophysiologists") [Edgar Douglas Adrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Douglas_Adrian "Edgar Douglas Adrian") and [B. H. C. Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Matthews "Bryan Matthews") confirmed Berger's basic observations in 1934, the importance of his discoveries in [electroencephalography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography "Electroencephalography") (EEG) was finally recognized at an international forum in 1937.[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-15) By 1938, electroencephalography had gained widespread recognition by eminent researchers in the field, leading to its practical use in diagnosis in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"), and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Wiedemann_1994-1)[\[9\]](#refnote-Wiedemann_1994)
In 1938, at the retirement age of 65, Berger was made [Professor Emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_Emeritus "Professor Emeritus") in [Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"). According to biographers Niedermeyer and Lopes da Silva, the appointment occurred in an unceremonious manner as his relationship with the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") regime was particularly strained.[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-16) Numerous sources report that, given their hostile relationship, the Nazis forced Berger into retirement that same year with a complete ban of any further work on EEG.[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17) These biographical accounts were contradicted in 2005 by [Ernst Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Klee "Ernst Klee"), a German journalist specializing in the exposure and documentation of Nazi medical crimes.[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18) In 2005, Dr. Susanne Zimmermann, medical historian at the University of Jena, found evidence that Berger had not been forced into retirement but had "served on the selection committee for his successor"[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19) Berthold Kihn, who was sacked as a Nazi after the war. Moreover, official records at the University of Jena dating from the 1930s proved that Berger had served on the _Erbgesundheitsgericht_ (Court for Genetic Health) that imposed sterilizations, while his diaries contained anti-Semitic comments.[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-20) Dr Zimmermann's findings corroborated research published in Germany in 2003, documenting Berger's invitation by the SS racial hygienist [Karl Astel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Astel "Karl Astel") to work for the EGOG ( _Erbgesundheitsobergericht_, [Higher Genetic Health Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Genetic_Health_Court "Higher Genetic Health Court")) in 1941. Berger replied: "I am gladly willing to work again as an assessor at the Court for Genetic Health in Jena, for which I thank you."[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-21) Berger did not join the [SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutzstaffel "Schutzstaffel"), SA, or Nazi Party "despite the significant Nazification of the University of Jena, but was a supporting SS member, possibly for self-protection."[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22)

After a long period of clinical depression, and suffering from a severe skin infection,[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23) Berger died from [suicide by hanging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide_by_hanging "Suicide by hanging") on June 1, 1941, in the southern wing of the clinic.

## Research

Among his many research interests in [neurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology "Neurology"), Berger studied brain [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system "Circulatory system"), [psychophysiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysiology "Psychophysiology"), and brain [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature "Temperature"). One of his early experiments on the brain involved a gentleman with a cranial defect that left part of the brain exposed. The gentleman allowed Berger to insert a liquid-filled rubber tube through the hole in his skull. A latex cap sealed the opening, creating a pressure gauge. At the other end of the rubber tube was a pen that recorded the pressure fluctuations on paper wrapped around a rotating drum. Berger noticed that the pen recorded waves that would change when he asked his conscious participant to perform different cognitive tasks. He also observed changes when the participant experienced changes in emotions or sensory stimulation. Berger followed this work with his most significant contribution to modern science.[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24) British physician, [Richard Caton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Caton "Richard Caton") (1842-1926), had previously described electrical potentials recorded from the exposed cortices of dogs and nonhuman apes. Berger's patient gave him the opportunity to apply Caton's technique to a human. The result was the first demonstration of human [electroencephalography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalography "Electroencephalography") (EEG). In 1924, Berger made the first EEG recording of human brain activity and called it _Elektrenkephalogramm_.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7e/1st-eeg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1st-eeg.png) An early EEG recording done by Berger

Using the EEG, he was also the first to describe the different waves or rhythms that were present in the normal and abnormal brain, such as the [alpha wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_wave "Alpha wave") rhythm (7.812 to 13.28Hz), also known as "Berger's wave"; and its suppression (substitution by the faster [beta waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_wave "Beta wave")) when the subject opens the eyes (the so-called _alpha blockade_). He also studied and described for the first time the nature of EEG alterations in brain diseases such as [epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilepsy "Epilepsy").

His method involved inserting [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver "Silver") wires under the patients [scalp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalp "Scalp"), one at the front of the head and one at the back. Later, he used silver foil electrodes attached to the head by a rubber bandage. As a recording device, he first used the [Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Lippmann "Gabriel Lippmann") [capillary electrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_electrometer "Capillary electrometer"), but results were disappointing. He then switched to the string [galvanometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanometer "Galvanometer") and later to a double-coil [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_AG "Siemens AG") [recording galvanometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_galvanometer?action=edit&redlink=1 "Recording galvanometer"), which allowed him to record electrical [voltages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage "Voltage") as small as one ten-thousandth of a volt. The resulting output, up to three seconds in duration, was then photographed by an assistant.

The EEG has been a useful tool in modern medical and psychological practices by allowing clinicians to describe and diagnose various conditions and diseases. If an individual goes to get an EEG, the doctor will look at the waveform patterns to determine how different stimuli affect the brain. These waveform patterns are called [evoked potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evoked_potentials "Evoked potentials") or EPs. If a patient's brain waves do not follow the normal EP pattern, that is an indication of a deficit in the processing of the stimuli. There are also [event-related potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event-related_potentials "Event-related potentials"), or ERPs, which provide information about mental processing of patients. Both EPs and ERPs are tools that can help doctors determine any abnormalities. The studies researching ERPs have also been helpful in the development of the [brain mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_mapping "Brain mapping") technique.[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-25)

## Hans-Berger-Preis

A prize awarded triennially by the _[Deutsche Gesellschaft für Klinische Neurophysiologie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Gesellschaft_f%C3%BCr_Klinische_Neurophysiologie?action=edit&redlink=1 "Deutsche Gesellschaft für Klinische Neurophysiologie")_ (German Society of Clinical Neurophysiology) for long-standing, extensive academic work in theoretical or clinical [neurophysiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurophysiology "Neurophysiology") was named after Berger: the _Hans-Berger-Preis_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-26-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-26)[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-27) In 2021, however, in part due to Berger's and other namesakes' work's associations with the Nazi regime (e.g., [Gottlob Berger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlob_Berger "Gottlob Berger")), the society decided to rename all its awards to a purely factual description: The Hans-Berger-Preis is now the "Medal of Merit for Lifetime Achievement in Neurophysiology and Functional Imaging".[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28)
