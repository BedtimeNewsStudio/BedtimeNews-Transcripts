The **Pershing II Weapon System**[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[a\]](#refnote-3) was a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-fuel_rocket "Solid-fuel rocket")-fueled [two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket "Multistage rocket")-stage [medium-range ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium-range_ballistic_missile "Medium-range ballistic missile") designed and built by [Martin Marietta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Marietta "Martin Marietta") to replace the [Pershing 1a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pershing_1a "Pershing 1a") Field Artillery Missile System as the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army "United States Army")'s primary nuclear-capable theater-level weapon. The U.S. Army replaced the Pershing 1a with the Pershing II Weapon System in 1983, while the [German Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Air_Force "German Air Force") retained Pershing 1a until all Pershings were eliminated in 1991. The U.S. Army [Missile Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Aviation_and_Missile_Command "United States Army Aviation and Missile Command") (MICOM) managed the development and improvements, while the [Field Artillery Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_Artillery_Branch_(United_States) "Field Artillery Branch (United States)") deployed the systems and developed tactical doctrine.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-4)

## Development

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Development")\]

Development began in 1973 for an updated Pershing. The Pershing 1a had a 400kt warhead, which was greatly over-powered for the [Quick Reaction Alert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quick_Reaction_Alert "Quick Reaction Alert") (QRA) tactical role the weapon system filled. Reducing warhead yield, however, required a significant increase in accuracy to match Pershing 1a's ability to kill hard targets like command bunkers. The contract went to Martin Marietta in 1975 with the first development launches in 1977. The first launch took place on July 22, 1982 from [Launch Complex 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Canaveral_Launch_Complex_16 "Cape Canaveral Launch Complex 16") at [Cape Canaveral Air Force Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Canaveral_Space_Force_Station "Cape Canaveral Space Force Station").[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-5) Pershing II was to use the new [W85 warhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W85_(nuclear_warhead) "W85 (nuclear warhead)") with a 5 to 80kt [variable yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_yield "Variable yield") or an earth-penetrator [W86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W86 "W86") warhead as [nuclear bunker buster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_bunker_buster "Nuclear bunker buster"). The warhead was packaged in a [maneuverable reentry vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maneuverable_reentry_vehicle "Maneuverable reentry vehicle") (MARV) with active radar guidance and would use the existing rocket motors. Requests from [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel") to buy the new Pershing II were rejected in 1975.[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-6)

The Soviet Union began deployment of the [RSD-10 Pioneer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSD-10_Pioneer "RSD-10 Pioneer") (NATO designation SS-20 Saber) in 1976. Since the first version of the RSD-10 had a range of 2,700 miles (4,300km) and two warheads, the Pershing II requirement was changed to increase the range to 900 miles (1,400km), giving the ability to reach targets in eastern [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic "Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic"), [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic "Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republic") or [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic "Lithuanian Soviet Socialist Republic"). The [NATO Double-Track Decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Double-Track_Decision "NATO Double-Track Decision") was made to deploy both the medium-range Pershing and the longer-range, but slower [BGM-109G Ground Launched Cruise Missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BGM-109G_Ground_Launched_Cruise_Missile "BGM-109G Ground Launched Cruise Missile") (GLCM) to strike potential targets farther to the east. The Pershing II with the longer-range motors was initially referred to as Pershing II Extended Range (PIIXR), then reverting to Pershing II.[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-7)

Both the hard-target capability and W86 nuclear warhead were cancelled in 1980, and all production Pershing II missiles carried the W85.[&#8202;](#ref-back-TM_9-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-TM_9) A concept warhead using [kinetic energy penetrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_penetrator "Kinetic energy penetrator") for counter-airfield operations never materialized.[&#8202;](#ref-back-8-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-8)[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-9)

### Launcher

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Launcher")\]

Because of [SALT II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Arms_Limitation_Talks "Strategic Arms Limitation Talks") agreements, no new launchers could be built, therefore the Pershing 1a M790 launchers were modified into the Pershing II M1003 launchers. The functions of the vehicle-mounted programmer test station needed for the older systems were consolidated into the Launch Control Assembly (LCA) in the Ground Integrated Electronics Unit (GIEU) on the side of the launcher. The warhead and radar sections were carried as an assembly on a pallet that rotated to mate with the main missile.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

There were two prime movers for the launcher, both with a crane used for missile assembly and a generator to provide power for the launcher and missile. The U.S. units used the [M983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M983 "M983") HEMTT with a [Hiab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiab "Hiab") 8001 crane and a 30kW generator. Tactical units in Germany used the [M1001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M1001 "M1001") MAN tractor with an _Atlas Maschinen GmbH_ AK4300 M5 crane and a 30kW generator. Since the new guidance system was self-orienting, the launcher could be emplaced on any surveyed site and the missile launched within minutes.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

#### Reentry vehicle

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Reentry vehicle")\]

The reentry vehicle (RV) was structurally and functionally divided into three sections: the radar section (RS), the [warhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warhead "Warhead") section (WHS), and the [guidance and control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidance_system "Guidance system")/adapter (G&C/A) section.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

##### Guidance and control/adapter

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Guidance and control/adapter")\]

The guidance and control/adapter (G&C/A) section consisted of two separate portions, the G&C and the adapter connected by a manufactured splice. At the forward end of the G&C there was a quick access splice for attachment to the warhead section. At the aft end, the adapter was grooved to accept the V-band that spliced the propulsion section to the G&C section. The RV separation system consisted of a linear shaped charge ring assembly bolted to the G&C section so that separation occurred just forward of the G&C manufactured splice. A protective collar on the outer surface of the adapter, mounted over the linear shaped charge, provided personnel protection during G&C/A handling operations.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

The G&C portion contained two guidance systems: a [Singer-Kearfott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kearfott_Guidance_&_Navigation "Kearfott Guidance & Navigation") inertial [navigation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_navigation_system "Inertial navigation system") that provided guidance through the terminal phase and as primary terminal guidance system a Goodyear Aerospace [active radar guidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_radar_guidance "Active radar guidance") system. Using [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") maps of the target area, the Pershing II had an accuracy of 30 metres (100ft) [circular error probable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_error_probable "Circular error probable").[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-12) If the radar guidance failed, the inertial guidance would keep the missile on-target with a lower accuracy. The G&C also contained the Pershing Airborne Computer (PAC), the digital correlator unit (DCU) and actuators to drive the air fins.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

##### Radar section

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Radar section")\]

The radar section consisted of the Goodyear radar unit with the antenna enclosed in an ablative [radome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radome "Radome"). The radar unit transmitted [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave "Radio wave") to the target area during the terminal phase, received altitude and video information and sent the detected video and altitude data to the data correlator unit (DCU) in the G&C section.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

See also: [DSMAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSMAC "DSMAC"), [Automatic target recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_target_recognition "Automatic target recognition"), [Radar imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_imaging "Radar imaging"), and [Topographic map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_map "Topographic map")

The highly accurate [terminal guidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_guidance "Terminal guidance") technique used by the Pershing II RV was radar area correlation, using a [Goodyear Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodyear_Aerospace "Goodyear Aerospace") [active radar homing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_radar_homing "Active radar homing") system.[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-13) This technique compared live radar video return to pre-stored reference scenes of the target area and determined RV position errors with respect to its trajectory and target location. These position errors updated the inertial guidance system, which in turn sent commands to the vane control system to guide the RV to the target.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

At a predetermined altitude, the radar unit activated to provide altitude update data and begin scanning the target area. The analog radar video return was digitized into two-bit pixels by the correlator unit and was formatted into a 128 by 128 array. The target reference scene data, loaded prior to launch via the ground and missile data links, were also encoded as two-bit pixels and placed in reference memory formatted in a 256 by 256 array. The reference scene resolution necessary to correspond to the decreasing altitude of the RV was effected by placing four reference data arrays in memory, each representing a given altitude band. This correlation process was performed several times during each of four altitude bands and continued to update the inertial guidance system until just before the impact.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-14)

If for some reason the correlator system failed to operate or if the correlation data quality was faulty the [inertial guidance system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_guidance_system "Inertial guidance system") continued to operate and guided the RV to the target area with inertial accuracy only.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Goodyear also developed the Reference Scene Generation Facility, a truck-mounted shelter containing the equipment required to program the missile targeting controlled by a [DEC PDP-11/70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDP-11 "PDP-11").[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-15) Radar maps of target areas were stored on disk, then specific targeting data was transferred to a [quarter-inch cartridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter-inch_cartridge "Quarter-inch cartridge") in a hardened carrier. During countdown operations, the cartridge was plugged into the launcher control panel to program the missile with targeting data.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Flight")\]

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| [![icon](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/60px-Question_book-new.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Question_book-new.svg) | This section **does not [cite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources") any [sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")**. Unsourced material may be challenged and [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Burden_of_evidence "Wikipedia:Verifiability")._(January 2021)__([Learn how and when to remove this message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal "Help:Maintenance template removal"))_ |

Prior to launch, the missile was referenced in azimuth by its [gyrocompass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyrocompass "Gyrocompass") inertial platform. After launch, the missile followed an inertially guided [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory "Trajectory") until RV separation. Attitude and guidance commands during powered flight (except for roll attitude) were executed via the swivel nozzles in the two propulsion sections. Roll control was provided by two movable air vanes on the first stage during first stage flight and by the RV air vanes during second stage flight. The first stage also had two fixed air vanes for stability during first stage powered flight.

The midcourse phase of the trajectory was initiated at RV separation and continued until the terminal phase began. At the beginning of the midcourse phase, the RV was pitched down to orient it for reentry and to reduce its radar cross-section. The midcourse attitude was then controlled by the RV vane control system during atmospheric exit and reentry, and by a reaction control system during exoatmospheric flight.

At a predetermined altitude above the target, the terminal phase would begin. A velocity control manoeuvre (pull up, pull down) was executed under inertial guidance control to slow down the RV and achieve the proper impact velocity. The radar correlator system was activated and the radar scanned the target area. Radar return data was compared to pre-stored reference data and the resulting position fix information was used to update the inertial guidance system and generate RV steering commands. The RV was then manoeuvered to the target by the RV vane control system.

### Range

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Range")\]

The weapon had a range of 1,770 kilometres (1,100mi).[&#8202;](#ref-back-PFIAB-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-PFIAB)

Soviet estimates put the system's range at 2,500 kilometres (1,600mi) and they also believed that the missile was armed with an earth-penetrating warhead. These two errors contributed to Soviet fears of the weapon, believing it could be used to [decapitate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapitation_(military_strategy) "Decapitation (military strategy)") the Soviet Union. In reality, from positions in West Germany the system could not target Moscow.[&#8202;](#ref-back-PFIAB-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-PFIAB)[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-17)

## Deployment

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Deployment")\]

On 12 December 1979, at a special meeting of NATO Foreign and Defence Ministers in Brussels, the [NATO Double-Track Decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Double-Track_Decision "NATO Double-Track Decision") was adopted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-18) The ministers resolved to deploy 108 Pershing II launchers to Western Europe to replace existing Pershing 1a launchers and 464 [BGM-109G Ground Launched Cruise Missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BGM-109G_Ground_Launched_Cruise_Missile "BGM-109G Ground Launched Cruise Missile").[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-19)[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-20) Separately, the German Air Force had planned to replace their 72 Pershing 1a missiles with the short-range Pershing 1b, but this never materialised. On 26 August 1987, West German Chancellor [Helmut Kohl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Kohl "Helmut Kohl") pledged to unilaterally dismantle these missiles if the United States and the Soviet Union scrapped all their INF missiles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-21) These missiles were decommissioned by 1991 due to the [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty "Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty").

The first Pershing II launchers were deployed in West Germany beginning in late November 1983 and the deployment was completed in late 1985 with a total of 108 launchers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-22) Initial Operational Status (IOS) was achieved on 15 December 1983 when A Battery, [1st Battalion, 41st Field Artillery Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Battalion,_41st_Field_Artillery_Regiment "1st Battalion, 41st Field Artillery Regiment") rotated onto operational status with the Pershing II at its site in [Mutlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutlangen "Mutlangen"). The West German government announced on 13 December 1985 that the US Army [56th Field Artillery Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/56th_Artillery_Command#56th_Field_Artillery_Brigade "56th Artillery Command") was equipped with 108 Pershing II launchers across three missile battalions stationed at [Neu-Ulm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neu-Ulm "Neu-Ulm"), Mutlangen and [Neckarsulm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neckarsulm "Neckarsulm").[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-23)

## Protests

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Protests")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c3/Anti_kernwapendemonstratie_in_Den_Haag_%28_550_duizend_deelnemers_%29_overzichten%2C_Bestanddeelnr_253-8821.jpg/250px-Anti_kernwapendemonstratie_in_Den_Haag_%28_550_duizend_deelnemers_%29_overzichten%2C_Bestanddeelnr_253-8821.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Anti_kernwapendemonstratie_in_Den_Haag_(_550_duizend_deelnemers_)_overzichten,_Bestanddeelnr_253-8821.jpg) Protest against the deployment of Pershing II missiles, [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague "The Hague"), Netherlands, 1983

The deployment of Pershing II and GLCM missiles was a cause of significant protests in Europe and the US, many organized by the [Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_for_Nuclear_Disarmament "Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament").[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-24)[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-25)

Protests against the short-range [MGM-52 Lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM-52_Lance "MGM-52 Lance") nuclear missile began in July 1981 in [Engstingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engstingen "Engstingen"), West Germany.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEQuint200813-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEQuint200813) In October 1981, 300,000 protesters assembled in [Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonn "Bonn").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEQuint200824-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEQuint200824) [European Nuclear Disarmament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Nuclear_Disarmament "European Nuclear Disarmament") began a campaign for nuclear disarmament in 1982. The [Seneca Women's Encampment for a Future of Peace and Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Women's_Encampment_for_a_Future_of_Peace_and_Justice "Seneca Women's Encampment for a Future of Peace and Justice") was formed in 1983 to protest the deployment.

In 1983, protesters went to court to stop the Pershing II deployment as a violation of Article 26(1) of the [Basic Law for the Federal Republic of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Law_for_the_Federal_Republic_of_Germany "Basic Law for the Federal Republic of Germany"), which prohibited West Germany from preparing for an offensive war.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEQuint200820-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEQuint200820) The [Federal Constitutional Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Constitutional_Court "Federal Constitutional Court") rejected these claims. Again in Bonn in October 1983, as many as 500,000 people protested the deployment and a [human chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_chain_(politics) "Human chain (politics)") was formed from the US Army headquarters in [Stuttgart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuttgart "Stuttgart") to the gates of Wiley Barracks in Neu-Ulm, the site of one of the Pershing battalions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-29-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-29) Due to accessibility, the protests focused on the Mutlangen Missile Storage Area from Easter 1983 until the signing of the [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty "Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty") in 1987.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEQuint200824-1)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEQuint200824)[&#8202;](#ref-back-30-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-30)[&#8202;](#ref-back-31-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-31) On 19 April 1985, the US Army wrote to Congress that a security review conducted after the January 1985 explosion found the missiles "extremely vulnerable" to terrorist attacks and requested funds to secure the missiles in concrete sheds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-32-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-32)

The [Plowshares movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plowshares_movement "Plowshares movement") was active in actions against the deployment. 14 July 1983, Plowshare activists entered the [Avco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avco "Avco") plant in Wilmington, Massachusetts and damaged equipment related to Pershing II and MX missiles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELaffin200317-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELaffin200317) On 4 December 1983, four Plowshare activists cut through a fence in [Schwäbisch Gmünd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schw%C3%A4bisch_Gm%C3%BCnd "Schwäbisch Gmünd") and damaged a truck.[&#8202;](#ref-back-34-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-34)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELaffin200319-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELaffin200319) On 22 April 1984, eight Plowshare activists associated entered the Martin Marietta Aerospace plant in Orlando, Florida where they damaged Pershing II components and a Patriot missile launcher and poured containers of their own blood on equipment.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELaffin200320-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELaffin200320) Four Plowshare activists entered the missile storage area (MSA) at Schwäbisch Gmünd, West Germany on 12 December 1986 and damaged the tractor of a Pershing II erector launcher and hung a banner over the truck.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELaffin200319-1)[\[34\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELaffin200319)

## Incidents

### 1984 rollover

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: 1984 rollover")\]

On 24 September 1984, elements of 1st Battalion, 41st Field Artillery were on a field exercise near [Mutlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutlangen "Mutlangen"). A launcher and MAN tractor were parked on the edge of a dirt road when it slid off and rolled over into deep snow. The equipment was recovered after a six-hour operation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurns2014149-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurns2014149)

### 1985 fire

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: 1985 fire")\]

On 11 January 1985, three soldiers of C Battery, [3rd Battalion, 84th Field Artillery Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Battalion,_84th_Field_Artillery_Regiment "3rd Battalion, 84th Field Artillery Regiment") were killed in fire at Camp Redleg, the CAS site near [Heilbronn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heilbronn "Heilbronn"). The fire occurred while removing a missile stage from the storage container during an assembly operation. An investigation revealed that the [Kevlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevlar "Kevlar") rocket bottle had accumulated a [triboelectric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triboelectric_effect "Triboelectric effect") in the cold dry weather; as the motor was removed from the container, the electrical charge began to flow and created a hot spot that ignited the propellant.[&#8202;](#ref-back-38-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-38)[&#8202;](#ref-back-39-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-39)[&#8202;](#ref-back-40-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-40) A moratorium on missile movement was enacted through late 1986, when new grounding and handling procedures were put into place. Ballistic covers were later added to the Pershing II missiles and to the Pershing 1a missiles still in use by the German Air Force.

The fire provoked further protests against the Pershing II deployment in the name of safety. The [56th Field Artillery Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/56th_Field_Artillery_Command "56th Field Artillery Command") worked closely with local authorities, the press and representatives of the protest groups to keep them informed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-41)

## Variants

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Variants")\]

[![missile erect on launcher, soldiers launcher posed for photo](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Pershing_1B_-_A_Battery_3-84_-_White_Sands_%281986%29.png/250px-Pershing_1B_-_A_Battery_3-84_-_White_Sands_%281986%29.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pershing_1B_-_A_Battery_3-84_-_White_Sands_(1986).png) Pershing 1b during an Engineering Development shoot, January 1986

Pershing 1b was a single-stage, reduced-range version of Pershing II with the same range as the Pershing 1a. The Pershing II launcher was designed so that the cradle could be easily repositioned to handle the shorter missile airframe. The intent was to replace the German Air Force's Pershing 1a systems with Pershing 1b, since [SALT II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SALT_II "SALT II") limited the range of German-owned missiles. The German government agreed to destroy its Pershing 1a systems when the U.S. and the U.S.S.R. signed the INF Treaty, thus the Pershing 1b was never deployed. The single-stage missile was used for [launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pershing_missile_launches "Pershing missile launches") from [White Sands Missile Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Sands_Missile_Range "White Sands Missile Range") due to range limitations.

Pershing II Reduced Range (RR) was a follow-on concept that would have modified the launchers to hold two single-stage missiles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-42-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-42)

Pershing III was a proposal for a four-stage 25,000 pounds (11,000kg) version that would have replaced the [LGM-118 Peacekeeper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGM-118_Peacekeeper "LGM-118 Peacekeeper").[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-43)

Pershing III is also a proposal for a coast-based missile system to counter the Chinese [DF-21D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DF-21D "DF-21D") anti-ship ballistic missile.[&#8202;](#ref-back-44-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-44)

## Elimination

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Elimination")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/Reagan_and_Gorbachev_signing.jpg/250px-Reagan_and_Gorbachev_signing.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Reagan_and_Gorbachev_signing.jpg) Soviet General Secretary [Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev "Mikhail Gorbachev") and U.S. President [Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan") signing the [INF Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty "Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty") on 8 December 1987[![rocket motor burning](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Pershing_static_burn.jpg/250px-Pershing_static_burn.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pershing_static_burn.jpg) Pershing rocket motor being destroyed by static burn, September 1988

The Pershing systems were eliminated after the ratification of the [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty "Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty") on 27 May 1988.[&#8202;](#ref-back-redstoneelimination-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-redstoneelimination) The missiles began to be withdrawn in October 1988 and the last of the missiles were destroyed by the static burn of their motors and subsequently crushed in May 1991 at the [Longhorn Army Ammunition Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longhorn_Army_Ammunition_Plant "Longhorn Army Ammunition Plant") near [Caddo Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo_Lake "Caddo Lake"), Texas. Although not covered by the treaty, West Germany agreed unilaterally to the removal of the Pershing 1a missiles from its inventory in 1991, and the missiles were destroyed in the United States.

## Legacy

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Legacy")\]

The INF treaty only covered the destruction of the launchers and rocket motors. The W-85 warheads used in the Pershing II missiles were removed, modified, and reused in the [B61 nuclear bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B61_nuclear_bomb "B61 nuclear bomb").

The [Orbital Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Sciences_Corporation "Orbital Sciences Corporation") Storm I target missile used air vanes from the Pershing 1a.[&#8202;](#ref-back-46-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-46) The Pershing II guidance section was re-used in the Coleman Aerospace [Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera_(rocket) "Hera (rocket)") and the Orbital Sciences Corporation Storm II target missiles.

### Displays

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Displays")\]

Main article: [Pershing missile displays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pershing_missile_displays "Pershing missile displays")

The INF Treaty allowed for seven inert Pershing II missiles to be retained for [display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pershing_missile_displays "Pershing missile displays") purposes. One is now on display in the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution "Smithsonian Institution")'s [National Air and Space Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Air_and_Space_Museum "National Air and Space Museum") in Washington, D.C., alongside a Soviet SS-20 missile. Another is at the [Central Armed Forces Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Armed_Forces_Museum "Central Armed Forces Museum") in Moscow, Russia, also with an SS-20.[&#8202;](#ref-back-redstoneelimination-1)[\[44\]](#refnote-redstoneelimination) A number of inert Pershing 1 and Pershing 1a missiles are displayed in the U.S. and Germany.

Scrap material from the Pershing II and SS-20 missiles was used in several projects. [Zurab Tsereteli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurab_Tsereteli "Zurab Tsereteli") created a sculpture called _Good Defeats Evil_, a 39-foot (12m), 40- [short-ton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_ton "Short ton") (36,000 [kg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram "Kilogram")) monumental bronze statue of [Saint George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George "Saint George") fighting the dragon of nuclear war, with the dragon being made from sections of the Pershing II and SS-20 missiles. The sculpture was donated to the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations") by the Soviet Union in 1990 and is located on the grounds of the [United Nations Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Headquarters "United Nations Headquarters") in New York City.

In 1991, [Leonard Cheshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Cheshire "Leonard Cheshire")'s World Memorial Fund for Disaster Relief sold badges of the group logo made of scrap material. The [Parker Pen Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_Pen_Company "Parker Pen Company") created a series of pens with a Memorial Fund badge made of scrap missile material, with half the proceeds going to the fund.[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-47)

On 4 November 1991 the [Ronald Reagan Presidential Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan_Presidential_Library "Ronald Reagan Presidential Library") opened in Simi Valley, California. The then five living presidents, Richard Nixon, Gerald Ford, George Bush, Jimmy Carter and Ronald Reagan were present at the opening. Parker presented them each with a black ballpoint Duofold Centennial with the Presidential seal on the crown formed from scrap Pershing and SS-20 material, and engraved signatures of the presidents. The pen was also offered in a walnut box also with the names of all five presidents and the Presidential seal.[&#8202;](#ref-back-48-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-48)

## Veterans

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pershing_II&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Veterans")\]

In 2000, a number of U.S. Army Pershing missile veterans decided to seek out their fellow veterans and to start acquiring information and artifacts on the Pershing systems. In 2004, the Pershing Professionals Association was incorporated to meet the long-term goals—to preserve, interpret and encourage interest in the history of the Pershing missile systems and the soldiers who served, and to make such information accessible to present and future generations to foster a deeper appreciation of the role that Pershing played in world history.[&#8202;](#ref-back-49-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-49)

Veterans of the [2nd Battalion, 4th Infantry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Battalion,_4th_Infantry_Regiment "2nd Battalion, 4th Infantry Regiment"), who had performed the security for the Pershing systems formed a subchapter known as the Pershing Tower Rats. The two German Air Force missile squadrons also formed veterans groups known as the Flugkörpergeschwader 1 eV.[&#8202;](#ref-back-50-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-50)[&#8202;](#ref-back-51-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-51)
