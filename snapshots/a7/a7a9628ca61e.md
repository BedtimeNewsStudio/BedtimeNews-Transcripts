*   What was the Nigerian Civil War?  

The Nigerian Civil War took place from 1967 to 1970 between the Nigerian federal government and Biafra, a secessionist state. Biafra declared its independence from Nigeria in May 1967; it comprised the former Eastern Region and was mainly inhabited by Igbo people.

The war's origins lay in ethnic conflict, economic inequality, and educational disparities. In 1966, violence against the Igbo people in the north resulted in many deaths and mass migration to the east. After failed attempts to reconcile, Biafra seceded, leading to war. Although Biafran troops initially had successes, the federal forces eventually pushed them back. The conflict led to starvation and disease, with estimated deaths ranging from 500,000 to 3,000,000. Biafra collapsed in early 1970, and its officers surrendered.

*   When did the Nigerian Civil War happen?  

The Nigerian Civil War took place from 1967 to 1970 between the federal government of Nigeria and Biafra, a secessionist state. Factors such as ethnic conflict, economic inequality, and educational disparities contributed to the war, which resulted in an estimated 500,000 to 3,000,000 deaths.

In May 1967, the Eastern region authorized Ojukwu to establish a sovereign republic. Simultaneously, the federal military government divided the four regions into 12 states to break their power.

Ojukwu declared the secession of the Eastern region as the Republic of Biafra on May 30, 1967, which the federal government saw as an act of rebellion. Fighting began in early July, escalating into a full-scale civil war within weeks. The Biafran collapse started on December 24, 1969, and Biafra surrendered in Lagos on January 15, 1970.

*   Who were the main groups fighting in the war?  

The main combatants in the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970) were the [federal government of Nigeria](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref1) and the secessionist state of [Biafra](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref1). Biafra was composed of the three states of the Eastern region and was led by Lieut. Col. Odumegwu Ojukwu. Factors that led to the war included ethnic conflict, economic inequality, and educational disparities. The [United Kingdom](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref355916) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref355916) provided arms to the federal government, while France supplied weapons to Biafra.

*   Why did the war start?  

The Nigerian Civil War, also known as the Biafran War, began due to a complex mix of ethnic tensions, economic disparities, and political instability in Nigeria during the 1960s.

Resentment and violence against the Igbo people in the Hausa-dominated north led to massacres and a refugee crisis, as many Igbo people fled to the east. After failed attempts to reach an agreement, the Eastern Region declared itself the independent Republic of Biafra in May 1967, which the federal government refused to recognize, leading to the outbreak of war in July.

*   How did the war affect the people of Nigeria?  

The [Nigerian Civil War](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref1) had a devastating impact on the people of Nigeria. From 1967 to 1970, the conflict between Nigeria's federal government and the secessionist state of Biafra resulted in an estimated 500,000 to 3,000,000 deaths.

The war was rooted in long-standing regional stresses, including ethnic competitiveness, educational inequality, and economic imbalance. These tensions led to political instability and ultimately the secession of the Eastern region as the Republic of Biafra. The conflict led to heavy casualties among civilians and soldiers and a humanitarian crisis caused by ethnic cleansing and starvation.

*   What was the outcome and impact of the Nigerian Civil War?  

The [Nigerian Civil War](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nigerian-civil-war/Introduction#ref1) (1967-1970) resulted in the defeat of Biafra, and the secessionist states were integrated back into Nigeria. Estimates of the war's fatalities range from 500,000 to 3,000,000, caused by battlefield deaths, ethnic cleansing, and starvation.

After the war, General Gowon reconciled the two sides, and the oil boom helped the federal government finance development programs and consolidate power. However, ethnic conflict, previously suppressed during military rule, reemerged, and friction grew between Muslims and Christians. Protests against the government's oil policies and the exploitation of the Niger Delta region also arose.

The **Nigerian Civil War** was fought from 1967 to 1970 between [Nigeria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nigeria)’s federal government and the secessionist state [Biafra](https://www.britannica.com/place/Biafra). Ethnic conflict, economic [inequality](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/inequality), and educational disparities were among the factors that contributed to the start of the [war](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war), which killed an estimated 500,000 to 3,000,000 people.

## Background

Nigeria became an independent country on October 1, 1960. [Abubakar Tafawa Balewa](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Abubakar-Tafawa-Balewa) was its first federal prime minister—he had held that position since 1957—and [Nnamdi Azikiwe](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nnamdi-Azikiwe) became its president of the Senate, which was a largely ceremonial role. Following a [UN](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-Nations)-supervised referendum in 1961, the northern part of the Trust Territory of the Cameroons joined Nigeria’s Northern region, while in October the Southern Cameroons united with Cameroun to form the [Federal Republic of Cameroon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cameroon). On October 1, 1963, Nigeria became a republic, with Azikiwe as its president, although, as [prime minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prime-minister), Balewa remained more politically powerful.

[![Image 13: Nigeria in 1960](https://cdn.britannica.com/23/61723-004-4EDF3E92/Nigeria-boundaries-1960.jpg)](https://cdn.britannica.com/23/61723-004-4EDF3E92/Nigeria-boundaries-1960.jpg)

[Nigeria in 1960](https://cdn.britannica.com/23/61723-004-4EDF3E92/Nigeria-boundaries-1960.jpg)Nigeria's administrative boundaries in 1960.(more)

In Nigeria and its surrounding region, long-standing regional stresses—ethnic competitiveness, educational inequality, and economic imbalance being the most prominent—again came to the fore in a controversial [census](https://www.britannica.com/science/census) during 1962–63. In an attempt to stave off [ethnic conflict](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ethnic-conflict), Nigeria’s Mid-West region was created in 1963 by dividing the Western region. Despite this division, the country still was segmented into three large geographic regions, each of which was essentially controlled by an ethnic group: the west by the [Yoruba](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Yoruba) people, the east by the [Igbo](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Igbo) people, and the north by the [Hausa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hausa)-[Fulani](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Fulani) people. Conflicts were [endemic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/endemic), as regional leaders protected their privileges; the south complained of northern domination, and the north feared that the southern elite was bent on capturing power. In the west the government had fallen apart in 1962, and a [boycott](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycott) of the federal election of December 1964 brought Nigeria to the brink of breakdown.

The point of no return was reached in January 1966, when, after the collapse of order in the west following the fraudulent election of October 1965, a group of army officers attempted to overthrow the federal government, and Balewa and two of the regional premiers were murdered. A military administration was set up under Maj. Gen. Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi, but his plan to abolish the regions and impose a unitary government met with anti-Igbo riots in the north. The military intervention worsened the political situation, as the army itself split along ethnic lines, its officers clashed over power, and the instigators and leaders of the January coup were accused of favouring Igbo domination. In July 1966 northern officers staged a countercoup, Aguiyi-Ironsi was assassinated, and Lieut. Col. (later Gen.) [Yakubu Gowon](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yakubu-Gowon) came to power. The crisis was [compounded](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compounded) by intercommunal clashes in the north and threats of secession in the south.

## Outbreak of war

[![Image 19: Odumegwu Ojukwu in military uniform with a peaked cap and decorative cords, looking at the camera, circa 1966.](https://cdn.britannica.com/34/229434-050-03E6A234/Odumegwu-Ojukwu-Nigeria-Biafra-1966.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/34/229434-050-03E6A234/Odumegwu-Ojukwu-Nigeria-Biafra-1966.jpg)

[Odumegwu Ojukwu](https://cdn.britannica.com/34/229434-050-03E6A234/Odumegwu-Ojukwu-Nigeria-Biafra-1966.jpg)Lieut. Col. (later Gen.) Odumegwu Ojukwu, pictured here c. 1966, led the secessionist state of Biafra from its declaration of independence in 1967 through its surrender in 1970, which ended the Nigerian Civil War.(more)

Gowon’s attempt to hold a conference to settle the [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) future of Nigeria was abandoned after a series of ethnic massacres in October 1966. A last-ditch effort to save the country was made in January 1967, when the Eastern delegation, led by Lieut. Col. (later Gen.) [Odumegwu Ojukwu](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Odumegwu-Ojukwu), agreed to meet the others on neutral ground at Aburi, [Ghana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ghana). The situation deteriorated, however, after differences developed over the interpretation of the accord. In May the Eastern region’s consultative assembly authorized Ojukwu to establish a [sovereign](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereign) republic, while, at the same time, the federal [military government](https://www.britannica.com/topic/military-government)[promulgated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/promulgated) a decree dividing the four regions into 12 states, including 6 in the north and 3 in the east, in an attempt to break the power of the regions.

On May 30, 1967, Ojukwu declared the secession of the three states of the Eastern region under the name of the [Republic of Biafra](https://www.britannica.com/place/Biafra), which Nigeria’s federal government interpreted as an act of rebellion. Fighting broke out in early July. Within weeks the conflict had escalated into a full-scale civil war.

In August 1967 Biafran troops crossed the [Niger](https://www.britannica.com/place/Niger-River), seized [Benin City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Benin-City), and were well on their way to [Lagos](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lagos-Nigeria) before they were checked at Ore, a small town in Western state (now [Ondo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ondo-state-Nigeria) state). Shortly thereafter, federal troops entered [Enugu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Enugu-Nigeria), the provisional capital of Biafra, and penetrated the Igbo heartland. The next two years were marked by stiff resistance in the shrinking Biafran [enclave](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/enclave) and by heavy casualties among civilians as well as in both armies, all set within what threatened to be a military stalemate.

Peacemaking attempts by the Organization of African Unity (now the [African Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/African-Union)), the pope, and others were ineffective, and Biafra began gaining recognition from African states ([Côte d’Ivoire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cote-dIvoire), [Gabon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gabon), [Tanzania](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tanzania), and [Zambia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Zambia)). [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) provided weapons to Biafra, while the [U.K.](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) sent arms to the federal government. Biafra also received aid from international organizations for its population, which was suffering from starvation.

The final Biafran collapse began on December 24, 1969, when federal troops launched a significant offensive. Biafra was short on ammunition, its people were desperate for food, and its leaders controlled only one-sixth of the territory that had formed the Biafran republic in 1967. Ojukwu fled to [Côte d’Ivoire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cote-dIvoire) on January 11, 1970, and a Biafran deputation formally surrendered in [Lagos](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lagos-Nigeria) four days later. The Republic of Biafra had come to an end.

Estimates of the number of people who died during the Nigerian Civil War vary significantly, from 500,000 to 3,000,000. Causes included battlefield deaths, [ethnic cleansing](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ethnic-cleansing), and starvation.

## Aftermath

[![Image 23: Yakubu Gowon in military uniform, shown from the chest up, looking slightly to the side with a serious expression.](https://cdn.britannica.com/33/229433-050-8595B7FC/Yakubu-Gowan-Nigeria-Head-of-State-1968.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/33/229433-050-8595B7FC/Yakubu-Gowan-Nigeria-Head-of-State-1968.jpg)

[Yakubu Gowon](https://cdn.britannica.com/33/229433-050-8595B7FC/Yakubu-Gowan-Nigeria-Head-of-State-1968.jpg)Nigerian head of state Lieut. Col. (later Gen.) Yakubu Gowon, May 27, 1968.(more)

Gowon was able, through his own personal magnetism, to [reconcile](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/reconcile) the two sides so that the former Biafran states were [integrated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrated) into Nigeria once again and were not blamed for the Nigerian Civil War. The [oil](https://www.britannica.com/science/petroleum) boom that followed the war allowed the federal government to finance development programs and consolidate its power. In 1974 Gowon postponed until 1976 the target date for a return to civilian rule, but he was overthrown in July 1975 and fled to [Great Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Britain-island-Europe).

Nigeria’s new head of state, Brig. Gen. Murtala Ramat Mohammed, initiated many changes during his brief time in office: he began the process of moving the federal capital to [Abuja](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abuja-national-capital-Nigeria), addressed the issue of government inefficiency, and initiated the process for a return to civilian control. He was assassinated in February 1976 during an unsuccessful [coup](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/coup) attempt, and his top aide, Lieut. Gen. [Olusegun Obasanjo](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Olusegun-Obasanjo), became head of the government. He did not run for the presidency in 1979, and Nigeria shifted to civilian rule, thus closing the era of military control during and around the Nigerian Civil War.
