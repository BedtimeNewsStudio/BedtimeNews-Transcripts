Top Questions

### How long was the Allied occupation of Japan?

The Allied occupation of Japan lasted almost seven years. It began on August 29, 1945, when elements of the U.S. 11th Airborne Division secured the airstrip at Atsugi, ahead of the arrival of Gen. Douglas MacArthur later that day. It officially ended on April 28, 1952, when the Treaty of San Francisco came into effect.

### What was the role of Gen. Douglas MacArthur during the occupation of Japan?

Gen. Douglas MacArthur was the supreme commander for the Allied Powers, exercising authority through the Japanese government to further Allied objectives.

### What were the main changes introduced in Japan’s new constitution of 1947?

The new constitution vested power in a democratic government, renounced Japan’s right to wage war, reduced the emperor to a ceremonial status, and gave women the right to vote.

### How did the Korean War influence the occupation of Japan?

The Korean War increased pressure for a peace treaty with Japan and convinced many in Japan to align with Western powers for security reasons.

**occupation of Japan**, (1945–52) military occupation of Japan by the [Allied Powers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Allied-Powers-international-alliance) after its defeat in [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II). Theoretically an international occupation, in fact it was carried out almost entirely by U.S. forces under Gen. [Douglas MacArthur](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Douglas-MacArthur).

During the occupation period, Japanese soldiers and civilians from abroad were repatriated to Japan, arms industries were [dismantled](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/dismantled), and political prisoners were released. Wartime leaders stood trial for [war crime](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war-crime) s, and seven were executed. A new constitution (promulgated 1947), vesting power in a democratic government, replaced the [Meiji Constitution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Meiji-Constitution); in it Japan renounced its right to wage war, the emperor was reduced to ceremonial status, and women were given the [right to vote](https://www.britannica.com/topic/suffrage). Though the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) wanted to end the occupation in 1947, the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) vetoed a peace treaty with Japan; a treaty was signed in 1951, and the occupation ended the following year.

## The surrender of Japan

[![USS Missouri: Japanese surrender](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/125042-050-EFE02FAF/representatives-Japanese-Shigemitsu-Mamoru-board-Gen-Umezu-September-2-1945.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/125042-050-EFE02FAF/representatives-Japanese-Shigemitsu-Mamoru-board-Gen-Umezu-September-2-1945.jpg)

[USS _Missouri_: Japanese surrender](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/125042-050-EFE02FAF/representatives-Japanese-Shigemitsu-Mamoru-board-Gen-Umezu-September-2-1945.jpg) Japanese representatives, including Foreign Minister Shigemitsu Mamoru (with walking cane) and Gen. Umezu Yoshijiro (front right), on board the USS _Missouri_ during the surrender ceremonies, September 2, 1945.(more)

On August 14, 1945, the same day that Japan accepted the terms of the [Potsdam Declaration](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Potsdam-Declaration), Gen. MacArthur was [designated](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/designated) the supreme commander for the Allied Powers. On the grounds that Allied relations with Japan did not rest on a contractual basis, but on unconditional surrender, the power of the emperor and the Japanese government was subordinated to the Allied occupation authorities. MacArthur became, in effect, the [viceroy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/viceroy-government-official) of Japan. As the sole executive for the Allied Powers, he exercised his authority through the Japanese governmental machinery and agencies to the extent that this arrangement satisfactorily furthered Allied objectives.

The Allied occupation began on August 29, when elements of the U.S. 11th Airborne Division secured the airstrip at [Atsugi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atsugi), just west of [Yokohama](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yokohama). Later that same day, MacArthur arrived in Japan; he had received by radio a statement of U.S. government policy entitled “U.S. Initial Post-Surrender Policy for Japan.” This document was prepared jointly by a committee composed of representatives of the [Department of State](https://www.britannica.com/topic/US-Department-of-State), the [War Department](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-United-States-Army), and [Department of the Navy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-States-Navy). It dealt with such subjects as military occupation, relationship of Allied authority to the Japanese government, disarmament and demobilization, [war criminals](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war-crime), encouragement of desire for individual liberties and democratic processes, economic demilitarization, resumption of peaceful economic activity, and also provided for the participation in the occupation of Japan of the forces of other nations that had taken a leading part in the [war against Japan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pacific-War). In accordance with this provision, U.S. forces in Japan were joined by [British Commonwealth](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Commonwealth-association-of-states) troops during the first three months of 1946.

On September 2, 1945, on the USS _Missouri_ anchored in [Tokyo Bay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tokyo-Bay), MacArthur accepted the surrender of Japan. The instrument of surrender was also signed by representatives of the United States, the Republic of China, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, the commonwealth of Australia, the dominion of Canada, the provisional government of the [French Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/France), the kingdom of the Netherlands, and the dominion of [New Zealand](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand).

## Creation of the occupation government

[![Crowds of people gather around merchants selling goods spread on the sidewalk at a black market near Ueno, Japan, in 1946.](https://cdn.britannica.com/93/211893-050-EE62C33E/Black-market-vendors-United-States-occupation-Ueno-Japan-1946.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/93/211893-050-EE62C33E/Black-market-vendors-United-States-occupation-Ueno-Japan-1946.jpg)

[Japanese black market after World War II](https://cdn.britannica.com/93/211893-050-EE62C33E/Black-market-vendors-United-States-occupation-Ueno-Japan-1946.jpg) Black market vendors selling their wares during the U.S. occupation, Ueno, Japan, 1946.(more)

The “Big Three” Allied governments agreed, with the [concurrence](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/concurrence) of China, to establish in [Tokyo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tokyo-metropolis) an Allied Council for Japan to be composed of the supreme commander as chair; members from the United States, the Soviet Union, and China; and a member representing jointly the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and India. The purpose of the council was to consult with and advise the supreme commander in regard to the implementation of the terms of surrender, the occupation, and control of Japan. Although the terms of reference of the Allied council provided that the supreme commander would consult and advise with the council in advance of the issuance of orders on matters of substance, circumstances permitting, he was the sole executive authority for the Allied Powers in Japan, and in all cases action was to be carried out under and through him. He was to issue all orders for the implementation of the terms of surrender, the occupation and control of Japan, and supplementary directives.

[![Street scene in Hiroshima, Japan, showing people walking among rebuilt wooden buildings and shops, with city structures and mountains visible in the distance, circa 1949.](https://cdn.britannica.com/94/211894-050-7C862C5C/Rebuilt-area-Hiroshima-Japan-1949.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/94/211894-050-7C862C5C/Rebuilt-area-Hiroshima-Japan-1949.jpg)

[Hiroshima](https://cdn.britannica.com/94/211894-050-7C862C5C/Rebuilt-area-Hiroshima-Japan-1949.jpg) Rebuilt area of Hiroshima, Japan, four years after a U.S. atomic bomb destroyed the city, September 1949.(more)

If any disagreement arose in the council regarding fundamental changes in the Japanese [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) structure, or dealing with a change in the Japanese government as a whole, the supreme commander was to withhold action pending clarification of its decision by the Far Eastern Commission (FEC), a separate advisory body composed of the representatives from the Soviet Union, United Kingdom, United States, China, France, the Netherlands, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India, and the Philippines that met in Washington, D.C. However, when necessary, the supreme commander, after appropriate consultation with the council, could change individual ministers or fill vacancies. The first meeting of the Allied Council was held April 4, 1946, in Tokyo.

Japan’s constitution was [promulgated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/promulgated) on November 3, 1946 and came into force on May 3 1947, superseding the [Meiji Constitution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Meiji-Constitution) of 1889. It differs from the earlier document in two fundamental ways: the principle of [sovereignty](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereignty) and the stated aim of maintaining Japan as a peaceful and democratic country in perpetuity. The emperor, rather than being the embodiment of all [sovereign](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereign) authority (as he was previously), is the symbol of the state and of the unity of the people, while sovereign power rests with the people (whose fundamental [human rights](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-rights) are explicitly guaranteed). Article 9 of the constitution states that Japan “forever renounces war as a sovereign right of the nation”—a clause that has been much debated since the constitution’s promulgation.

Through the end of the 1940s, the stated goals of the occupation government were the demilitarization and deindustrialization of Japan as preconditions to the [conclusion](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/conclusion) of a final peace treaty. On June 19, 1947, the Far Eastern Commission unanimously approved a basic postsurrender policy for Japan that would remain effective until such a treaty could come in force. Designed to ensure that Japan would not again threaten the peace and security of the Pacific and to encourage a democratic and peaceful regime in Japan, this statement of principles reaffirmed in its essentials the earlier U.S. declaration of August 29, 1945. The FEC also forbade any Japanese activity in the field of [atomic energy](https://www.britannica.com/science/nuclear-energy) “for the time being”; laid down standards for the reduction of Japan’s [capacity](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/capacity) in war industries which were in excess of “the peaceful needs of the Japanese people”; and defined those needs as being “substantially the [standard of living](https://www.britannica.com/money/standard-of-living) prevailing in Japan during the period 1930–1934.”

During this time, the rift between the [Allied powers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Allied-powers-World-War-II) became more pronounced, especially after the victory of [Mao Zedong](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mao-Zedong)’s [Communist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chinese-Communist-Party) forces in the [Chinese Civil War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Chinese-Civil-War). The Soviets perceived, correctly, that U.S. interests in Japan had transitioned from punishing a defeated foe to helping a potential [Cold War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Cold-War) ally reform and rebuild. In this way, the Allied occupation of Japan would prove to be broadly similar to the [occupation of Germany](https://www.britannica.com/topic/How-Was-Germany-Divided-After-World-War-II). In both cases, the Allies disagreed over [reparations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/reparations) regimes, and the United States ultimately spent more on occupation costs and development loans than either Japan or Germany paid out in [reparations](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/reparations). The [zaibatsu](https://www.britannica.com/topic/zaibatsu), family owned enterprises that had dominated the Japanese economy before the war, were broken up by Allied authorities in 1946, but they were soon reformed as [keiretsu](https://www.britannica.com/money/keiretsu), conglomerates that were often run by those same zaibatsu families. The rapid reindustrialization of Japan would subsequently be viewed through the same lens as the [Wirtschaftswunder](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Wirtschaftswunder) (“economic miracle”) in postwar Germany.

## Occupied Japan and the Korean War

The Soviet Union left the FEC in January 1950, demanding the expulsion of the Chinese Nationalist member, but returned in October. Soviet participation in the Allied Council was [intermittent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intermittent), because of a continuing dispute over repatriation of Japanese war prisoners from the Soviet Union. In April 1950 the Soviets announced that repatriation had been completed, but the U.S. maintained that more than 300,000 prisoners were still unaccounted for. It was later estimated that as many as 200,000 Japanese POWs had died in Soviet custody.

After the Soviet-backed [North Korean invasion of South Korea](https://www.britannica.com/event/Korean-War), the Soviet Union charged that the U.S. was secretly rearming Japan and that it was using Japanese troops in the UN campaign on the Korean peninsula. Both charges were denied by the U.S., but in fact, a flotilla of 20 minesweepers from the Japanese Maritime Safety Agency (the [precursor](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/precursor) of the modern [Maritime Self-Defense Force](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Self-Defense-Force)) was operating in Korean waters under the command of the [United States Navy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-States-Navy) in the last months of 1950. In October 1950 a Japanese sailor was killed in action when his ship struck a mine off [Wŏnsan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Wonsan).

As war raged in Korea, pressure for the conclusion of a peace treaty increased in Japan and elsewhere. U.S. Pres. [Harry S. Truman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Harry-S-Truman) announced that the United States was beginning informal conversations with interested powers on the subject. An outline of proposed peace terms released by the U.S. on November 24, 1950, called for a U.S. [trusteeship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trusteeship-Council) under the UN for the [Ryukyu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ryukyu-Islands) and [Bonin Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bonin-Islands); the status of [Taiwan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taiwan), the [P’eng-hu Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Peng-hu-Islands), southern [Sakhalin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sakhalin-Island), and the [Kuril Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuril-Islands) would be jointly determined by the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and China or, failing agreement, by the [UN General Assembly](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-Nations-General-Assembly); and Japan would be admitted to the UN. The terms would permit the maintenance of U.S. and other troops in Japan, by agreement with the Japanese government, to safeguard Japan’s security. No restrictions on Japanese rearmament were proposed. Some of Japan’s former enemies found these terms too [lenient](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/lenient), demanding firmer guarantees against Japanese remilitarization, as well as further reparations.

The primary question, however, was whether the U.S. and other countries should conclude a separate treaty or treaties with Japan if agreement with the Soviet Union (and Communist China, which demanded a voice) proved impossible. On December 27, 1950, the U.S. [intimated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intimated) in a note to the Soviet Union that it was prepared to proceed without Soviet concurrence. Discussion in Japan itself was focused chiefly on the issue of a separate treaty. Throughout the occupation, the Japanese had broadly voiced approval for an overall treaty, which would give Japan full international status and diplomatic freedom of action. However, Prime Minister [Shigeru Yoshida](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yoshida-Shigeru) and his Liberal Party maintained that a separate treaty was better than none, deplored further delay, and favored alignment with the Western powers. Opposition parties, which hoped that Japan could maintain a neutral position in world affairs, argued for an overall treaty and opposed the maintenance of U.S. bases in Japan after the treaty was signed. [Sentiment](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Sentiment) for an overall treaty was strong in the first half of 1950, but the war in Korea caused a swing in the other direction, convincing many that Japan, for the sake of its own security, had no choice but to throw in its lot with the Western powers.

## Treaty of San Francisco and the end of occupation

In April 1951 MacArthur was relieved of command by Truman and replaced by Lieut. Gen. [Matthew B. Ridgway](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Matthew-Bunker-Ridgway). This step caused some initial uneasiness in Japan, which was later allayed by [assurances](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/assurances) that no changes in U.S. policies toward Japan were contemplated.

The signing of the Treaty of San Francisco (also called the Treaty of Peace with Japan) on September 8, 1951, represented the climax of years of diplomatic effort by the United States. The terms of the treaty had been worked out by consultation with the United States’ allies and with the Japanese government, during which [John Foster Dulles](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Foster-Dulles), ambassador at large, had visited Japan, the Philippines, Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, and France. The United States sought a treaty of reconciliation, minimizing [punitive](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/punitive) and restrictive features, which would encourage Japan to make a positive and voluntary contribution to the strength of the non-Communist world. Many of Japan’s neighbors were, however, concerned over the possible revival of Japanese militarism, aggression, and economic competition. Agreement was eventually reached on a treaty which in general was considered satisfactory in Washington, D.C., Canberra, Wellington, [Manila](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manila), London, and Paris. Although denounced by the Soviet delegate at San Francisco as a U.S. attempt to “enslave” Japan economically and to draw it into “aggressive military coalitions” directed against the Soviet Union and China, the treaty was signed by Japan and 48 other countries. All occupation forces were to be [withdrawn](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/withdrawn) from Japan within 90 days after the treaty came into force.

However, a supplementary treaty between Japan and the United States, also signed on September 8, permitted U.S. forces to be stationed in Japan after this date, as a provisional measure for Japan’s protection, with the expectation that Japan itself would increasingly assume responsibility for its own defense. The treaty contained no restrictions on Japan’s domestic political institutions, economy, or military establishment (though the maintenance of armed forces was still prohibited by the constitution of 1947). The treaty would enter into force when ratified by Japan and by a majority, including the United States, of 11 principal Pacific countries.

On April 28, 1952, the Japanese peace treaty came into effect, formally bringing to an end the Allied occupation. Japan thus resumed its place as a fully sovereign country. In proclaiming the treaty, Truman noted that six years of joint effort to restore Japan as a prosperous and progressive nation had strengthened the bonds of friendship between the Japanese and American peoples. He further expressed confidence that Japan was ready to play its full part in meeting the “menace” of Communism.

China had not been invited to sign the multilateral peace treaty, because of disagreement among the treaty’s sponsors as to whether it should be represented by the Nationalist or the Communist government. The Soviet Union denounced the treaty as illegal and as part of U.S. preparations for a new war in East Asia; these [sentiments](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sentiments) were echoed by Communist China. In June 1952 Japan formally applied for membership in the [United Nations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-Nations), but although its admission was generally favored, it was prevented by a Soviet veto in September (Japan was ultimately granted membership in December 1956).

Since Japan possessed no regular armed forces, U.S. troops remained in the country under the terms of the U.S.-Japanese security treaty of 1951, which became effective along with the peace treaty. Some fears that this arrangement might compromise Japan’s sovereignty were expressed in Japan, but the Diet eventually passed the necessary [implementing](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implementing) legislation. An administrative agreement signed on February 27, 1952, defined the conditions under which U.S. troops would be stationed in Japan, including a sharing of costs between the Japanese and U.S. governments and provision for a form of [extraterritoriality](https://www.britannica.com/topic/extraterritoriality). More than 50,000 U.S. troops remained stationed in Japan in the 21st century.

### What was the Cold War?

The Cold War was an ongoing political rivalry between the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) and their respective allies that developed after [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II). This hostility between the two superpowers was first given its name by [George Orwell](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell) in [an article](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/you-and-the-atom-bomb/) published in 1945. Orwell understood it as a nuclear stalemate between “super-states”: each possessed weapons of mass destruction and was capable of annihilating the other.

The Cold War began after the surrender of Nazi Germany in 1945, when the uneasy alliance between the United States and Great Britain on the one hand and the Soviet Union on the other started to fall apart. The Soviet Union began to establish left-wing governments in the countries of eastern Europe, determined to safeguard against a possible renewed threat from Germany. The Americans and the British worried that Soviet domination in eastern Europe might be permanent. The Cold War was solidified by 1947–48, when U.S. aid had brought certain Western countries under American influence and the Soviets had established openly [communist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/communism) regimes. Nevertheless, there was very little use of weapons on battlefields during the Cold War. It was waged mainly on political, economic, and propaganda fronts and lasted until 1991.

### How did the Cold War end?

The Cold War came to a close gradually. The unity in the [communist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/communism) bloc was unraveling throughout the 1960s and ’70s as a split occurred between [China](https://www.britannica.com/place/China) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union). Meanwhile, [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) and certain Western countries were becoming more economically independent. Increasingly complex international relationships developed as a result, and smaller countries became more resistant to superpower cajoling.

The Cold War truly began to break down during the administration of [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mikhail-Gorbachev), who changed the more totalitarian aspects of the Soviet government and tried to democratize its political system. Communist regimes began to collapse in eastern Europe, and democratic governments rose in [East Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Germany), [Poland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Poland), [Hungary](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hungary), and [Czechoslovakia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Czechoslovakia), followed by the reunification of West and East Germany under [NATO](https://www.britannica.com/topic/North-Atlantic-Treaty-Organization) auspices. Gorbachev’s reforms meanwhile weakened his own communist party and allowed power to shift to the constituent governments of the Soviet bloc. The Soviet Union collapsed in late 1991, giving rise to 15 newly independent nations, including a [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia) with an anticommunist leader.

### Why was the Cuban missile crisis such an important event in the Cold War?

In the late 1950s, both the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) were developing intercontinental ballistic [missiles](https://www.britannica.com/technology/nuclear-weapon). In 1962 the Soviet Union began to secretly install missiles in Cuba to launch attacks on U.S. cities. The confrontation that followed, known as the [Cuban missile crisis](https://www.britannica.com/event/Cuban-missile-crisis), brought the two superpowers to the brink of war before an agreement was reached to withdraw the missiles.

The conflict showed that both superpowers were wary of using their nuclear weapons against each other for fear of mutual atomic annihilation. The signing of the [Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty](https://www.britannica.com/event/Nuclear-Test-Ban-Treaty) followed in 1963, which banned aboveground nuclear weapons testing. Still, after the crisis, the Soviets were determined not to be humiliated by their military inferiority again, and they began a buildup of conventional and strategic forces that the United States was forced to match for the next 25 years.

**Cold War**, the open [yet](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/yet) restricted rivalry that developed after [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II) between the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) and their respective allies. The Cold War was waged on political, economic, and [propaganda](https://www.britannica.com/topic/propaganda) fronts and had only limited recourse to weapons. The term was first used by the English writer [George Orwell](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell) in an article published in 1945 to refer to what he predicted would be a [nuclear](https://www.britannica.com/technology/nuclear-power) stalemate between “two or three monstrous super-states, each possessed of a [weapon](https://www.britannica.com/technology/nuclear-weapon) by which millions of people can be wiped out in a few seconds.” It was first used in the United States by the American financier and presidential adviser [Bernard Baruch](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bernard-Baruch) in a speech at the State House in Columbia, [South Carolina](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Carolina), in 1947.

## Origins of the Cold War

Following the surrender of [Nazi](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nazi-Party) [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Germany) in May 1945 near the close of [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II), the uneasy wartime alliance between the United States and Great [Britain](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-United-Kingdom) on the one hand and the Soviet Union on the other began to unravel. By 1948 the Soviets had installed left-wing governments in the countries of eastern Europe that had been liberated by the [Red Army](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Red-Army). The Americans and the British feared the permanent Soviet domination of eastern Europe and the threat of Soviet-influenced communist parties coming to power in the [democracies](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/democracies) of western Europe. The Soviets, on the other hand, were determined to maintain control of eastern Europe in order to safeguard against any possible renewed threat from Germany, and they were intent on spreading [communism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/communism) worldwide, largely for ideological reasons. The Cold War had solidified by 1947–48, when U.S. aid provided under the [Marshall Plan](https://www.britannica.com/event/Marshall-Plan) to western Europe had brought those countries under American influence and the Soviets had installed openly communist regimes in eastern Europe.

## The struggle between superpowers

The Cold War reached its peak in 1948–53. In this period the Soviets unsuccessfully [blockaded](https://www.britannica.com/event/Berlin-blockade) the Western-held sectors of West [Berlin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Berlin) (1948–49); the United States and its European allies formed the [North Atlantic Treaty Organization](https://www.britannica.com/topic/North-Atlantic-Treaty-Organization) (NATO), a unified military command to resist the Soviet presence in Europe (1949); the Soviets exploded their first atomic warhead (1949), thus ending the American monopoly on the atomic bomb; the Chinese communists came to power in mainland China (1949); and the Soviet-supported communist government of [North Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Korea) invaded U.S.-supported [South Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Korea) in 1950, setting off an indecisive [Korean War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Korean-War) that lasted until 1953.

From 1953 to 1957 Cold War tensions relaxed somewhat, largely owing to the death of the longtime Soviet dictator [Joseph Stalin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin) in 1953; nevertheless, the standoff remained. A unified military organization among the [Soviet-bloc](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Eastern-bloc) countries, the [Warsaw Pact](https://www.britannica.com/event/Warsaw-Pact), was formed in 1955; and [West Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/West-Germany) was admitted into NATO that same year. Another intense stage of the Cold War was in 1958–62. The United States and the Soviet Union began developing intercontinental [ballistic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ballistic) [missiles](https://www.britannica.com/technology/nuclear-weapon), and in 1962 the Soviets began secretly installing missiles in Cuba that could be used to launch nuclear attacks on U.S. cities. This sparked the [Cuban missile crisis](https://www.britannica.com/event/Cuban-missile-crisis) (1962), a confrontation that brought the two superpowers to the brink of war before an agreement was reached to withdraw the missiles.

The Cuban missile crisis showed that neither the United States nor the Soviet Union were ready to use nuclear weapons for fear of the other’s retaliation (and thus of mutual atomic annihilation). The two superpowers soon signed the [Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty of 1963](https://www.britannica.com/event/Nuclear-Test-Ban-Treaty), which banned aboveground nuclear weapons testing. But the crisis also hardened the Soviets’ determination never again to be [humiliated](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/humiliated) by their military inferiority, and they began a buildup of both conventional and strategic forces that the United States was forced to match for the next 25 years.

Throughout the Cold War the United States and the Soviet Union avoided direct military confrontation in Europe and engaged in actual combat operations only to keep allies from defecting to the other side or to overthrow them after they had done so. Thus, the Soviet Union sent troops to preserve communist rule in [East Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Germany) (1953), [Hungary (1956)](https://www.britannica.com/event/Hungarian-Revolution-1956), [Czechoslovakia (1968)](https://www.britannica.com/event/Prague-Spring), and [Afghanistan (1979)](https://www.britannica.com/event/Soviet-invasion-of-Afghanistan). For its part, the United States [helped overthrow a left-wing government in Guatemala (1954)](https://www.britannica.com/place/Guatemala/The-postcolonial-period#ref40949), supported an [unsuccessful invasion of Cuba](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bay-of-Pigs-invasion) (1961), [invaded the Dominican Republic (1965)](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dominican-Republic/Bosch-Balaguer-and-their-successors#ref217439) and [Grenada (1983)](https://www.britannica.com/place/Grenada/Independence#ref54646), and undertook a long (1954–75) and unsuccessful effort to prevent communist North Vietnam from bringing South Vietnam under its rule ( _see_ [Vietnam War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Vietnam-War)).
