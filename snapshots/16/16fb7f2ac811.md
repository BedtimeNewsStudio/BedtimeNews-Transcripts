### What is Nagorno-Karabakh?

Nagorno-Karabakh is a region in southwestern [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan). It was an autonomous _oblast_ (province) in the former Azerbaijan Soviet Socialist Republic. Between 1992 and 2023 it was a self-declared republic, though its independence was never internationally recognized.

### What is the significance of the 1994 ceasefire agreement?

The 1994 ceasefire agreement marked the end of the first conflict, which began in 1988, and led to de facto Armenian control of the region. Although periodically violated, the agreement largely upheld peace between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh until 2020.

### How did the 2020 conflict end?

The 2020 conflict ended with a ceasefire brokered by [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia) on November 9. Armenia relinquished military control over Nagorno-Karabakh, and Russian peacekeepers were deployed to the region.

### What happened to Nagorno-Karabakh in 2023?

In 2023 Azerbaijan took control over Nagorno-Karabakh, leading to the dissolution of the self-declared Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh. By October more than 100,000 ethnic Armenians fled the region.

**Nagorno-Karabakh**, [region](https://www.britannica.com/science/region-geography) of southwestern [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan). The name is also used to refer to an [autonomous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/autonomous) _oblast_ (province) of the former Azerbaijan Soviet Socialist Republic (S.S.R.), whose population was predominantly [Armenian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Armenian-people), and to the Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh, a self-declared country whose independence from 1992 to 2023 was not internationally recognized. The old autonomous region occupied an area of about 1,700 square miles (4,400 square km), while the forces of the self-proclaimed Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh occupied some 2,700 square miles (7,000 square km). The region’s status was a source of tension between Azerbaijan and [Armenia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenia), which fought major conflicts in the area in 1988–94 and in 2020. In 2023 Azerbaijan took control over the Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh, which subsequently dissolved itself, and Armenia’s [prime minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prime-minister), [Nikol Pashinyan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nikol-Pashinyan), appeared ready to accept Azerbaijan’s [sovereignty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sovereignty) over Nagorno-Karabakh.

The general region includes the northeastern flank of the Karabakh Range of the [Lesser Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lesser-Caucasus) and extends from the crest line of the range to the margin of the [Kura River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kura-River) lowland at its foot. Nagorno-Karabakh’s [environments](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/environments) vary from [steppe](https://www.britannica.com/science/steppe-grassland) on the Kura lowland through dense forest of [oak](https://www.britannica.com/plant/oak), [hornbeam](https://www.britannica.com/plant/hornbeam), and [beech](https://www.britannica.com/plant/beech) on the lower mountain slopes to birchwood and alpine meadows higher up. The peaks of the Karabakh Range culminate in Mount Gyamysh (12,218 feet \[3,724 meters\]). Vineyards, orchards, and mulberry groves for silkworms are intensively developed in the valleys of Nagorno-Karabakh. Cereal grains are grown, and cattle, sheep, and pigs are kept. The region has some light industry and many food-processing plants. [Xankändi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Xankandi) (formerly Stepanakert) is the chief industrial center.

## First conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh (1988–94)

The region was acquired by [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia) in 1813, and in 1923 the [Soviet](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) government established it as an [Armenian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Armenian-people)-majority [autonomous](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/autonomous) oblast of the [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan) S.S.R. Detached from the Armenian S.S.R. to the west by the Karabakh Range, Nagorno-Karabakh thus became a minority enclave within Azerbaijan. The region developed quietly through decades of Soviet rule, but in 1988 the ethnic Armenians of Nagorno-Karabakh began agitating for the transfer of their oblast to Armenian jurisdiction, a demand that was strongly opposed by both the Azerbaijan S.S.R. and the Soviet government. Ethnic antagonisms between Armenians and Azerbaijanis grew inflamed over the issue, and, when Armenia and Azerbaijan gained their independence from the collapsing Soviet Union in 1991, Armenians and Azerbaijanis in the enclave went to war.

During the early 1990s the Karabakh Armenian forces, supported by [Armenia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenia), gained control of much of southwestern Azerbaijan, including Nagorno-Karabakh and territory connecting the enclave with Armenia. A series of negotiations followed—guided by Russia and a committee informally known as the “Minsk Group” (named for an [envisioned](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/envisioned) peace conference in [Minsk](https://www.britannica.com/place/Minsk), [Belarus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belarus), that was not realized)—that failed to reach a lasting resolution but did manage to yield a ceasefire agreement in 1994, which, though periodically violated, was largely upheld.

## Ceasefire and de facto Armenian control (1994–2020)

The ongoing search for a political solution to the conflict between Armenia and Azerbaijan was further complicated by the disputed territory’s political [aspirations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/aspirations). The self-proclaimed Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh declared its independence in early 1992 and held several independent elections in the decades that followed, as well as a 2006 referendum that approved a new constitution. Azerbaijan declared these actions illegal under [international law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/international-law). The independence of the self-proclaimed enclave nation was never internationally recognized.

In November 2008 Armenian Pres. [Serzh Sargsyan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Serzh-Sarkisyan), who was born in Nagorno-Karabakh, and Azerbaijani Pres. [Ilham Aliyev](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ilham-Aliyev) signed a landmark agreement—the first such agreement in 15 years—pledging to intensify efforts toward a resolution of the conflict over the Nagorno-Karabakh region. Despite occasional gestures of rapprochement between the two countries, episodic clashes occurred throughout the 2010s.

## Second conflict (2020) and return to Azerbaijani control

A new government in Armenia in 2019 brought hope of a fresh start to negotiations over Nagorno-Karabakh, but a breakdown in [diplomacy](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/diplomacy) in 2020 led to clashes in July. Though the clashes were brief, the region prepared for the possibility of escalation: Russia, a guarantor of Armenian security, conducted unilateral military drills near the [Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caucasus) only days after the ceasefire. [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) soon afterward held joint military exercises with Azerbaijan.

Amid the heightened tensions, clashes once again broke out on September 27. With both sides more prepared for sustained fighting than they had been in July and with Azerbaijan emboldened by Turkey’s firm support, the conflict quickly escalated to its worst fighting since the early 1990s. Heavy casualties and damage were sustained from a brutal ground war aided by the use of cluster munitions and [ballistic missiles](https://www.britannica.com/technology/ballistic-missile). The fighting was further characterized by the use of [drones](https://www.britannica.com/art/drone-music) whose footage helped fuel an extensive information war on [social media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-media).

With Armenian forces devastated by the war, Aliyev and Armenian Prime Minister [Nikol Pashinyan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nikol-Pashinyan) agreed on November 9 to a ceasefire deal brokered by Russia. The deal required Armenia to relinquish its military control over Nagorno-Karabakh and allowed Russian peacekeepers to guard the region for five years. The deal also guaranteed that [Xankändi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Xankandi) (Stepanakert) would retain access to Armenia through the Lachin Corridor mountain pass.

Russia’s role as a guarantor for Armenian security was [mitigated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/mitigated), however, by the demands of its full-scale [invasion of Ukraine](https://www.britannica.com/event/Russia-Ukraine-War) in February 2022. Despite the ceasefire agreement between Armenia and Azerbaijan, the Lachin Corridor mountain pass was blockaded in December 2022, and Russia did little to intervene. A renewed offensive by Azerbaijan in September 2023 further solidified Azerbaijan’s control over Nagorno-Karabakh, and by October more than 100,000 of about 120,000 ethnic [Armenians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Armenian-people) in the region had fled. The autonomous Republic of Nagorno-Karabakh came to a [de facto](https://www.britannica.com/topic/de-facto) end, even decreeing its own dissolution, although it later retracted the decree in exile.

Negotiations between Armenia and Azerbaijan began in 2024, even as both countries filed cases with the [International Court of Justice](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Court-of-Justice) accusing the other of [ethnic cleansing](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ethnic-cleansing). In April the two countries moved to formally [delineate](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/delineate) their border and Armenia agreed to hand over four abandoned Azerbaijani villages that Armenia had controlled since the first conflict. In March 2025 negotiators announced that a peace deal had been reached. Azerbaijan refused to sign the accord, however, until Armenia removed from its constitution what Azerbaijan considers to be [implicit](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implicit) references to Armenia’s territorial claims over Nagorno-Karabakh. Pashinyan, meanwhile, pushed for a [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) referendum to take place in 2027.

- What is the Caucasus?

The Caucasus is a region and mountain system in Eurasia, located between the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea, encompassing parts of Russia, Georgia, Azerbaijan, and Armenia. The Caucasus Mountains consist of two ranges: the Greater Caucasus in the north and the Lesser Caucasus in the south.

The region is known for its diverse ethnic and linguistic groups, with more than 50 separate peoples speaking languages from four families: Caucasian, Indo-European, Turkic, and Semitic. Historically, the Caucasus has served as a gateway for migration between Central and Southwest Asia and as a buffer zone between rival empires.

The Caucasus region has played a significant role in history, serving as a route for cultural diffusion and experiencing waves of migration and conquest. After a period as a single unit under the Soviet system, the Caucasus was divided into the republics of Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia in 1936.

- What are the main mountain ranges in the Caucasus?

The Caucasus region, situated between the Black and Caspian Seas, features two principal mountain ranges: the [Greater Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caucasus/Introduction#ref1) in the north and the [Lesser Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lesser-Caucasus/Introduction#ref1) in the south.

Key mountains along its main axis include [Mount Elbrus](https://www.britannica.com/story/7-or-8-summits-the-worlds-highest-mountains-by-continent/Introduction#ref421866), the range's tallest peak at 18,510 feet (5,642 metres), Mount Dombay-Ulgen, and Mounts Shkhara, Dykhtau, and Kazbek.

The Lesser Caucasus runs northwest-southeast, merging with the Armenian Highland to the south. Few peaks in this range exceed 8,000 feet (2,400 m), with the highest peak, Mount Aragats, rising to 13,418 feet (4,090 meters).

- What is the significance of the Caucasus Mountains for the people living there?

The Caucasus Mountains, situated between the Black and Caspian Seas, are significant to the people living there due to several factors:

  - **Cultural diversity** The Caucasus is home to over 50 distinct ethnic groups, each with its own language and traditions. This has led to the region being called the Mountain of Languages.

  - **Economic resources** The area is rich in resources like oil, natural gas, metallic ores, and mineral water springs, fostering economic development. Agriculture is also well-suited to the region, allowing for the cultivation of crops and livestock.

  - **Climate barrier** The mountains act as a barrier, influencing the climate by impeding cold air masses from the north, thereby creating a unique subtropical climate in Transcaucasia.

  - **Historical importance** Throughout history, the Caucasus has served as a gateway for migration and a buffer zone between empires.

- How does the location of the Caucasus affect its culture and biodiversity?

The location of the [Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caucasus) region, situated between the Black and Caspian Seas, significantly influences its culture and biodiversity. Its position as a gateway of migration has resulted in extraordinary ethnic and cultural diversity, with more languages spoken here than in any other region of similar size.

The Greater Caucasus range acts as a barrier, influencing temperature and precipitation patterns in Ciscaucasia and Transcaucasia. **Caucasus**, mountain system and [region](https://www.britannica.com/science/region-geography) lying between the Black Sea (west) and the Caspian Sea (east) and occupied by [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia), [Georgia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Georgia), [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan), and [Armenia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenia).

![Caucasus Mountains](https://cdn.britannica.com/23/5923-004-7A08A3DC/Caucasus-Mountains.jpg)

[Caucasus Mountains](https://cdn.britannica.com/23/5923-004-7A08A3DC/Caucasus-Mountains.jpg) The Caucasus Mountains.(more)

The great historic barrier of the Caucasus Mountains rises up across the wide isthmus separating the [Black](https://www.britannica.com/place/Black-Sea) and [Caspian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caspian-Sea) seas in the region where [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) and [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia) [converge](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/converge). Trending generally from northwest to southeast, the mountains consist of two ranges—the [Greater Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greater-Caucasus) (Russian: Bolshoy Kavkaz) in the north and the [Lesser Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lesser-Caucasus) (Maly Kavkaz) in the south. [Mount Elbrus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Elbrus) in the Greater Caucasus range, at 18,510 feet (5,642 metres), is the highest peak. The watershed of the Greater Caucasus, the backbone of the system, traditionally has been part of the line dividing Europe and Asia, but Europe’s eastern boundary has been the subject of much debate. One widely accepted scheme draws the dividing line along the crest of the Greater Caucasus range, putting the portion of the region north of the line in Europe and the portion south of it in Asia. Another puts the western portion of the Caucasus region in Europe and the eastern part (the bulk of Azerbaijan and small portions of Armenia, Georgia, and Russia’s Caspian Sea coast) in Asia. Still another scheme identifies the [Aras River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Aras-River) and the Turkish border as the line of continental demarcation, thereby locating Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia in Europe.

The name Caucasus is a Latinized form of Kaukasos, which the ancient Greek geographers and historians used; the Russian Kavkaz is of the same origin. The ultimate derivation is thought to be from Kaz-kaz, the [Hittite](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hittite) name for a people living on the southern shore of the Black Sea. This ancient [nomenclature](https://www.britannica.com/science/nomenclature) reflects the historical importance of the region: in [Greek mythology](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Greek-mythology) the range was the scene of the sufferings of [Prometheus](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Prometheus-Greek-god), and the [Argonauts](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Argonaut-Greek-mythology) sought the Golden Fleece in the land of [Colchis](https://www.britannica.com/place/Colchis) (the modern [Kolkhida](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kolkhida-coastal-plain-Georgia) Lowland of Georgia), which nestles against the range on the Black Sea coast. The ranges also became a major land route to the north for cultural [diffusion](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diffusion) of the Middle Eastern [Fertile Crescent](https://www.britannica.com/place/Fertile-Crescent) civilizations. The peoples of the region have exhibited an extraordinary ethnic and cultural [diversity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diversity) since early times: the [Colchians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Colchian), for example, as described in the 5th century bce by the Greek historian [Herodotus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Herodotus-Greek-historian), were black-skinned Egyptians, though their true origin remains unclear. In subsequent centuries, successive waves of peoples migrating across Eurasia added to and were molded by the more established groups in the region. Not surprisingly, a greater variety of languages is spoken in Caucasia than in any other area of similar size in the world.

Caucasia includes not only the mountain ranges of the Caucasus proper but also the [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state) immediately north and south of them. The land north of the Greater Caucasus is called [Ciscaucasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ciscaucasia) (Predkavkazye, or “Hither Caucasia”) and that south of it is [Transcaucasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Transcaucasia) (Zakavkazye, or “Farther Caucasia”). The whole region, which has an area of 170,000 square miles (440,000 square km), is nevertheless predominantly mountainous. It extends southward from the lowlands of the Kuma and Manych river basins (the [Kuma-Manych Depression](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuma-Manych-Depression)) in the north to the northern frontiers of [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) and [Iran](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran) in the south and so [comprises](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprises) the southernmost portion of Russia (including [Dagestan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dagestan-republic-Russia) and several other administrative units [constituted](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituted) on an ethnic basis) and the Transcaucasian republics of [Georgia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Georgia), [Armenia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenia), and [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan).

## Physical features

## Physiography

The Greater Caucasus range extends for approximately 750 miles (1,200 km) southeastward across the Caucasian isthmus from the Taman Peninsula, which separates the Black Sea from the [Sea of Azov](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sea-of-Azov), to the [Abşeron Peninsula](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abseron-Peninsula), which juts into the Caspian Sea east of the oil-rich port of [Baku](https://www.britannica.com/place/Baku), Azerbaijan. The vast plains and highlands of Ciscaucasia stretch from the northern foothills of the Greater Caucasus to the [Kuma-Manych Depression](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuma-Manych-Depression), running from the Sea of Azov to the Caspian Sea. Western Ciscaucasia consists largely of plains, such as the extensive lowland north of the [Kuban River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuban-River) that slopes gradually upward to the foothills of the mountains farther south. Central Ciscaucasia includes the [Stavropol Upland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Stavropol-Upland), characterized mainly by tablelands of [limestone](https://www.britannica.com/science/limestone) or [sandstone](https://www.britannica.com/science/sandstone) separated by deep valleys; the Mineralnye Vody-Pyatigorsk zone to the southeast, where Mount Beshtau rises to 4,593 feet (1,400 metres) from the surrounding plateau; and, still farther to the southeast, the Terek and the Sunzha ranges, separated by the Alkhanchurt Valley. Eastern Ciscaucasia is a [lowland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lowlands-region-Scotland) [traversed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/traversed) by the lower [Terek River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Terek-River) and, to the north beyond the sands of the vast Nogay Steppe, by the Kuma River. Both rivers flow into the Caspian Sea.

The northern slopes of the Greater Caucasus are not as steep as the southern. The middle of the system is comparatively narrow, but its western and eastern ends have widths of 100 miles (160 km) or more. The main axis of the system contains, in addition to Mount Elbrus, [Mount Dombay-Ulgen](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Dombay-Ulgen) (Dombey-Yolgen; 13,274 feet \[4,046 metres\]), in the western sector; Mounts [Shkhara](https://www.britannica.com/place/Shkhara), [Dykhtau](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Dykhtau), and [Kazbek](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Kazbek), all over 16,000 feet (4,800 metres), in the central sector; and [Mounts](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/Mounts) [Tebulosmta](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Tebulosmta) and [Bazardyuzyu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Bazardyuzyu), both over 14,600 feet (4,550 metres), in the east. Spurs tonguing north and south from the main axis occasionally reach elevations approaching 10,000 feet (3,000 metres).

South of the Greater Caucasus, on the Black Sea coast, lies the alluvial Kolkhida Lowland, site of ancient Colchis. South of the range on the Caspian side, the Shirak Steppe, between the Greater and Lesser Caucasus ranges, falls sharply into the [Kura-Aras (Kura-Araks](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kura-Aras-Lowland)) Lowland. At the centre of this extensive depression the [Kura River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kura-River) receives its major right-bank tributary, the [Aras (Azerbaijani: Araz) River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Aras-River). To the northeast the hills of southeastern Kobystan separate the Kura-Aras Lowland from the Abşeron Peninsula; and to the extreme southeast the narrow Länkäran Lowland extends south between the Caspian Sea and the [Talish (Talysh) Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Talish-Mountains), which reach elevations exceeding 8,000 feet (2,400 metres).

West of the Kura-Aras Lowland rises the Lesser Caucasus range, which is extended southward by the Dzhavakhet Range and the [Armenian Highland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenian-Highland), the latter extending southwestward into Turkey. East of [Lake Sevan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Sevan) in the eastern Lesser Caucasus, the highest peaks rise above 12,000 feet (3,600 metres), while [Mount Aragats](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Aragats) (Alagöz), the highest peak in the range, rises west of the lake to 13,418 feet (4,090 metres). From their western sources in the Armenian Highland, the Kura and Aras rivers both flow around the Lesser Caucasus—the Kura to the north of the range and the Aras to the south—to their [confluence](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/confluence) in the east.
