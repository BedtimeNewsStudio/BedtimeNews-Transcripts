The **M48 Patton** is an American [first-generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_main_battle_tanks_by_generation#First "List of main battle tanks by generation") [main battle tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_battle_tank "Main battle tank") (MBT) introduced in February 1952, being designated as the **90mm Gun M48**, armored, full-tracked, combat vehicle of the medium-gun tank class.[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-10) It was designed as a replacement for the [M26 Pershing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M26_Pershing "M26 Pershing"), [M4 Sherman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M4_Sherman "M4 Sherman"), [M46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M46_Patton "M46 Patton") and [M47 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M47_Patton "M47 Patton") tanks, and was the main battle tank of the [US Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Army "US Army") and [US Marine Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Marine_Corps "US Marine Corps") in the Vietnam War.[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-11)[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):5 Nearly 12,000 M48s were built, mainly by [Chrysler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler "Chrysler") and [American Locomotive Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Locomotive_Company "American Locomotive Company"), from 1952 to 1961. The M48 Patton was the first US medium gun tank[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-13) with a four-man crew, featuring a centerline driver's compartment and no bow machine gunner.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-14) As with nearly all new armored vehicles it had a wide variety of suspension systems, [cupola styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupola_(military) "Cupola (military)"), power packs, fenders and other details among individual tanks.

The early designs, up to the M48A2C, were powered by a gasoline engine. The M48A3 and A5 versions used a diesel engine. However, gasoline engine versions were still in use in the US Army National Guard through 1968 and by many West German Army units through 1975. Numerous examples of the M48 saw combat in various [Arab–Israeli conflicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_conflict "Arab–Israeli conflict") and the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"). Beginning in 1959, most American M48A1s and M48A2s were upgraded to the M48A3 model.[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-15)

The M48 Patton-series saw widespread service with the United States and [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO "NATO") until it was superseded by the [M60 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_tank "M60 tank"). It was widely exported. The tank's hull also became the basis for a wide variety of experimental, utility and support vehicles such as armored recovery vehicles and bridge layers. Some M48A5 models served into the mid-1980s with US Army [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Guard_(United_States) "National Guard (United States)") units, and M48A3s were used as targets for weapons and radar testing into the mid-1990s.

Many M48s remain in service in countries other than the US. Most of these have been modified and their firepower, mobility and protection upgraded to increase their combat effectiveness on the modern battlefield. As of 2015,[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=M48_Patton&action=edit) Turkey is the largest operator with over 750 units in service, Taiwan is second with approximately 500 upgraded variants, and Greece is third with 390 in service.

## History

After the conclusion of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), the United States [Ordnance Tank-Automotive Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Tank-automotive_and_Armaments_Command "United States Army Tank-automotive and Armaments Command") (OTAC) drastically slowed or canceled many tank development and design programs. On 7 November 1950, the Ordnance Technical Committee mandated a change in the nomenclature for tanks in the US military.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-1)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):2 It was decided that weight designations (Light, Medium, Heavy) were no longer applicable due to changes in the way tanks were developed and employed on the battlefield, and the varying calibers of main guns now available. Thus the caliber of the gun replaced its weight designation. For example, the M103 Heavy Tank was redesignated as the [120mm Gun Tank M103](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M103_(heavy_tank) "M103 (heavy tank)") and the Light Tank M41 Walker Bulldog as the [76mm Gun Tank M41 Walker Bulldog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M41_Walker_Bulldog "M41 Walker Bulldog").[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB)

The M47 Patton entered production in 1951 and was used by the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army "United States Army") and [Marine Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marine_Corps "United States Marine Corps") but ongoing technical and production problems kept it from serving in the [Korean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War "Korean War"). This forced the US to field older tank models, such as the [M26 Pershing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M26_Pershing "M26 Pershing") and [M46 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M46_Patton "M46 Patton"). In response, the Army launched several design projects for a replacement of the M46 and M26. The United States entered a period of frenzied activity during the crisis atmosphere of the Korean War, when the US seemed to lag behind the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") in terms of tank quality and quantity.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks197122–23-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks197122–23)

Testing and development cycles occurred simultaneously with production to ensure speedy delivery of new tanks. Such rapid production caused problems but the importance given to rapidly equipping combat units with new tanks precluded detailed testing and evaluation prior to its quantity production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1998_Cameron-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-1998_Cameron):31 Notable among these were the [T42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T42_medium_tank "T42 medium tank"), [T69](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T69_(tank) "T69 (tank)") and T48 projects as well as continuing to pursue further improvements to the M47.

Compromise was inevitable, but not always welcomed, as General [Bruce C. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_C._Clarke "Bruce C. Clarke") ironically observed: "We know exactly what we want. We want a fast, highly mobile, fully armored, lightweight vehicle. It must be able to swim, cross any terrain, and climb 30 degree hills. It must be air-transportable. It must have a simple but powerful engine, requiring little or no maintenance. The operating range should be several hundred miles. We would also like it to be invisible".[&#8202;](#ref-back-1960_Clarke-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-1960_Clarke):11

### T48 project

The T48 project was to focus on improving the turret of the [M47 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M47_Patton "M47 Patton") and upgrading performance with a more powerful yet more efficient gasoline engine. 1⁄4 and 1⁄8 scale design [mockups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mockup "Mockup") of the turret were constructed during May 1950 using the T119 90mm main gun of the [M47 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M47_Patton "M47 Patton"). The design study was accepted by the Army in December and a contract for the advanced production design and engineering (APE) of a 90mm armed tank was awarded to the Chrysler Defense.

Hull redesign included moving the driver's station to the front center and removal of the bow-mounted machine gun and its associated crew station, which was converted to safe container storage for additional main gun ammunition.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02) The front glacis was sloped to offer much better ballistic protection than former welded and rather flat designs.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-1)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02) An aircraft style steering wheel (a [yoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoke_(aircraft) "Yoke (aircraft)")) replaced lever steering. The power pack consisted of the Continental AV-1790-5B gasoline engine producing 704 brake horsepower coupled to an Allison CD-850-4A cross-drive transmission with 2 forward and 1 reverse ranges. The hull armor was increased to 4in (100mm) on the front glacis of rolled homogeneous steel. It had six roadwheel pairs per side and five return rollers, a torsion bar suspension system, and used T97E2 steel tracks. A new hemisphere-shaped turret eliminated the M47s noted [shot traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_trap "Shot trap") and lowered the vehicle's height.

T48 pilot No. 1 was constructed by Chrysler Engineering to begin testing at the OTAC Detroit Arsenal Test Center in December 1951. Six prototypes were built in all.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1987_Hilmes-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-1987_Hilmes):11

On 27 February 1951, shortly after the outbreak of the [Korean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War "Korean War"), the Ordnance Technical Committee Minutes (OTCM) #33791 initiated the simultaneous production and design refinement of the new tank, designating the production tanks as the 90mm Gun Tank M48.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt201585,_152-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt201585,_152) The Army planned to produce some 9,000 M48s within three years of development. Chrysler Corporation became the principal producer of the tank. Expected production and teething troubles led to the creation of integrating committees to coordinate tank and component development. These Army Combat Vehicle (ARCOVE) committees included military and industrial representatives who provided early warning of defects and recommended remedies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-2)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):21–22

Testing trials of the T48 tanks began in February 1952 and continued until the end of 1955. However, the perceived immediate threat of Soviet aggression in Western Europe and the ongoing Korean War impelled Army senior leadership to rush the T48 tank into series production before the inevitable bugs could be worked out of the new tank design. Instead, it was decided that any needed design changes uncovered by the continued testing and evaluation of the T48 tanks at the OTAC Detroit Arsenal Test Center would be incorporated into the M48 series production vehicles as quickly as possible.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-3)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):21–27

T48 pilot No. 1 was designed and constructed by Chrysler Engineering to begin the APE design development at the OTAC Detroit Arsenal Test Center in December 1951. This tank was provisionally armed with the M36/T119 gun using a Y-shaped deflector and a muzzle brake.[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto1-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-auto1) This gun was not used for production M48 tanks. It used the Mod A hull and turret designs with smaller diameter crew hatches. The driver's hatch incorporated a mechanism that dropped his three periscope heads to provide clearance for the hatch door as it swung to the right, and the driver then had to reposition the periscopes by hand once the hatch was closed again. It had 5 return rollers, 6 roadwheel pairs per side with the drive sprocket at the rear and a torsion bar suspension system.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\]

Pilot No. 2 was built in February 1952 and also used the early Mod A design with small hatches. It was fitted with the T139/M41 90mm gun replacing the muzzle brake with a cylindrical blast deflector. A T-shaped deflector was used for production M48 tanks. This vehicle had two machine guns installed, a .30cal M1919E4 mounted coaxially to the left of the main gun and a .50cal M2HB mounted on the commander's cupola.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-1)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] The full image stereoscopic T46E1/M12 rangefinder was placed in the central section of the turret.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-2)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02)

Pilot No. 3 was constructed in November 1952 and used the Mod B hull and turret designs with a simplified, larger diameter hatch that was easier for the driver to operate. Also the front vision block was removable and allowed for the future use of IR vision blocks by the driver. It also had 5 return rollers, 6 roadwheel pairs per side with the drive sprocket at the rear and a torsion bar suspension system. The Mod B turret also had larger hatches for the commander and loader. An additional three hulls were constructed (T48 pilots 4 through 6) in 1953. These tanks were used into 1955 for the component development of the M48A2 production tanks including fire control systems, turret cupolas, suspension configurations and [powerpacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerpack_(drivetrain) "Powerpack (drivetrain)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-25) At least one of these hulls was fitted with experimental silica glass composite armor panels.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1958_Salter-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-1958_Salter)

### Components

#### Armor

In conjunction with the development of the T48 project there was some discussion regarding armor. The weight of conventional armored steel needed to provide protection against the emerging large-caliber high-velocity main guns and improved APDS kinetic energy penetrators was making its continued use impractical. Composite [applique armor panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applique_armour "Applique armour") made with [fused silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_quartz "Fused quartz") glass was envisioned to be fitted to the hull. It was also desired that a turret be constructed using this special armor.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1958_Salter-1)[\[25\]](#refnote-1958_Salter) The [OTAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Tank-automotive_and_Armaments_Command "United States Army Tank-automotive and Armaments Command") and [Carnegie Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institute_of_Technology "Carnegie Institute of Technology") began development of the armor in November 1952 at [Fort Belvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Belvoir "Fort Belvoir") VA as Project TT2-782/51.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1958_Salter-2)[\[25\]](#refnote-1958_Salter) This composite armor offered protection against HEAT, [HESH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-explosive_squash_head "High-explosive squash head"), and HE rounds. Its overall slow development limited its use to the T48 and was dropped from consideration in the M48 by 1953, however its development was continued with the [T95 Medium Tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T95_Medium_Tank "T95 Medium Tank") until 1958.

There were two different hulls used for the M48 series. The M48 hull had a wedge-shaped front glacis compared to the M46's rather flat design. Early Mod A hulls had smaller diameter driver's hatches. The suspension consisted of six roadwheel pairs per side with a torsion bar suspension system and five return rollers. The engine exhaust vents were located on the top of the rear deck. There was a dual compensating idler arm at the front, and dual auxiliary track tension wheels behind the last road wheels, using the T97E2 track assembly. The drive sprocket was located at the rear of the hull. Shock absorbers were mounted at the first two and last roadwheel arms. This hull design was manufactured for the original M48 and M48A1 versions of the M48 series.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners) Many Mod B hull designs used for the M48A1 (T48 Pilot #3) were further upgraded to the A2 hull design standard but retained their original 5 return roller configuration.

The M48A2 hull design saw several modifications including a reduction from five to three return rollers per side. The suspension system was simplified, a more compact [powerpack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerpack_(drivetrain) "Powerpack (drivetrain)") compartment and additional fuel cells, with large louvered grill access doors replaced the complicated grill work of the rear engine decks of the M48/M48A1 hull designs. This hull was used on newly constructed M48A2 and M48A3 tanks of the M48 series.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-1)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners)

The turret used for the M48 series was hemispherical in shape and was made from cast homogeneous steel. It had a flat gun shield and a standard pop-up styled hatch for the commander and an additional hatch for the loader's station. Early production Mod A models of the turret had somewhat smaller diameter hatches. The gunner was provided with an M37 .30 cal machine gun with 5,900 rounds of ammunition.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-2)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners)

#### Rangefinders and fire control systems

During WWII and the Korean War, most tanks used a direct sighting system where the gun sights and rangefinders were slaved directly to the gun's barrel but its long range accuracy depended upon the focusing abilities of the individual gunner. There are two basic types of optical [rangefinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangefinders "Rangefinders"), stereoscopic and coincidence. With a [stereoscopic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoscopic_rangefinder "Stereoscopic rangefinder") range determination occurred by measuring the distance from the observer to a target using the observer's capability of [binocular vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binocular_vision "Binocular vision"). The [coincidence rangefinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coincidence_rangefinder "Coincidence rangefinder") uses a single eyepiece. Light from the target enters the rangefinder through two windows located at either end of the instrument. The ARCOVE emphasis upon increased long range accuracy led to the incorporation of a [fire control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-control_system "Fire-control system") (FCS)[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-28) in the M48.

The fire control system included a rangefinder, mechanical ballistic computer, ballistic drive, and gunner's sight. Collectively, these mechanical devices resembled in miniature the fire control systems used in [naval gunnery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_gunnery "Naval gunnery"). Only after the Second World War did such systems become small enough for use in combat vehicles. These mechanical fire control systems permitted tanks to engage effectively at much longer ranges than in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), a critical consideration for the Army, expecting to enter the European battlefield outnumbered. Instead of a gunner's sight being slaved to the gun tube, the ballistic computer and drive computed the range and elevated the gun. The gunner's primary responsibility lay in keeping the sight on the target. The mechanical ballistic computer made a more accurate computation of range possible by mathematically accounting for such factors as vehicle cant and ammunition type.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-4)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):1–2 Many developmental range finders based on pulses of IR light, such as the Optical Tracking, Acquisition and Ranging (OPTAR) rangefinder of the [T95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T95_Medium_Tank "T95 Medium Tank") continued into 1957.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Ogorkiewicz-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-1991_Ogorkiewicz):174

#### Autoloader systems

Another requirement of the T48 project was to explore the use of [autoloaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoloader "Autoloader") for the main gun as they became available. Preliminary experiments with a loading system using the T48 turret were unsuccessful due to the limited space and the need to line up the breech with the loading system after each firing. It also was dropped from consideration for use on the M48 but was further developed on the [T69 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T69_(tank) "T69 (tank)") as the 90mm T178 gun. It was fitted with an eight-round auto-loader system mounted in an oscillating turret.[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto1-1)[\[22\]](#refnote-auto1)

#### 90 mm main gun

ARCOVE also involved the Ordnance Department with the goal of improving the 90mm gun and its ammunition. The Army expected difficulties in engagements with the Soviet [IS-3 heavy tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IS_tank_family#Object_703_IS-3 "IS tank family"), since the M47's M36/T119 90mm rifled main gun could not consistently penetrate its frontal armor, even with special armor-piercing capped (APC) or HEAT ammunition.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1954_Conference-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-1954_Conference):12 Work was performed to address this by developing the improved T300E53 HEAT ammunition and the T137 series of hypervelocity armor-piercing discarding-sabot (HVAP-DS or simply APDS) ammunition for the M36 gun. The new APDS round could penetrate 11.1in (280mm) of homogeneous steel armor, angled at 30 degrees, at a distance of 1,000yd (910m), but was not accepted for service.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1954_Conference-1)[\[29\]](#refnote-1954_Conference):12

Instead it was decided to use an improved version of the 90mm gun, the T139 for the M48 production tanks. It allowed for easier barrel changing and weighed less than the preceding M36/T119 gun. It was later standardized as the 90mm Gun M41 in February 1951.

#### Turret cupolas

The T48 and M48(Mod A) tanks featured a remote controllable machine gun mount as the tank commander's weapon on a pedestal, which allowed him to fire the .50 caliber (12.7mm) M2HB machine gun from within the vehicle's turret. It used a 100-round ammunition box and could also be employed manually by the commander. However, servicing and reloading of the weapon required the tank commander to open his roof hatch and expose his head and upper torso. This remote-controllable mount was designated as the .50 Caliber Machine Gun Mount, M1 and designed by Chrysler Defense Engineering.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

By October 1954, the Army decided to amend the design of the tank by installing a cupola. During the production of M48s it was modified several times resulting in a wide mix of cupola styles among individual tanks. Afterwards it was renamed as the Cupola, Tank Commander's Caliber .50 Machine Gun, M1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1987_Hilmes-1)[\[20\]](#refnote-1987_Hilmes):23 The new tank commander cupola would allow the tank commander to aim and fire his weapon while remaining under armor protection via a remote controlled M2HB machine gun, however the commander had to open the cupola hatch and expose his head to reload or service the machine gun due to the limited headroom.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] These cupolas had a small rearward opening hatch and a single, non-removable vision block. They were designed by [Aircraft Armaments Incorporated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AAI_Corporation "AAI Corporation"). Because of its smaller turret roof hatch opening those M1 cupolas retro-fitted to early production M48 Mod A turrets used an adaptor ring.

The M1E1 cupola design used a G305 cupola riser with 9 non-removable vision blocks installed (some versions had 7 with the 2 rear blocks deleted) between the turret roof and the cupola. It also came with a new bulged hatch cover to provide the tank commander with more headroom and allowed him to reload the weapon while remaining under armor protection. A major drawback of both these cupolas was their inability to mount either daylight or infrared vision devices. The [receiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_(firearms) "Receiver (firearms)") of the M2HB took up a great deal of space in the already cramped cupola's interior. Also due to restraints in the cupola, smaller 50 round ammunition boxes were used. Development of its eventual replacement, the [T9/M19 cupola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_Patton#M19_commander's_cupola "M60 Patton") of the XM60 tank was continued into 1958 and some M19 cupolas were retro-fitted to M48A5s to allow for the use of IR and daylight periscopes by the commander.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015109–111-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015109–111)

### M48

_"Land Battleship: M48"_ (1952) - Official US Army promotional film.

Chrysler began building the [Newark Tank Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newark_Assembly "Newark Assembly") in Newark, Delaware, in March 1951 to produce the M48 while Chrysler Defense Engineering and ARCOVE continued advanced production engineering (APE) to evolve the design using the T48 prototypes at the OTAC Detroit Arsenal. In May 1952 Chrysler agreed to take control of a Government-Owned and Contractor Operated (GOCO) basis of the Newark Tank plant production facility with the US Government and continue production design refinement of the T48 at the OTAC Detroit Arsenal. To meet the urgent need for tanks, production contracts were also awarded to [General Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors "General Motors") [Fisher Body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_Body "Fisher Body") Division (Grand Blanc Tank Plant) and [Ford Motor Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Motor_Company "Ford Motor Company") (Livonia Tank Plant) to produce the tank in Michigan starting in April 1952. Also in July 1952 the Army awarded [American Locomotive Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Locomotive_Company "American Locomotive Company") a $200million contract to start producing the tank in 1953 at the Schenectady Tank Plant in New York.[&#8202;](#ref-back-TankOrder-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-TankOrder) All four companies were given initial production contracts in that same year for around 400 tanks each. The first production tank was unveiled on 1 July 1952 as the 90mm Gun Tank M48 and christened the M48 Patton by Mrs. Beatrice Ayer Patton, widow of the late General [George S. Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_S_Patton "George S Patton").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Christened-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-Christened)

The M48 featured a gasoline-powered engine, different models were used depending on availability and manufacturer. Originally the M48 (Mod A) that was built by Chrysler Defense at the OTAC Test Center (T48 Pilot #1) in 1951 used the Continental AV-1790-5B coupled to a General Motors CD-850-4A cross-drive transmission as used for the M47.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015317-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015317) The M48s built by American Locomotive used a Continental AV-1790-5C gasoline engine and either an Allison CD-850-4A or 4B cross-drive transmission. Additionally a two-cylinder, gasoline, air-cooled engine (sometimes referred to as the "Little Joe" by tank crews) was provided to power a 28 volt, 300 ampere generator when the main engine was not needed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-3)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02)

Fuel capacity was 200USgal (760L) providing a cruising range of about 70mi (110km). The suspension consisted of six roadwheel pairs with a torsion bar suspension system and five return rollers. There was a dual compensating idler arm at the front, and dual auxiliary track tension wheels behind the last road wheels, as had the preceding M46 and M47 tanks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-4)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02) The M48 used the new 28in (0.71m) wide T97E2 track assembly.

It had hydraulic shock absorbers mounted on the first, second and sixth roadwheel pairs (opposite of the M46 and M47). The bow mounted machine gun and its related crew station was eliminated thus providing more ammunition storage for the main gun. The driver's station was moved to the front center of the hull. The steering controls were redesigned. A large aircraft-styled steering wheel (replacing the wobble stick control of the M46 and M47) and placing the transmission range selector on the floor to the driver's right.

Mod A hulls had a small oval overhead hatch for the driver. T48 Pilots No. 1 and 2 incorporated a mechanism that dropped the three periscope heads to provide clearance for the hatch door as it swung to the right, and the driver then had to reposition the periscopes by hand once the hatch was closed again. The vehicle did not have an [NBC protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC_protection "NBC protection") system for the crew and a fording depth of approximately 1.2m (3.9ft). It had a crew of 4; the commander, gunner and loader were positioned in the turret and the driver stationed in the front center of the hull.

The M48's turret consisted of a redesigned flat gun shield and standard pop-up hatch for the commander's station. The M48 Mod A turret design had smaller roof hatches for both the commander and loader. A .50 cal M2HB using a M1 remote control mount on a pedestal was available for the commander. When not in use, the machine gun mount could be stowed on a bracket welded to the turret roof in front of the loader. The M48's direct fire control system consisted of an M12/T41 stereoscopic rangefinder with a field of view of 5 degrees and magnification of x7.5, an azimuth indicator, an M20 gunner's periscope and a T13 super elevation actuator. Two main bearings are bolted to the mounting plate on the roof of the tank turret to support the range finder on steel radial ball bearings. A parallel gun linkage between the gun trunnion and the range finder assures that the line of sight of the range finder reproduces the exact motion of the gun in elevation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-TM9718A-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-TM9718A):60

A .30 caliber M1919E4 machine gun was coaxially mounted to the main gun's left and was operated by the gunner. An M12 full field optical range finder was used, the same as the M47. The tank was not fitted with any night fighting or computerized fire control systems.[&#8202;](#ref-back-M47_PattonMania-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-M47_PattonMania) Some early production M48s used a Y-shaped blast deflector on the barrel and carried 53 rounds. Eight ready rounds were stored in the left side of the turret bustle for the loader, the rest were stored inside safe containers in the hull. Tank crews complained that the Y-shaped fume extractor was found to be prone to snagging brush. It was replaced with a T-shaped model early in production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-5)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02)

These early M48 tanks suffered from engine, transmission, track, and suspension problems. The first production vehicles suffered from excessive oil consumption and engine failures after only 1,000mi (1,600km). The gasoline engine managed only 0.33 mpg US (0.14km/L), limiting range to 75 miles (121km). The M12/T41 rangefinder was too fragile and often broke. Army Field Forces (AFF) found these tanks to be unsuitable for combat use in Europe and was regulated to limited use by US Army CONUS units until numerous shortcomings were corrected. Furthermore, some 120 hulls were found to have insufficient hull armor protection. These were denoted as the M48C and relegated by the [Continental Army Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Training_and_Doctrine_Command "United States Army Training and Doctrine Command") (CONARC) for non-ballistic training use by the Armor School at [Fort Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Knox "Fort Knox") to train crewmembers and maintenance personnel.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks197125-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks197125)

### M48A1

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/M48_Medium_Tank_Patton_at_Fort_Greely_Alaska%2C_14th_April_1955.jpg/500px-M48_Medium_Tank_Patton_at_Fort_Greely_Alaska%2C_14th_April_1955.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48_Medium_Tank_Patton_at_Fort_Greely_Alaska,_14th_April_1955.jpg) Late series M48A1 during cold weather tests at Fort Greely, Alaska, 1955. The main gun is equipped with a T-shaped blast deflector on the muzzle and the driver's station is under a protective cover.[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/79/Tensions_ran_high_at_Checkpoint_Charlie_in_1961_as_Easterners_fled_to_West%2C_Berlin_Wall_went_up.jpg/500px-Tensions_ran_high_at_Checkpoint_Charlie_in_1961_as_Easterners_fled_to_West%2C_Berlin_Wall_went_up.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tensions_ran_high_at_Checkpoint_Charlie_in_1961_as_Easterners_fled_to_West,_Berlin_Wall_went_up.jpg) Two US Army M48A1s of F Co., 40th Armor of the [Berlin Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Brigade "Berlin Brigade") face off in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin") against [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army "Red Army") [T-54s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-54 "T-54") during one of several standoffs at [Checkpoint Charlie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkpoint_Charlie "Checkpoint Charlie") in 1961.

The Army concluded in January 1952 that the T42[&#8202;](#ref-back-1952_Baldwin-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-1952_Baldwin) had failed as a tank capable of combat in Europe against Soviet tanks and reached the same conclusion in November 1953 for the M48 Mod A versions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1998_Cameron-1)[\[17\]](#refnote-1998_Cameron):32 The M48A1 focused on improving the engine and the vehicle's operational range and fixing numerous mechanical problems. To cope with the lack of range, an add-on modification allowed the use of 4 external jettisonable 55USgal (210L) fuel drums of [MOGAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas "Avgas") (motor gasoline) that was mounted to the rear deck, extending the range to 135mi (217km). This proved very unpopular with tank crews and operational range remained unsatisfactory.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2016_Pike-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-2016_Pike)

Further ARCOVE changes included a desire for the commander to observe and operate his weapons station while remaining under armor protection.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2016_Pike-1)[\[38\]](#refnote-2016_Pike) At this same time the US Army Field Forces (AFF) declared the T42 medium tank unfit for production in November 1952, mainly due to serious shortcomings of its hull design. Ford's production of the M48 ended in August 1953 after a fire destroyed most of the Livonia Tank Plant.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2020_Jackson-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-2020_Jackson)

During evaluation by the Army Review Committee on Development of Equipment (ARCOVE), testing on the T48 pilot No. 1 and No. 2 prototypes revealed that the original driver's hatch on the M48 Mod A hull restricted driver comfort and posture when operating the tank with the hatch open. To address this clearance issue, a redesigned, larger driver's hatch was developed and integrated into series production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-41) Also the driver's front vision block was removable and could be replaced with an infrared IR vision block. A redesigned power pack featured an AV-1790-7B gasoline engine and CD-850-4B transmission.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-3)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners) The turret was fitted with the M1 cupola that enclosed the M2HB machine gun. Cupolas fitted on those tanks with early production Mod A turrets used an adapter ring.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-6)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02) In April 1953, the Army standardized this configuration as the 90mm Gun Tank M48A1 Patton[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-2)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] and originally applied this designation to both the early Mod A hull design and the Mod B hulls currently in series production.

Between April 1952 and December 1954, nearly 7,000 M48s and M48A1s were produced, with production contracts for an additional 2,500 tanks to be built through 1956.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks1971-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks1971)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] Army units in Europe immediately received 2,120 of these early M48A1 tanks, but correction of ARCOVE defects discovered after production delayed the fielding of the remaining tanks. The Marines Corps however continued to use the [M47 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M47_Patton "M47 Patton"). The M48A1's width proved too wide for many European tunnels, complicating rail transport.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks1971-1)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks1971)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] Operational readiness rates of M48-equipped units tended to be low. The tanks continued experiencing engine, transmission, track, and suspension problems, and the M12 stereoscopic rangefinder was uncomfortable to operate.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks1971-2)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks1971)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\]

However, the M48A1 was considered an even match for its Soviet counterpart, the T-54 and fielded to combat units in Europe. On 25 October 1954, the Army decided to amend its designation of the M48 tank. Thereafter, the tanks with the original Mod A hull design and the Chrysler-designed M1 remote-control machine gun mount attached to the tank commander's pop-up hatch would remain the 90mm gun tank M48. However, all those tanks with the Mod B hull (some production M48A1 tanks had the Mod B turret) and fitted with the new small turreted M1 cupola armed with a .50 caliber M2HB machine gun would be designated as the 90mm gun tank M48A1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks1971-3)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks1971)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] M48A1 tanks with 5 return rollers and the ModB driver's hatch were modified to the M48A2 hull configuration. The hull's armor framing for the engine compartment was modified. In addition detachable headlights, armored boxes around the taillights, newly redesigned mudguards, an updated fuel delivery system and a tank infantry phone were added.

### M48A2

The concurrent testing of the T48 and production of the M48 was the source of widespread debate among Congressional Budget Oversight committees. The [Bureau of the Budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Management_and_Budget "Office of Management and Budget") believed that the Army was not progressing with sufficient speed in its tank modernization program and recommended the immediate replacement of the M48A2 as well as better quality control. Under the ARCOVE's "single, efficient producer" model, Defense Secretary [Charles Erwin Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Erwin_Wilson "Charles Erwin Wilson") directed the Army to reduce the number of contractors producing each model of tank. In a new round of production bids for the M48A2, [General Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors "General Motors") underbid Chrysler, and in September 1953 [Army Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_the_Army "United States Secretary of the Army") [Robert T. Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_T._Stevens "Robert T. Stevens") awarded GM's [Fisher Body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_Body "Fisher Body") division a $200million contract to become the sole producer of the M48A2 and started production at its [Grand Blanc Tank Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Blanc_Metal_Center "Grand Blanc Metal Center") in Michigan in April 1953.[&#8202;](#ref-back-underbid-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-underbid)

The decision raised skepticism among lawmakers. Senator [Estes Kefauver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estes_Kefauver "Estes Kefauver") noted the move would effectively leave GM as the only producer of light (the [M41 Walker Bulldog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M41_Walker_Bulldog "M41 Walker Bulldog")) and medium tanks when the Chrysler production contract for M48A1 production was set to expire in April 1956. Months later Chrysler underbid GM in the new round of bidding. In September 1954 the Army awarded Chrysler an exclusive $160.6million contract to restart production.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chrysler_underbids-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-Chrysler_underbids) In November 1955 the Army awarded [American Locomotive Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Locomotive_Company "American Locomotive Company") a $73million contract to begin producing 600 M48A2s the next year at the Schenectady Tank Plant, New York.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Alco-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-Alco)

However Alco opted to wrap up its tank business when its contract ended in July 1956. In May the Army awarded Chrysler, the only bidder, a $119million contract to continue production of the M48A2 at the Delaware Tank Plant.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1957_NYT-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-1957_NYT) In December 1955 Chrysler took on orders initially intended for the American Locomotive after Alco opted production cutbacks to its tank program[&#8202;](#ref-back-American_Locomotive_canceled-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-American_Locomotive_canceled) and became the only manufacturer of the tank by the end of 1956.

During the ARCOVE Questionmark III conference in June 1957 it was predicted that the Congress would not approve the procurement of the M48A2 after the fiscal year 1959. However, the ongoing [T95 Tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T95_Medium_Tank "T95 Medium Tank") Program was progressing slowly as its new technologies were being developed. Several ideas were proposed to replace the M48A2. General [Bruce C. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_C._Clarke "Bruce C. Clarke") wrapped competing and even conflicting desires in irony, observing that "We know exactly what we want. We want a fast, highly mobile, fully armored, lightweight vehicle. It must be able to swim, cross any terrain, and climb 30-degree hills. It must be air-transportable. It must have a simple but powerful engine, requiring little or no maintenance. The operating range should be several hundred miles. We would also like it to be invisible".[&#8202;](#ref-back-48-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-48)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ae/R32_tank_arrangement.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:R32_tank_arrangement.jpg) Envelope-stretching [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") powered R-32 tank design proposal

Some ideas proposed were to use a [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") power plant in the R-32 or a vapor-cycled power plant fueled by hydrocarbons in the [Chrysler TV-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_TV-8 "Chrysler TV-8") tank.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015123–127-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015123–127) In response to these findings an eventual replacement for the M48A2 was recommended. ARCOVE and the Deputy Chief of Staff, Logistics (DCSLOG) submitted a proposal for a tank based on the M48A2 featuring improved firepower and a [compression ignition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_ignition "Compression ignition") engine and started the design development of the XM60 in September 1957.

The engine deck was redesigned with two large louvered doors that replaced the complicated grill work of the rear engine decks of the M48/M48A1 hull designs. This new rear hull arrangement on the M48A2 tank helped in cooling the engine and minimizing the infrared (heat) signature of the vehicle.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1992_Crismon-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-1992_Crismon):73 The suspension system was simplified. The track tensioner arm was eliminated, modifications to the idler arm, the addition of bumper springs and friction snubbers, and a relocation of the air cleaner assembly. The number of return rollers was reduced to 3 per side. With the more compact AVI-1790-8 engine, additional fuel tanks were installed in the engine compartment, increasing fuel capacity to 335 gallons.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-3)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] Many older model M48A1s were rebuilt to this production configuration but retained their original 5 return rollers per side.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1999_Decker-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-1999_Decker):144

The M48A2C saw improvements for the turret implementing the Mod B turret design with larger hatches for the commander and loader. The M12 rangefinder was replaced with a full-field coincidence model along with an improved turret control system. The M13 Fire Control System (FCS) consists of the M5A2 ballistic drive and mechanical M13A3 gun data computer which integrated barrel temperature data[&#8202;](#ref-back-1980_M60-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-1980_M60) with an M17 coincidence range finder.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-4)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners) The rangefinder is a [double image coincidence image instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coincidence_rangefinder "Coincidence rangefinder") used as the ranging device of the gunner's primary direct sighting and fire control system. The gunner is provided with an M20 day periscope with a magnification of x8 and an M105D day telescopic sight with a magnification of x8 and a field of view of 7.5 degrees.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1980_M60-1)[\[50\]](#refnote-1980_M60)

Range information from the rangefinder is fed into the ballistic computer through a shaft. The ballistic computer is a mechanically driven unit that permits ammunition selection, range correction, and superelevation correction by the gunner. The ballistic drive receives the range input and, through the use of cams and gears, provides superelevation information to the superelevation actuator. The superelevation actuator adds sufficient hydraulic fluid to the elevating mechanism to correctly position the gun.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEIcks197125-1)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEIcks197125)

M48A2s were widely deployed to both the US Army and [US Marine Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marine_Corps "United States Marine Corps") fully replacing the [M47 Patton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M47_Patton "M47 Patton") in combat units as well as being exported to NATO allies and foreign governments. In 1967 M48A2 tanks still in service with National Guard units were upgraded to the A3 configuration. The M48A2 hull received modified armored boxing around the taillights, an updated fuel delivery system and a tank infantry phone (TIP). The gasoline engine was replaced with the AVDS-1790-2A. They remained in [CONUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONUS "CONUS") training use with the [Army National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_National_Guard "Army National Guard") through 1979.

### M48A3

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Vietnam._Marines_of_E_Company%2C_2nd_Battalion%2C_3rd_Marines%2C_riding_on_an_M-48_tank._-_NARA_-_532441.jpg/500px-Vietnam._Marines_of_E_Company%2C_2nd_Battalion%2C_3rd_Marines%2C_riding_on_an_M-48_tank._-_NARA_-_532441.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Vietnam._Marines_of_E_Company,_2nd_Battalion,_3rd_Marines,_riding_on_an_M-48_tank._-_NARA_-_532441.jpg) Infantry of E Company, 2nd Battalion, 3rd Marines, riding on an M48A3 in Vietnam, 1966

Citing high fuel consumption, poor low-end torque, and the extreme fuel flammability of gasoline engines, ARCOVE industrial representatives had expressed desires for a diesel engine for the M48 since 1953.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2014_DavidB_02-7)[\[19\]](#refnote-2014_DavidB_02) Continental Motors and OTAC began to develop an experimental X-shaped vapour-cycled engine design powered by hydrocarbons in 1954 for the [T95 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T95_Medium_Tank "T95 Medium Tank") but it remained unreliable. In June 1955, OTAC adopted the recommendations and allowed a [diesel engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine "Diesel engine") to be used if it significantly improved fuel economy.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1987_Hilmes-2)[\[20\]](#refnote-1987_Hilmes):31

By August 1956 the diesel powered AVDS-1790 was recommended as meeting this requirement,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-4)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\] and the Army requested the initial retrofitting of approximately 1,020 older M48A1s and A2s with the new engine in December.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-5)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners) By February 1957, the Army had around 600 converted M48A3 Patton tanks and the Marine Corps had received 419.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2015_Tencza-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-2015_Tencza) Because many M48A3 tanks were conversions from earlier models many details varied among individual examples of this type. M48A3 tanks could have either three or five support rollers on each side and might have either the early or later type headlight assemblies, some retained their earlier Mod A turrets and different cupola styles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-5)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\]

During the [Hungarian Revolution of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956 "Hungarian Revolution of 1956"), a Soviet [T-54A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-54A_tank "T-54A tank") medium tank was driven onto the grounds of the UK's embassy in Budapest by the Hungarians.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2018_Beast-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-2018_Beast) After a brief examination of this tank's armor and [100mm gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-10_tank_gun "D-10 tank gun") by a British military attaché, the UK decided that their [20 pounder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_QF_20_pounder "Ordnance QF 20 pounder") (84mm L/66.7) was apparently incapable of defeating it. There were also rumors of an even larger [115mm gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-5TS "U-5TS") in the works.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENordeenIsby2010137-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENordeenIsby2010137) In response to this the US Army started [development of the XM60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_tank#XM60_development "M60 tank") to replace the M48 tank series and incorporating the ARCOVE recommendation of a more powerful main gun, the [Royal Ordnance L7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Ordnance_L7 "Royal Ordnance L7") (105mm T254). Instead of immediately getting a 105mm main gun, large stockpiles of 90mm main gun ammunition and funding shortfalls that made it impossible to supply enough 105mm main gun rounds for all the planned tank conversions left the M48A3s with their M41 90mm guns.

In addition to the conversion of older model M48s, Chrysler continued to build new vehicles. These used the M48A2 hull design (with 3 return rollers) and had redesigned fenders and mudguards, armored boxes around the taillights among other minor details. They were fitted with another re-designed commander's cupola, the M1E1. It had a modified hatch to allow more room in comparison to the previous M1 cupola and the two rear vision blocks were eliminated.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt201587-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt201587) The driver's steering wheel was replaced with a T-bar control and received a padded seat.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2019_Conners-6)[\[26\]](#refnote-2019_Conners) M48 procurement for the Army ceased and M48A2 hull production ended in May 1961. The Newark Tank plant was closed in October.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015103-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015103) This newest version of the M48 series received the designation Tank, Combat, Full-Tracked; 90mm Gun, M48A3 on 19 March 1958.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1991_Terry-5)[\[11\]](#refnote-1991_Terry):29

The M48A3 was withdrawn from Europe by October 1961, being replaced by the [M60 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_tank "M60 tank"). As US armored and cavalry units rotated out of combat deployments to [South Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Vietnam "South Vietnam") most of their M48A3s were either directly transferred to the [South Vietnamese army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Republic_of_Vietnam "Army of the Republic of Vietnam") or to [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand"). [FORSCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FORSCOM "FORSCOM") withdrew the M48A3 from combat service with both the [US Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Army "US Army") and [US Marine Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Marine_Corps "US Marine Corps") in 1973, replacing them with the M60A1. Some M48A3s continued in service with National Guard units until 1979. Some were repurposed as [Armoured vehicle-launched bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armoured_vehicle-launched_bridge "Armoured vehicle-launched bridge") (AVLBs). Afterwards they were relegated to target use for the testing of radar and weapons systems until the mid 1990s.[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-57) They were replaced in this role by the QM60.

### M48A4

While the M60A1E1, later standardized as the M60A2, was under development, it appeared there would be many leftover M60 turrets, this resulted in a plan to transfer these turrets to M48A1 hulls modernized to the M48A3 standard. The turrets were essentially the same, although fitted with different guns, rangefinders and commander's cupolas.[&#8202;](#ref-back-58-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-58) However, the M60 turret featured a deeper basket necessitating the addition of spacers on the turret ring which elevated the turret by 2 inches. Also, the ammunition stowage had to be rearranged to accommodate the 105 mm ammunition, which was done by modifying the M60A1 racks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-59-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-59)

Two of these tanks were built, with one being sent to Fort Knox during the spring of 1967, the program was approved to retrofit 243 M48A1 tanks to this new configuration. The tank was planned to be designated as the 105 mm Gun, Full-Tracked, Combat Tank M48A4, however due to the decision to limit the amount of M60 tanks converted to M60A2s, the project was cancelled with no more tanks being built.[&#8202;](#ref-back-60-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-60)

### M48A5

The M48 was retrofitted with a 105 mm gun, which fit nicely in its turret, and the M60 power pack, which was an idea that was explored since 1958, and the M48A1E1 tank passed the trials in 1960. However, the cost of replacing large stores of 90 mm ammunition was considered too high, and the idea dragged without adoption for a decade and a half until the 90 mm gun became hopelessly obsolete.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-:0)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/C155AmpShipTurkey.jpg/500px-C155AmpShipTurkey.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:C155AmpShipTurkey.jpg) Türkish made M48A5T2 in amphibius excercise.

The [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War "Yom Kippur War") in 1973 demonstrated high attrition rates of tanks due to ATGMs and, besides diverging hundreds of American tanks to make up Israeli losses, forced the US Army to raise its inventory objectives from 8,300 initially to 10,300 and by 1976 to 14,400 tanks. The opened gap had to be closed as quickly and cheaply as possible, so it was decided to rearm and re-engine reserve M48s. The program cost on average under $170,000 ($961,842in 2025) per tank[&#8202;](#ref-back-62-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-62) ($110,000 for diesel M48A3s and $236,000 for gasoline M48A1/A2Cs), which was about three times cheaper than a new M60A1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-63-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-63)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/28/M48A5_dozer_Team_Spirit_1984.jpg/500px-M48A5_dozer_Team_Spirit_1984.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48A5_dozer_Team_Spirit_1984.jpg) A US M48A5 Patton with a dozer blade participates in [Team Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Team_Spirit "Team Spirit") in Korea, 1984

The last major US upgrade of the M48 tank series was initially designated XM736, then M48A3E1 and was finally standardized as M48A5 in May 1975.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-1)[\[60\]](#refnote-:0)

This conversion upgrade was applied to M48A3 versions still in service with [Army National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_National_Guard "Army National Guard") units in order to maintain the training levels of Guard units as well as using a commonality in ammunition amongst tanks. The upgrade featured the mounting of the [M68 105mm gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Ordnance_L7 "Royal Ordnance L7") carried in the M116 mount and the all-metric measurement M16 fire control system. The hull was upgraded by applying the [M60A1 RISE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_Tank "M60 Tank") Hull PIP Update Kit and incorporated as many of the components as possible.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Rolf_Hilmes_1987_pp._11-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-Rolf_Hilmes_1987_pp._11)

Some of these included the retrofit of the AVDS-1790-2C RISE diesel engine incorporating TLAC engine panels coupled to with a CD-850-6 cross-drive transmission, a 300-gallon fuel capacity and the T142 track assembly, M13A1 [NBC protection system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBRN "CBRN") for the crew and the replacement of the .30 caliber M37 coaxial machine gun with the 7.62mm NATO [M219](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M73_machine_gun "M73 machine gun")/T175 machine gun.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Rolf_Hilmes_1987_pp._11-1)[\[63\]](#refnote-Rolf_Hilmes_1987_pp._11) Because all M48A5 tanks were conversions from earlier models, many characteristics varied among individual examples of this type. M48A5 tanks could have either three or five support rollers on each side and might have either the early or later type headlight assemblies, some retained their earlier cupola styles.

Starting in 1975 M48A3 tanks that were still in service with the [Army National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_National_Guard "Army National Guard") were converted to the M48A5 standard. These tanks were initially converted from former M48A3 tanks using the M48A1 hull design (with 5 return rollers) and given the designation M48A5PI. But, after August 1976 these early hull conversions were further upgraded, the Product Improvement (PI) designation was removed, and these tanks were redesignated as the M48A5. By March 1978, 708 M48A5 tanks had been converted from the M48A1 hull model.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-6)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015):242 In conjunction with the conversion of M48A1 tank hulls, the conversion of M48A3s mated to M48A2 hulls (with 3 return rollers) to the M48A5 standard was also implemented in 1976. The conversion program ran from October 1975 to December 1979 with a total of 2,069 M48A3s converted to the A5 standard.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-7)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015):242

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/M48_Aggressor_Sentry_Castle_1981.jpg/500px-M48_Aggressor_Sentry_Castle_1981.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48_Aggressor_Sentry_Castle_1981.jpg) An M48A5 of B Co., 1st Battalion/ 127th Armor Regiment, [42nd Infantry Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/42nd_Infantry_Division_(United_States) "42nd Infantry Division (United States)"), New York Army National Guard performing as the [OPFOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposing_force "Opposing force") force during joint exercise Sentry Castle 1981 at [Ft. Drum, NY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Drum "Fort Drum").

They were used by Guard units in [CONUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONUS "CONUS") to maintain their training levels and proficiency. In May 1987, at [Fort McCoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_McCoy,_Wisconsin "Fort McCoy, Wisconsin"), 1st Battalion, 632nd Armor Regiment of the [Wisconsin Army National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_Army_National_Guard "Wisconsin Army National Guard") retired the last M48 tanks in the US military. The tanks were parked in a holding pen at the WI ARNG Mobilization and Training Equipment Site (MATES) and later transferred to the [Moroccan Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_Army "Moroccan Army"). They were replaced in National Guard service by the M60A3.[&#8202;](#ref-back-65-0)[\[64\]](#refnote-65) However many other countries continue to use these M48 models.

Israel's wartime experiences with their M48/E48C [Magach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magach "Magach") tanks spurred the [IDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Defense_Forces "Israel Defense Forces") to implement its own changes. These included replacing the M1 cupola with a low-profile "Urdan" type that mounted an [M60D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_machine_gun "M60 machine gun") machine gun for use by the tank commander. A second M60 machine gun was mounted on the turret roof for use by the loader. Internal ammunition stowage for the 105mm main gun was also increased to 54 rounds. Many of them were fitted with Blazer [ERA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactive_armour#Explosive_reactive_armour "Reactive armour") panels.

### E48 series

M48s for use in foreign military service were designated as the E48 series by the [US Foreign Military Sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Military_Sales "Foreign Military Sales") (FMS). These were essentially M48s with minor modifications requested by approved foreign purchasers. Some of the modifications included removal of the M1 cupola, different models of machine guns, electronics, fire control systems or radios, external armor plates, smoke launchers and power packs.[&#8202;](#ref-back-66-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-66) Israel purchased many of these tanks forming the genesis of the [Magach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magach#Versions "Magach") series.

This series included the following designations:

- **E48**: modified M48/M48A1 variant for non-US service
- **E48A**: modified M48A2 variant for non-US service
- **E48B**: modified M48A3 variant for non-US service
- **E48C**: modified M48A5 variant for non-US service
- **E48 AVLB**: modified M48 AVLB variant for non-US service

## Combat service

### Asia

### Vietnam War

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Vietnam....Marines_riding_atop_an_M-48_tank_cover_their_ears_as_te_90mm_gun_fires_during_a_road_sweep_southwest_of..._-_NARA_-_532483.tif/lossy-page1-500px-Vietnam....Marines_riding_atop_an_M-48_tank_cover_their_ears_as_te_90mm_gun_fires_during_a_road_sweep_southwest_of..._-_NARA_-_532483.tif.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Vietnam....Marines_riding_atop_an_M-48_tank_cover_their_ears_as_te_90mm_gun_fires_during_a_road_sweep_southwest_of..._-_NARA_-_532483.tif) US Marines riding atop an M48 in Vietnam in April 1968

The M48 saw extensive action with the US military during the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"). Over 600 Pattons would be deployed with US forces during that war.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-8)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\][&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-0)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry) The initial M48s first landed with the US Marine 1st and 3rd Tank Battalions in 1965,[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-0)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan) with the 5th Marine Tank Battalion later becoming a back-up/reinforcement unit. The remaining Pattons deployed to South Vietnam were in three US Army battalions, namely the [1-77th Armor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/77th_Armor_Regiment_(United_States) "77th Armor Regiment (United States)") near the [DMZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_Demilitarized_Zone "Vietnamese Demilitarized Zone") (67 M48A2C (23 tanks supplied from US Army Training Center at [Fort Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Knox "Fort Knox"), and 44 tanks from [Letterkenny Army Depot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterkenny_Army_Depot "Letterkenny Army Depot")) tanks were used by the 77th Armor from August 1968 to January 1969.

These were later replaced with M48A3s, the [1-69th Armor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/69th_Armor_Regiment "69th Armor Regiment") in the [Central Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Highlands_(Vietnam) "Central Highlands (Vietnam)") of central South Vietnam and the [2-34th Armor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/34th_Armor_Regiment_(United_States) "34th Armor Regiment (United States)") positioned near the [Mekong Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong_Delta "Mekong Delta"). Each battalion consisted of approximately 57 tanks. M48s were also used by Armored Cavalry Squadrons in Vietnam until replaced by [M551 Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M551_Sheridan "M551 Sheridan") Armored Reconnaissance Airborne Assault Vehicles (ARAAV) in the Divisional Cavalry Squadrons. M48A3 tanks remained in service with the [11th Armored Cavalry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11th_Armored_Cavalry_Regiment "11th Armored Cavalry Regiment") until the unit was withdrawn from the conflict. Some M48 variants, nicknamed "Zippos", or M67A1 [Flame tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_tank "Flame tank") were used by some U.S Marine Corps units, but the US Army no longer used them.[&#8202;](#ref-back-69-0)[\[68\]](#refnote-69) From 1965 to 1968, 120 US M48A3 tanks were written off.[&#8202;](#ref-back-armor.kiev.ua-0)[\[69\]](#refnote-armor.kiev.ua)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/ee/Men_of_Troop_B%2C_Vietnam.jpg/500px-Men_of_Troop_B%2C_Vietnam.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Men_of_Troop_B,_Vietnam.jpg) M48 of B Troop, 1st Squadron, 10th Cavalry, [4th Infantry Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4th_Infantry_Division_(United_States) "4th Infantry Division (United States)"), in the Central Highlands, June 1969

The M48 Patton has the distinction of playing a unique role in an event that was destined to radically alter the conduct of armored warfare.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-1)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan) When US forces commenced redeployment operations, many of the M48A3 Pattons were turned over to the [Army of the Republic of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Republic_of_Vietnam "Army of the Republic of Vietnam") (ARVN) forces, in particular creating the battalion-sized ARVN 20th Tank Regiment, which supplemented their [M41 Walker Bulldog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M41_Walker_Bulldog "M41 Walker Bulldog") units. During the [North Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam "North Vietnam") [People's Army of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Army_of_Vietnam "People's Army of Vietnam") (PAVN) [Easter Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Offensive "Easter Offensive") in 1972, tank clashes between PAVN [T-54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-54 "T-54")/ [PT-76](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PT-76 "PT-76") and ARVN M48/M41 units became commonplace.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-2)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan)

On 23 April 1972, tankers of the 20th Tank Regiment were attacked by a PAVN infantry-tank team, which was equipped with the new _[9M14M Malyutka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9M14_Malyutka "9M14 Malyutka")_ (NATO designation: Sagger) wire-guided [anti-tank missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-tank_missile "Anti-tank missile"). During this battle, one M48A3 Patton tank and one [M113](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M113_armored_personnel_carrier#M113_armored_cavalry_assault_vehicle_(ACAV)_variant "M113 armored personnel carrier") Armored Cavalry Assault Vehicle (ACAV) were destroyed, becoming the first losses to the Sagger missile; losses that would echo on an even larger scale a year later during the [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War "Yom Kippur War") in the Middle East in 1973.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-3)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan) By 2 May, the 20th Tank Regiment had lost all of their tanks to enemy fire.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-1)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry):212 During the first month of the [First Battle of Quảng Trị](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Tr%E1%BB%8B "First Battle of Quảng Trị"), all ARVN M48 Pattons (100 tanks) were lost.[&#8202;](#ref-back-71-0)[\[b\]](#refnote-71)[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Ngo-0)[\[70\]](#refnote-1979_Ngo):52

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/NARA_111-CCV-372-CC33992_25th_Infantry_Division_M48A3_Patton_moving_through_Viet_Cong_territory_Operation_Lincoln_1966.jpg/500px-NARA_111-CCV-372-CC33992_25th_Infantry_Division_M48A3_Patton_moving_through_Viet_Cong_territory_Operation_Lincoln_1966.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NARA_111-CCV-372-CC33992_25th_Infantry_Division_M48A3_Patton_moving_through_Viet_Cong_territory_Operation_Lincoln_1966.jpg) A 1st Battalion, 69th Armor M48 during [Operation Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Lincoln_(Vietnam) "Operation Lincoln (Vietnam)")

The M48s performed admirably[&#8202;](#ref-back-1987_Nolan-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-1987_Nolan) in South Vietnam in the infantry-support role. However, there were few actual tank-versus-tank battles. One was between the US 1-69th Armor and PT-76 light amphibious tanks of the PAVN 202nd Armored Regiment at [Ben Het Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Het_Camp "Ben Het Camp") in March 1969.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-4)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan) The M48s provided adequate protection for its crew from small arms, mines, and [rocket-propelled grenades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket-propelled_grenade "Rocket-propelled grenade"). South Vietnamese M48s and M41s fought in the [1975 Spring Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Spring_Offensive "1975 Spring Offensive"). In several incidents, the ARVN successfully defeated PAVN [T-34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-34 "T-34") and [T-55](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-55 "T-55") tanks and even slowed the North's offensive.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1976_NM-0)[\[72\]](#refnote-1976_NM):10

However, due to shortages of fuel and munitions faced by the South Vietnamese military because of the US Congress-placed ban on the further funding and supply of military equipment and logistics to the country, the American-made tanks soon ran out of ammunition and fuel and were quickly abandoned to the PAVN, which then put them in their service after the war ended in May 1975. In total, 250 of the ARVN's M48A3s were destroyed and captured[&#8202;](#ref-back-1976_NM-1)[\[72\]](#refnote-1976_NM):10 and those captured (at least 30) were only used briefly before being phased out and turned into war-memorial displays all over Vietnam.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/M48A3_Detonates_Mine_Vietnam.jpg/500px-M48A3_Detonates_Mine_Vietnam.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48A3_Detonates_Mine_Vietnam.jpg) A destroyed M48A3 during Vietnam war - victim of a mine.[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/M48_Tanks_in_Herringbone_Formation.jpg/500px-M48_Tanks_in_Herringbone_Formation.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48_Tanks_in_Herringbone_Formation.jpg) M48 tanks in a defensive herringbone formation along a road in Vietnam, west of [Pleiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiku "Pleiku"), April 1, 1966.

M48s, along with Australian [20 pounder (84mm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20_pounder "20 pounder")-gunned[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-2)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry):113[Centurions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion_tank "Centurion tank") of the [1st Armoured Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Armoured_Regiment_(Australia) "1st Armoured Regiment (Australia)"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-1982_Dunstan-5)[\[67\]](#refnote-1982_Dunstan) were the only vehicles in use by the anti-communist side in the Vietnam War that could reasonably protect their crews from [land mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_mine "Land mine"). They were often used for minesweeping operations along Highway 19 in the Central Highlands, a two-lane paved road between [An Khe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Khe "An Khe") and [Pleiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiku "Pleiku"). Daily convoys moved both ways along Highway 19. These convoys were held up each morning while the road was swept for mines. At that time, minesweeping was done by soldiers walking slowly over the dirt shoulders of the highway with hand-held mine detectors.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-3)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry):80

During this slow process, convoys would build up into a dangerously inviting target for the enemy, especially their guerillas and partisans. As a result, a faster method was improvised, the _"Thunder Run"_, in which one M48 lined up on each side of the road, with one track on the dirt shoulder and the other track on the asphalt, and then with all guns firing,[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-4)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry):80 they raced to a designated position miles away. If the M48s made it without striking a mine, the road was clear, and the convoys could proceed. In most cases, an M48 that struck a land mine in these operations only lost a road wheel or two in the explosion; seldom was there any hull damage that would be considered a [catastrophic kill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophic_kill "Catastrophic kill").[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-5)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry)

Supply of M48A3 tanks to South Vietnam:

- 1971: 54.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2015_Hatcher-0)[\[73\]](#refnote-2015_Hatcher)
- May 1972: 120.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2005_Moïse-0)[\[74\]](#refnote-2005_Moïse):135
- October 1972: 72.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2005_Moïse-1)[\[74\]](#refnote-2005_Moïse):135
- November 1972: 59.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1979_Starry-6)[\[66\]](#refnote-1979_Starry):218
- January 1973 - July 1974: 16.[&#8202;](#ref-back-77-0)[\[75\]](#refnote-77)
- Total: at least 321 M48 tanks.

According to official US data 343 M48s were delivered to the ARVN up to March 1975.,[&#8202;](#ref-back-78-0)[\[76\]](#refnote-78) all of which were destroyed or captured.

The United States lost at least 123 M48 tanks (non-repairable) during the war.[&#8202;](#ref-back-armor.kiev.ua-1)[\[69\]](#refnote-armor.kiev.ua) As a result, the United States with South Vietnam lost about 500 M48 tanks.

### Indo-Pakistani wars

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/M48_Patton.jpg/500px-M48_Patton.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48_Patton.jpg) A [Pakistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Army_Armoured_Corps "Pakistan Army Armoured Corps") M48 Patton advances at [Chumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chumb "Chumb") in 1965.

M47s and M48s were used in tank warfare by the [Pakistan Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Army "Pakistan Army") against the [Indian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Army "Indian Army")'s Soviet T-55s, British Centurions and US M4 Sherman tanks in both the [Indo-Pakistani War of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1965 "Indo-Pakistani War of 1965") as well as the [following war in 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971 "Indo-Pakistani War of 1971") with at least some good results. During [Operation Grand Slam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Grand_Slam "Operation Grand Slam") Pakistani tank forces, composed mainly of M47 and M48 Patton tanks, thrust through the Indian defense-lines very quickly and swiftly defeated Indian Army armored counterattacks. At the Battle of Chumb, India's only tank battalion in the region was wiped out.

The Pakistanis used approximately a division's worth of tanks in the operation, although not all were Pattons, with upgraded Shermans included as well. In contrast, Pakistan's Patton tank failed to live up to its high expectations in the [Battle of Asal Uttar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Asal_Uttar "Battle of Asal Uttar") in September 1965, where about 97 Pakistani tanks were lost, the majority of them being Pattons (M47s and M48s). Later, the Patton tank was the main Pakistani tank at the [Battle of Chawinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chawinda "Battle of Chawinda") and its performance at that battle was deemed satisfactory against Indian armor.

The Patton was later used by Pakistan again, this time, in the [Indo-Pakistani War of 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971 "Indo-Pakistani War of 1971"). A counterattack led by the 13th Lancers and the 31st Cavalry army units was defeated by the Indian 54th Division around [Battle of Barapind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Barapind "Battle of Barapind") in December 1971. The Pakistan Army Patton tanks could not stop an assault by Indian T-55 Soviet-supplied tanks of the 2nd Armored Brigade.[&#8202;](#ref-back-strategic_affairs-0)[\[77\]](#refnote-strategic_affairs) At least 9 of the Pattons were destroyed by T-55 tanks during the battle of Nainakot.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1995_Chakravorty-0)[\[78\]](#refnote-1995_Chakravorty):342 It total, more than 80 Pakistani Pattons were knocked out during the war, mainly by Centurion and T-55 fire.[&#8202;](#ref-back-strategic_affairs-1)[\[77\]](#refnote-strategic_affairs)

India later set up a temporary war-memorial so named " _Patton Nagar_" (or "Patton City") in [Khemkaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khemkaran "Khemkaran") District in Punjab, where the captured Pakistani Patton tanks were displayed for a short period of time before being scrapped or sent all across India for use as war monuments and military memorials.

Analyzing their overall performance in their wars with India, the Pakistani military held that the Patton was held in reasonably-high esteem by both sides and that combat-tactics were to blame for the setback at Asal Uttar.[&#8202;](#ref-back-81-0)[\[79\]](#refnote-81) However, a post-war US study of the tank battles in South Asia concluded that the Patton's armor could, in fact, be penetrated by the [20-pounder tank gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_QF_20_pounder "Ordnance QF 20 pounder") (84mm) of the Centurion (later replaced by the even more successful L7 105mm gun on the Mk. 7 version which India also possessed) as well as the 75mm tank gun of the [AMX-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMX-13 "AMX-13") light tank.[&#8202;](#ref-back-82-0)[\[80\]](#refnote-82)

### Other countries

In the [2025 Cambodian-Thai conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Cambodian-Thai_conflict "2025 Cambodian-Thai conflict"), [The Royal Thai Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces "Royal Thai Armed Forces") employed M48A5PI tanks in combat against [Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodia "Cambodia"). Thai M48A5PI tanks engaged variety of Cambodian targets as videos showing these engagements were published by the Thai side.

In one incident, Royal Thai Army M48A5 tanks engaged 4 Cambodian T-55 tanks on the [Thai-Cambodian border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai-Cambodian_border "Thai-Cambodian border"), shortly after the video was posted, another video emerged depicting at least one Cambodian T-55 tank destroyed during the engagement.[&#8202;](#ref-back-83-0)[\[81\]](#refnote-83)

### Middle East

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Israeli_M48_tank_captured_by_Egypt.jpg/500px-Israeli_M48_tank_captured_by_Egypt.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Israeli_M48_tank_captured_by_Egypt.jpg) An ex-Israeli M48 captured by Egypt during the [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War "Yom Kippur War") in 1973

M48s were also used with mixed results during the [Six-Day War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War "Six-Day War") of 1967. On the [Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula "Sinai Peninsula") battlefront, [Israeli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel") M48s upgunned with the then-advanced 105mm L7 rifled tank gun were used with considerable success against [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") [IS-3s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IS-3_(tank) "IS-3 (tank)"), T-54s/T-55s, T-34/85s and [SU-100s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU-100 "SU-100") supplied by the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") during the 1950s and the 1960s (such as during the [Second Battle of Abu-Ageila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Abu-Ageila_(1967) "Battle of Abu-Ageila (1967)"). On the Sinai front, Israel lost 50 M48 tanks of 117: 39 M48A2C and 11 M48A3.[&#8202;](#ref-back-84-0)[\[82\]](#refnote-84) However, on the [West Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bank "West Bank") war-front, [Jordanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan "Jordan") M48s (Jordan was also a user of the M48 Patton as was Israel at the same time-period) were often defeated by Israeli 105mm-armed Centurions and WWII-era upgraded M4 Shermans - [M-51s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Sherman "Super Sherman") upgunned with French-built 105mm tank guns, not to be confused with the British L7 105mm tank gun.

In purely technical terms, the Pattons were far superior to the much-older Shermans, with shots at more than 1,000 meters simply glancing off the M48's armor. However, the 105mm main gun of the Israeli Shermans fired a [HEAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_explosive_anti-tank_warhead "High explosive anti-tank warhead") round designed to defeat the Soviet [T-62 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-62 "T-62"), which was the USSR's response to the M48's successor in US service, the [M60 tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_tank "M60 tank"). The Jordanian Pattons' general failure on the West Bank could also be attributed to excellent Israeli [air superiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_superiority "Air superiority").\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] The Israeli Army captured about 100 Jordanian M48 and M48A1 tanks and pressed them into service in their own units after the war, as were the Jordanian M113 APCs they seized during the war.

Israel used 445 M48 tanks in 1973 during the [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War "Yom Kippur War").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENordeenIsby201020-0)[\[83\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENordeenIsby201020) From 15 to 18 October, M48 tanks participated in the largest[&#8202;](#ref-back-86-0)[\[84\]](#refnote-86) tank battle of the war - [Battle of the Chinese Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Chinese_Farm "Battle of the Chinese Farm"). The battle involved the Egyptian [21st Armored Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21st_Armoured_Division_(Egypt)?action=edit&redlink=1 "21st Armoured Division (Egypt)") (136 T-55s),[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chinese_Farm-0)[\[85\]](#refnote-Chinese_Farm) 25th Armored Brigade (75 T-62s),[&#8202;](#ref-back-Gab_Battle-0)[\[86\]](#refnote-Gab_Battle) tank battalion (21 T-55s) from 24th Armored Brigade (in the total 232 Egyptian tanks) and the Israeli 143rd and 162nd Armored Divisions (about 440 tanks).[&#8202;](#ref-back-1973_Suez_Map-0)[\[87\]](#refnote-1973_Suez_Map)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Destroyed_Israeli_Armour_%28M48_Tank%29_During_Yom_Kippur_War.jpg/500px-Destroyed_Israeli_Armour_%28M48_Tank%29_During_Yom_Kippur_War.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Destroyed_Israeli_Armour_(M48_Tank)_During_Yom_Kippur_War.jpg) An Israeli M48 destroyed during fighting in the Yom Kippur War.

The battle ended with an Israeli victory, but both sides lost a huge number of tanks in this battle (each side lost about two hundred tanks). On the night of 15/16 October, the Israeli 14th Brigade of the 143rd Division lost 70 tanks out of 97. Between the 16th at 0900 and the 17th at 1400, the Israeli 143rd and 162nd Divisions have lost 96 tanks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1999_Razoux-0)[\[88\]](#refnote-1999_Razoux) As of 18 October, the Egyptian 21st Armored Division had no more than 40 tanks remaining of an original 136 tanks available at the start of the battle[&#8202;](#ref-back-2000_Gawrych-0)[\[89\]](#refnote-2000_Gawrych):64, 25th Armored Brigade had only 10 tanks of an original 75 T-62s.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chinese_Farm-1)[\[85\]](#refnote-Chinese_Farm)

Totally, when the [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War "Yom Kippur War") broke out, Israel had 540 M48-series (with 105mm gun) and M60/M60A1 tanks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-test-0)[\[90\]](#refnote-test)[&#8202;](#ref-back-SIPRI-0)[\[91\]](#refnote-SIPRI) During the war, the tanks suffered heavy losses. The location of flammable hydraulic fluid at the front of the turret was discovered to be a severe vulnerability. Egypt had destroyed a large number of Israeli tanks, and after the war, only 200 M48 and M60A1 tanks remained. Israel entrenched most of these tanks in the Sinai front against opposite entrenched Egyptian infantry armed with [9M14 Malyutka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9M14_Malyutka "9M14 Malyutka") anti tank missiles.[&#8202;](#ref-back-test-1)[\[90\]](#refnote-test)

Aside from the [Israel Defense Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Defense_Forces "Israel Defense Forces") (IDF), the M48 was also operated by the [Lebanese Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Armed_Forces "Lebanese Armed Forces"), the Christian [Lebanese Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Forces_(militia) "Lebanese Forces (militia)") militia, the [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Lebanon "Druze in Lebanon") [Progressive Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Socialist_Party "Progressive Socialist Party")'s [People's Liberation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Liberation_Army_(Lebanon) "People's Liberation Army (Lebanon)") militia, the Shia [Amal militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Resistance_Regiments "Lebanese Resistance Regiments") and the [South Lebanon Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Lebanon_Army "South Lebanon Army"). All the tanks seized by the militias were captured from the Army's mostly Druze [4th Infantry Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4th_Infantry_Brigade_(Lebanon) "4th Infantry Brigade (Lebanon)") which collapsed due to sectarian divisions in 1983. On 10 June 1982, eight Israeli M48A3s, two M60A1s and at least three M113 APCs were lost in a successful ambush by Syrian T-55 tanks and [BMP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMP-1 "BMP-1") infantry fighting vehicles (IFVs) during the [Battle of Sultan Yacoub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sultan_Yacoub "Battle of Sultan Yacoub") in 1982.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/49/AGM-65_M-48_post_impact.jpg/500px-AGM-65_M-48_post_impact.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:AGM-65_M-48_post_impact.jpg) A target QM48 struck by an [AGM-65](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-65 "AGM-65") Maverick missile during a weapons test

The Lebanese Army still operates about 100 M48s. In 2007, during the [2007 North Lebanon conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_North_Lebanon_conflict "2007 North Lebanon conflict"), Lebanese Army M48s shelled militant outposts located in a refugee camp.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2007_ynet-0)[\[92\]](#refnote-2007_ynet)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Sultan-0)[\[93\]](#refnote-Sultan)

Together with the M47, M48 tanks were used by the [Turkish Armed Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Armed_Forces "Turkish Armed Forces") during the [Turkish invasion of Cyprus in 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_operations_during_the_Turkish_invasion_of_Cyprus "Military operations during the Turkish invasion of Cyprus"). The [Turkish Armed Forces in Northern Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Armed_Forces_in_Northern_Cyprus "Turkish Armed Forces in Northern Cyprus") continue to use M48 tanks today.

When the [Kurdish–Turkish conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish%E2%80%93Turkish_conflict_(1978%E2%80%93present) "Kurdish–Turkish conflict (1978–present)") began, the Turkish Armed Forces had a number of M48s. These were used throughout the 1980s and the 1990s as static artillery and was used in defending military-base perimeters from enemy attacks.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Iranian M48 tanks were used widely in the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War "Iran–Iraq War") from 1980 to 1988, where they faced Iraqi T-55s, T-62s and T-72s, alongside M60 Pattons, in fierce and harsh combat with their Iraqi foes, with mixed results. M48s of the 37th Armored Brigade were used in the [Battle of Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Abadan "Battle of Abadan"). About 150 M48s were lost in this tank battle alone.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2011_Farrokh-0)[\[94\]](#refnote-2011_Farrokh):362

### Africa

In 1973, Morocco took delivery of its first M48A3s. By the end of the 1970s, further deliveries of M48A5 had occurred and the upgrade to M48A5 was achieved locally with the aid of US consultants. In 1987, a final shipment of 100 M48A5 tanks from the Wisconsin National Guard was delivered to the Moroccan army. There are unconfirmed reports of deliveries of Israeli M48A5s during the 1980s. The tanks were used in the Western Sahara desert against Polisario guerrillas.

Pakistan used M48 Pattons while reinforcing American troops during the [Battle of Mogadishu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mogadishu_(1993) "Battle of Mogadishu (1993)") in 1993.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1999_Bowden-0)[\[95\]](#refnote-1999_Bowden):324

## Variants

- **T48**: Developmental prototypes designed in 1950 and used between 1951 and 1955.
- **M48**: Initial production variant with Mod A hull & turret designs featuring M41/T139 90mm gun, M1 remote-controllable machine gun mount and AV-1790-5 or -7 gasoline engine. Deemed not fit for combat use in Europe, relegated to [CONUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONUS "CONUS") use only.[&#8202;](#ref-back-104-0)[\[102\]](#refnote-104)
  - **M48C**: M48 with incorrect hull ballistic protection. Relegated by CONARC to the [Armored Forces School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Armor_School "United States Army Armor School") at [Fort Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Knox "Fort Knox") for non-ballistic training of crewmembers and maintenance personnel.
- **M48A1**: First variant to have M1 cupola and Mod B hull design. Fitted with external fuel drums to extend its range.
- **M48A2**: Redesigned hull with simplified suspension system and louvered engine access doors, AVI-1790-8 fuel-injected gasoline engine and Mod B turret design.

  - **M48A2C**: M48A2 fitted with M13 fire control system
- **M48A3**: Featured AVDS-1790-2A diesel engine and M1E1 cupola. Withdrawn from combat use in 1973, replaced by M60A1.
- **M48A4**: M60 turrets on M48A1 hulls modernized to the M48A3 standard. Fitted with spacers on the turret ring to elevate the turret and the rearrangement of ammunition stowage to accommodate the 105 mm ammunition.
- **M48A5PI**: Early conversions of M48A1 hulls to the M48A5 standard. Retained the AVDS-1790-2A engine, CD-850-5A transmission and T97 track. All were further upgraded in 1976 with components from the M60A1 RISE Hull PIP Update Kit and redesignated M48A5.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015-9)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHunnicutt2015)\[ _[pageneeded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_\]
- **M48A5**: Featured M68 105mm gun, all metric-measurement M16 FCS, coax machine gun upgraded to M219 and a crew NBC protection system. Hull component upgrades included AVDS-1790-2C RISE engine, TLAC engine panels and T142 track. Some were fitted with the M19 cupola. All were relegated to the [Army National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_National_Guard "Army National Guard") for CONUS training use. Replaced by the M60A3.
- **QM48**: M48A3 designation for target vehicles. Withdrawn from use by 1994 and replaced in role by QM60.

### Specialized

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/M67_Flamethrower_Tank_Vietnam.jpg/500px-M67_Flamethrower_Tank_Vietnam.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M67_Flamethrower_Tank_Vietnam.jpg) A USMC M67 Flamethrower Tank in action near Da Nang, Vietnam.[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/M48_Patton_with_E202_Ensure.jpg/500px-M48_Patton_with_E202_Ensure.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:M48_Patton_with_E202_Ensure.jpg) The Ensure 202 expendable tank-mounted mine roller was tried in Vietnam on the M48 tank.

- **M48 AVLB**: [armored vehicle-launched bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armored_vehicle-launched_bridge "Armored vehicle-launched bridge") Former M48A3 with 60-foot (18 meters) scissors bridge mated to the M48A1/M48A2 hull. Most were upgraded with the M60 Hull PIP Update Kit.
- **M48 GAU-8:** a prototype [self propelled anti-aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-propelled_anti-aircraft_weapon "Self-propelled anti-aircraft weapon") platform created by [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric") that incorporated the [GAU-8 Avenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAU-8_Avenger "GAU-8 Avenger") cannon as its main gun.
- **[M48 Tagash AVLB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_AVLB#Israel "M60 AVLB")**: Israeli variant of the M48 AVLB. Former Jordanian M48A2s. Upgraded with Merkava-based track and suspension, upgraded engine and two _Tzmed_ tandem bridge sections.[&#8202;](#ref-back-105-0)[\[103\]](#refnote-105)
- **[M67 flame thrower tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M67_flame_thrower_tank "M67 flame thrower tank") "Zippo"**: An M48 tank armed with a flame thrower and a mock barrel and deflector. Named after a popular [cigarette lighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zippo "Zippo").
- **[M48 _Minenräumpanzer Keiler_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiler_(mine_flail) "Keiler (mine flail)")**: German [mine flail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mine_flail "Mine flail") variant based on the M48A2 hull with upgraded 986hp German MTU 871 Ka501 diesel engine and Renk HSWL 284 M transmission. As of 2007[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=M48_Patton&action=edit) they were still in service.[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto2-0)[\[104\]](#refnote-auto2)
- **M48 Marksman SPAAG** (Self-Propelled Anti-Aircraft Gun): British short range [Marksman anti-aircraft system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marksman_anti-aircraft_system "Marksman anti-aircraft system") designed by [GEC-Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEC-Marconi "GEC-Marconi"). Armed with two [Oerlikon 35mm cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oerlikon_GDF "Oerlikon GDF") mated to M48A2 hull. Not accepted for US service.
- **[M247 Sergeant York DIVAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M247_Sergeant_York "M247 Sergeant York")** (Division Air Defense): A short range air defense system designed by Ford Aerospace armed with 2 [Bofors 40mm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bofors_40_mm_Automatic_Gun_L/70 "Bofors 40 mm Automatic Gun L/70") cannons mounted on the M48A1 hull incorporating component upgrades from the M60A1 Hull PIP Kit. Canceled by 1985.
- **M48T5** Tamay: Turkish combat support vehicles based on the M48A2 hull designed by TLFC Kayseri and introduced at the IDEF Show at Ankara, Turkey in December 2005.[&#8202;](#ref-back-107-0)[\[105\]](#refnote-107)
  - **M48T5 ARV** ( [armored recovery vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armored_recovery_vehicle "Armored recovery vehicle")): The turret has been replaced with a welded armored superstructure that provides protection from shell fragments and small arms fire and a front-mounted stabilizer/dozer blade. It is fitted with a hydraulically operated winch with a maximum rated capacity of 70 tons and an auxiliary winch with a capacity of two tons. Both winches are located at the front of the vehicle.
  - **M48T5 CEV** (Combat Engineer Vehicle): Visually similar to the ARV and has the same two winches and a front-mounted stabilizer/dozer blade. The boom is mounted on the right side of the hull at the front but has an extendable jib that can be fitted with various attachments to meet specific engineer requirements, when used as a crane it has a lift capacity of seven tons. The boom can be traversed through 195 degrees.
