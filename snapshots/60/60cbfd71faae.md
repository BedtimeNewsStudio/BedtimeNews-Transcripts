# Ogaden War Producing Little but Refugees (Published 1979)

Special to The New York Times

IN THE OGADEN, Ethiopia — For nine months, from July 1977 until March 1978, the battle between Ethiopia and Somalia for this tortured region was the hottest war on earth, rupturing allegiances to superpowers that stretched back decades and resulting in tens of thousands of casualties on both SICIPC

Now, 20 months after Somali troops formally withdrew, a vicious little war persists in the desert region on the southeastern border of Ethiopia, with no visible gain save the flushing into Somalia of one of the largest single concentrations of refugees in the world.

On Nov. ‘I the official Somali count, in tabulation verified by international aid organizations, was 357,388 refugees from Ogaden in 21 camps within Somalia's borders. (There are more Indochinese refugees than there are in Somalia, but the Indochinese are not all in one country; there are far more Palestinian refugees, many on the West Bank and in Gaza.)

More than 90 percent of the refugees here are women and children. The men were killed, stayed behind to fight or tried to avoid crossfire while clinging to their meager possessions, usually goats, a few sheep and a lice‐ridden hut.

Deaths Are Put at 60,000

Since the Somali pullout, it is said, there have been 60,000 deaths in Ogaden, including 25,000 civilians and 6,000 Cuban soldiers supporting the Ethiopians. [Military analysts in Washington voiced doubt that the Cuban toll could have been that large; it is estimated that the Cuban force had 20,000 men at its high point.]

The fighting is rooted in the Somali belief that Ogaden, with eight million pea ple, two million of them ethnic Somalis, is in fact Western Somalia. A horn‐shaped arid plateau, it juts eastward into the Horn of Africa and was attached to Ethiopia under colonial policies. More than anything it is a zone of primitive warfare, where tribal doctors set bones cracked by bullets, and wounded guerrillas, trying to reach proper care, bleed to death on the backs of camels.

Of perhaps half a dozen guerrilla groups in the area, the Western Somali Liberation Front is the most prominent. With captured antitank rockets, captured Soviet‐designed automatic rifles and captured Land‐Rovers, the ragtag organization largely controls the countryside, restricting the Ethiopians to garrisons in the bigger towns. No numbers on fighters are available on either side.

The most serious fighting erupted in July 1977, when ethnic Somali guerrillas, with heavy support from Somalia, invaded Ogaden. The attackers swiftly conquered a large part of the territory and several key cities, but then troops of the Marxist Ethiopian Government, with help from Cuba and the Soviet Union, managed to drive the Somali‐supported forces out. The Soviet Union, the United States, long allied with Ethiopia, briefly considered sending them military aid. But as the conflict intensified, Washington decided not to help either side.

A Big Toll in Wells The war is now fought on the simplest lines. When the Ethiopians venture out, the Somali guerrillas ambush them. In retaliation, Ethiopian fighter planes, including MIG's with Cubans and East Germans at the controls, bomb and strafe villages. It is estimated that the planes have destroyed 200 wells in six months, forcing whole villages to move a few miles and resettle beneath thorn trees.

The chief occupation in daylight appears to be listening for mechanical noises, which in the stillness can be heard for great distances, but at night it is crossing the border, with a thousand or more refugees going into Somalia.

A sensitive side issue is just how much , the Somali Government in Mogadishu supports the liberation front. Having appealed for $70 million in world aid to feed the refugees, it could hardly afford to be seen as the aggressor. It is on record as morally supporting the guerrillas, but denies giving them material assistance.

There is no evidence of material support at the moment; rather, there is an air of finely tuned cooperation. A visitor to Mogadishu can ask the Government to be put in touch with the liberation front. Then, if need be, the guerrillas will bump a ticketed passenger from the nationalized Air Somalia flight to Hargeisa, capital of the Northwest District, to guarantee a visitor a seat. In Hargeisa the front puts visitors up in State House, the Government quarters, preparatory to arranging a trip into Ogaden.

Afraid of air strikes, the guerrillas travel at night. Two rundown LandRovers moved south from Hargeisa in fits and starts that suggested nothing so much as the overall conduct of the war. Lightning flashed just before dusk and rain tamped down the dust and turned the two‐rut track into a swift, muddy river. One mired vehicle was shoved free only to overheat. Water from an oil tin corrected that problem just before the accelerator linkage snapped on the second. A twisted piece of wire fixed it, and the two vehicles were off on dry land, slicing through dust a foot deep.

Later, while a red wafer moon rose as pulled on a chain, the passengers slept on the ground In a camouflaged village. In the morning, a MIG passed but did not attack.

Down an old road, built by the Italians when they were colonial rulers and worn through to its jagged bedrock, the guerrillas drove within 12 miles of the Ethiopian‐occupied city of Aware. Here, said the local commander of the liberation front, Ahmed Nur Gorod, the guerrillas ambushed a convoy recently and killed 284 Ethiopians. In two rag‐and‐stick huts not far from the road, seven Somali casualties still convalesced.

For three days the party from Hargeisa zigzagged between Ogaden villages, picking up information on skirmishes and determining where trucks should be sent at night to collect refugees. Then it drove back over pale dirt the same shade as the camels roaming it.

No end is seen to the battle for possession of this region. A Western analyst in

Mogadishu said he would not be surprised if it lasted 10 years or more. The Ethiopians, drained by the age‐old Eritrean secessionist struggle in the north, where Ethiopia's foes are assisted by the Sudan, appear to have only enough resources to hang onto the major Ogaden towns and nothing more. The Somali guerrillas, in turn, lack the tools for more than hit‐andrun ambushes. With no diplomatic or military solution at hand, the people of the desert remain trapped between hostile neighbors.
