The economic prosperity of Eurasia is highly dependent on trade. Yet, today, the global trade system is under strain from supply chain disruptions, heightened protectionism, and climate impacts. To safeguard trade and promote gains in prosperity, strategic investments in the Trans-Caspian Transport Corridor (TCTC), also known as the Middle Corridor, could deliver enormous economic benefits in the nine countries along the route: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, Kazakhstan, the Kyrgyz Republic, Tajikistan, Türkiye, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan.

For the nearly 200 million people living in these countries, a well-functioning Middle Corridor would mean more jobs, better access to markets, and greater opportunity. For the world, an additional transport corridor capable of becoming more reliable and efficient would give countries and shippers more routing choices with lower logistics costs, especially for trade between Europe and Asia.

## KEY MESSAGES

* Targeted investments to upgrade and better integrate the Middle Corridor could more than triple trade volumes along the route, halve travel times, boost GDP by 3.3%, and create 2 million more jobs by 2040. If the Middle Corridor countries pair infrastructure investments with reforms that improve trade and transport efficiency, corridor volumes could quadruple and travel times could fall by two-thirds by 2040, compared to 2023.

* Middle Corridor countries will need to invest at least $25 billion through 2040 to relieve physical infrastructure bottlenecks, which are primarily concentrated in rail segments, port capacity, and maritime links. In addition, an estimated $30 billion will be needed for enabling investments, such as connecting roads and rail links that tie the corridor into local economies, logistics hubs and inland terminals. A significant portion of these investments could be mobilized from the private sector.

* Cross-border collaboration will be key to ensuring smooth functioning of the corridor. Sequenced actions should prioritize adopting a single, digital corridor system to replace fragmented paperwork with one digital entry point for transport, transit, and trade data; establishing an integrated, market oriented rail freight and shipping operator for containerized freight as a joint venture between the rail and shipping operators of host countries, which would seamlessly link rail and maritime services; strengthening corridor-wide coordination to monitor performance, resolve bottlenecks, and support joint decisions; and modernizing corridor operators by improving governance, financial sustainability, and regulation.

Although recent investments and geopolitical shifts have led to a marked increase in TCTC traffic and policy interest in the corridor, its current operations face structural constraints across railway lines, maritime ports, border crossings, and shipping links that require sufficiently granular pinpointing and coordinated resolution. Fragmented governance and uneven policy implementation amplify physical bottlenecks, limiting corridor-wide optimization.

  TCTC’s ability to generate economic value will be shaped by how much freight it can attract and how reliably it can serve different market segments. Strategic investments in the TCTC could more than triple trade volumes along the route. Furthermoe, building regional transport infrastructure and lowering transport costs can deliver substantial benefits, including boosting GDP by 3.3% and creating 2 million new jobs across its host countries. While differentiated, the gains are positive for all countries. Larger economies, such as Türkiye and Kazakhstan, are expected to see bigger gains in absolute terms, while smaller or landlocked economies, such as the Kyrgyz Republic and Armenia, may experience larger proportional gains.

  Maximizing the corridor’s cargo capture and economic potential depends, in part, on improving its ability to serve shippers at lower levels of logistics costs both in absolute terms and relative to alternative routes. This will require infrastructure investments of at least $25 billion through 2040 to relieve the physical bottlenecks, combined with measures to improve trade facilitation, service delivery, and cross-border collaboration. Notably, of the 16 most critical infrastructure investments needed, 75% are ongoing while the rest are firmly embedded in government plans and expected to proceed in the near term. This leaves the attainment of operational, non-infrastructure improvements as the most likely primary challenge facing the TCTC going forward.

  To transition from transport route, i.e. one that moves freight, to an economic corridor that creates jobs, attracts investment, and contributes to economic growth and diversification, the TCTC will need investments in enabling activities, such as complementary linear infrastructure (e.g., feeder roads and last-mile rail links), nodal infrastructure (e.g., inland logistics clusters and terminals), and investments in logistics equipment (e.g., rolling stock and other conveyances; improved asset management systems) that tie the corridor into local economies. These economic activity-enabling investments would require about $30.5 billion across all countries over the next 15 years. A country-by-country mapping of the key enabling investments needed to support this transition details the operational rationale, estimated size, and potential for private sector participation.

  Across the TCTC, state-owned enterprises (SOEs) own and operate most rail, port, and shipping assets. Their performance determines whether the corridor delivers. However, many SOEs face persistent operational, financial, and governance constraints. Strengthening and commercializing these enterprises is the foundation for unlocking the corridor’s full strategic and economic potential. Strengthening SOEs is not a substitute for private sector participation — it is a prerequisite for it.
