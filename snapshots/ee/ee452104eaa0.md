## Madagascar Has 80% 4G Coverage, Yet Mobile Internet Remains Out of Reach for Most

* Madagascar had 80% 4G coverage in 2024, yet Internet penetration stood at only 20.4% in 2025.
* Infrastructure access, telecom pricing and taxes continue to limit competition and digital adoption.
* A pending telecom reform aims to strengthen competition in a market where operators remain highly unequal.

Madagascar’s Bill No. 040/2026 on electronic communications and digital infrastructure still awaits consideration by the National Assembly. Formally submitted by the government on August 28, 2026, the legislation aims to modernize the sector’s legal framework, regulate digital infrastructure and adapt the rules to changing uses. Its examination was postponed to a future parliamentary session after the country’s second extraordinary session of the year closed on September 4.

Behind the legal reform lies a major economic issue, particularly for the mobile telecommunications sector. The Ministry of Digital Development, Digital Transformation, Posts and Telecommunications (MNDPT) says the legislation “puts an end to all forms of monopoly as well as exclusive or reserved rights in the sector: all telecommunications networks and services are fully open to competition, without discrimination or privilege for any operator, whether incumbent, public or private.”

That ambition is rooted in a much older regulatory history. Law No. 2005-023 of October 17, 2005, had already established principles governing competition and interconnection. It provided for regulatory intervention to preserve free and fair competition, prevent abuses of dominant positions, regulate certain pricing conditions and, when necessary, require changes to interconnection agreements.

More than 20 years later, the central question remains whether those principles can be effectively applied in a market where operators have vastly unequal resources.

**Legal Market Opening Masks Persistent Imbalances**

On paper, Madagascar’s telecom market is no longer a monopoly. Several mobile operators—Yas, Orange and Airtel—compete for consumers, while new players have entered certain segments.

In practice, however, a significant gap remains between this legal opening and the conditions required for fair competition, particularly when it comes to access to key infrastructure and tariff regulation.

Yas Madagascar, a subsidiary of the Axian group, already claimed 68% of the subscriber market as of March 31, 2025. Its position can partly be traced to advantages inherited from the 2004 acquisition of incumbent operator Telma: nationwide network coverage and control of key infrastructure, particularly the national fiber-optic backbone, which is essential for high-speed services. Until April 2023, the operator also held a legal monopoly on fiber-optic deployment.

Since the monopoly on fiber deployment and operation was lifted, Orange and Airtel have started building their own infrastructure. But this catch-up phase requires both time and substantial investment. In the meantime, they remain dependent on Yas, which sets the tariffs for access to the national backbone.

The law leaves wholesale capacity prices to commercial negotiations between the parties and limits regulatory intervention to setting a maximum price that operators cannot exceed. Some market participants consider this arrangement unfavorable to genuine competition. They say access to backbone capacity can generate bills equivalent to 15% to 20% of their annual revenue.

Yas currently owns and operates more than 5,000 kilometers of fiber-optic network. Orange, meanwhile, reports 1,749 kilometers of deployed fiber, including a 558-kilometer fiber link between Antananarivo and Majunga that entered service in December 2025.

Orange then completed the deployment of another 33 kilometers of fiber in Antananarivo in January 2026. The operator plans to reach 3,000 kilometers of fiber across the country by 2033.

Airtel Madagascar has also invested in fiber-optic routes between Antananarivo and Majunga and between Antananarivo and Toamasina since 2023. In 2024, its “Tana Metro Ring” project, a looped fiber-optic network covering part of Antananarivo, was 92% complete.

**Pricing Differences Put Competitors at a Disadvantage**

Madagascar’s regulatory policy, which favors negotiations between operators, has also raised concerns over persistent price differences between on-net calls, made within the same network, and off-net calls to competing networks.

Madagascar consequently has a degree of market segmentation that restricts competition: calls from the Yas network to other operators cost more than those charged by competitors.

Regulation provides no measure to correct this situation and instead allows operators to negotiate call termination rates freely. The Communications Technology Regulatory Authority (ARTEC) does not require any alignment between on-net and off-net tariffs.

In practice, consumer choices are therefore influenced more by the size of individual networks—Yas claims the country’s largest telecom network—and the resulting club effects than by prices or innovation.

**Madagascar Lags on Digital Inclusion**

These market distortions, which constrain the development of attractive prices and greater consumption, are compounded by taxes that weigh on both entry-level devices and service offers.

In November 2025, Madagascar’s telecom operators association, the Groupement des opérateurs de télécommunications de Madagascar (GTM), called on the government to eliminate *“certain additional taxes recently applied to the sector or ineffective in their implementation.”*

The association specifically pointed to the 8% excise duty on telecommunications, the 5% tax on mobile finance and taxes on imports of affordable smartphones.

The situation has a direct impact on access to digital services in Madagascar, even though ARTEC reported that 4G and 3G networks already covered 80% and 98% of the country, respectively, in 2024.

Despite that extensive network coverage, mobile penetration stood at 66.2% at the end of 2025, according to DataReportal 2026. That represented 21.8 million active mobile subscriptions.

Internet penetration was much lower, at 20.4%, equivalent to 6.71 million Internet users, while smartphone ownership stood at 24%.

Madagascar’s Mobile Connectivity Index—which measures countries’ performance against the main factors that determine mobile Internet adoption—stood at 38.6, among the lowest levels in the Southern African Development Community (SADC). Against this backdrop, the outcome of Madagascar’s regulatory reform will help determine the country’s ability to turn telecommunications and digital services into drivers of national economic growth.
