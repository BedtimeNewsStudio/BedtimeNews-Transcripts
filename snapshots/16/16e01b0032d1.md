Technique for anonymous communication over a computer network

"Onion network" redirects here. For the parody news program, see [Onion News Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion_News_Network "Onion News Network").

**Onion routing** is a technique for [anonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity "Anonymity") communication over a [computer network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"). In an **onion network**, messages are encapsulated in layers of [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption "Encryption"), analogous to the layers of an [onion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion "Onion"). The [encrypted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption "Encryption") data is transmitted through a series of [network nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_nodes "Network nodes") called " **onion routers**," each of which "peels" away a single layer, revealing the data's next destination. When the final layer is decrypted, the message arrives at its destination. Each intermediary generally knows only the immediately preceding and following nodes, limiting what any single relay can learn about the route.[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1) While onion routing provides a high level of security and anonymity, there are methods to break the anonymity of this technique, such as timing analysis.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-:0)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]

Onion routing was developed in the mid-1990s at the [U.S. Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Naval_Research_Laboratory "U.S. Naval Research Laboratory") by employees [Paul Syverson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Syverson "Paul Syverson"), Michael G. Reed, and David Goldschlag[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3)[&#8202;](#ref-back-patent-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-patent) to protect U.S. [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Intelligence_Community "United States Intelligence Community") communications online.[&#8202;](#ref-back-pando-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-pando) Further development of onion routing was supported in part by the [Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Advanced_Research_Projects_Agency "Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency") (DARPA). The Navy filed a provisional patent application for onion routing in 1997, followed by the non-provisional application in 1998. The patent was granted in 2001.[&#8202;](#ref-back-patent-1)[\[4\]](#refnote-patent)[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-6)[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-7)

This method was publicly released by the same employees through publishing an article in the [IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Journal_on_Selected_Areas_in_Communications "IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications") the same year. It depicted the use of the method to protect the user from the network and outside observers who eavesdrop and conduct [traffic analysis attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_analysis_attack "Traffic analysis attack"). The most important part of this research is the configurations and applications of onion routing on the existing e-services, such as [virtual private networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_networks "Virtual private networks"), [web-browsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web-browsing "Web-browsing"), [email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email "Email"), [remote login](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_login "Remote login"), and [electronic cash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_cash "Electronic cash").[&#8202;](#ref-back-8-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-8)

Based on the existing onion routing technology, computer scientists [Roger Dingledine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Dingledine "Roger Dingledine") and [Nick Mathewson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Mathewson "Nick Mathewson") joined [Paul Syverson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Syverson "Paul Syverson") in 2002 to develop what has become the largest and best-known implementation of onion routing, then called The Onion Routing project ( [Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_(network) "Tor (network)") project).

After the Naval Research Laboratory released the code for Tor under a [free license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_license "Free license"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-pando-1)[\[5\]](#refnote-pando)[&#8202;](#ref-back-prealpha-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-prealpha)[&#8202;](#ref-back-torproject-faq-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-torproject-faq) Dingledine, Mathewson and five others founded [The Tor Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tor_Project "The Tor Project") as a [non-profit organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/501(c)(3) "501(c)(3)") in 2006, with the [financial support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_sponsorship "Fiscal sponsorship") of the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation "Electronic Frontier Foundation") and several other organizations.[&#8202;](#ref-back-torproject-sponsors-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-torproject-sponsors)[&#8202;](#ref-back-wp-attacks-prompt-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-wp-attacks-prompt)

## Data

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Data")\]

[![A client, represented as a phone, sending traffic to an onion labelled "Guard" with four nested lines, then going to a "Middle" onion with three nested lines, then going to an "Exit" onion with two nested lines, and finally going to the Server with one line.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b1/Tor_Circuit_Diagram.svg/250px-Tor_Circuit_Diagram.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tor_Circuit_Diagram.svg) A diagram of an onion routed connection, using [Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_(network) "Tor (network)")'s terminology of guard, middle, and exit relays

Metaphorically, an onion is the data structure formed by "wrapping" a message with successive layers of encryption to be decrypted ("peeled" or "unwrapped") by as many intermediary computers as there are layers before arriving at its destination. The original message remains hidden as it is transferred from one node to the next, and no intermediary knows both the origin and final destination of the data, allowing the sender to remain anonymous.[&#8202;](#ref-back-tor-design-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-tor-design)

### Onion creation and transmission

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Onion creation and transmission")\]

To create and transmit an onion, the originator selects a set of nodes from a list provided by a "directory node". The chosen nodes are arranged into a path, called a "chain" or "circuit", through which the message will be transmitted. To preserve the anonymity of the sender, no node in the circuit is able to tell whether the node before it is the originator or another intermediary like itself. Likewise, no node in the circuit is able to tell how many other nodes are in the circuit. The exit node can identify that it is the final relay before the destination.[&#8202;](#ref-back-tor-design-1)[\[13\]](#refnote-tor-design)

[![A picture of an onion node in Canada.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/The_Tor_Node_at_the_FIMS_Graduate_Library.jpg/250px-The_Tor_Node_at_the_FIMS_Graduate_Library.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Tor_Node_at_the_FIMS_Graduate_Library.jpg) An onion node in use.

Using asymmetric key cryptography, the originator obtains a [public key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key "Public key") from the directory node to send an encrypted message to the first ("entry") node, establishing a connection and a [shared secret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_secret "Shared secret") ("session key"). Using the established encrypted link to the entry node, the originator can then relay a message through the first node to a second node in the chain using encryption that only the second node, and not the first, can decrypt. When the second node receives the message, it establishes a connection with the first node. While this extends the encrypted link from the originator, the second node cannot determine whether the first node is the originator or just another node in the circuit. The originator can then send a message through the first and second nodes to a third node, encrypted such that only the third node is able to decrypt it. The third, as with the second, becomes linked to the originator but connects only with the second. This process can be repeated to build larger and larger chains but is typically limited to preserve performance.[&#8202;](#ref-back-tor-design-2)[\[13\]](#refnote-tor-design)

When the chain is complete, the originator can send data over the Internet anonymously. When the final recipient of the data sends data back, the intermediary nodes maintain the same link back to the originator, with data again layered, but in reverse such that the final node this time adds the first layer of encryption and the first node adds the last layer of encryption before sending the data, for example a web page, to the originator, who is able to decrypt all layers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-tor-design-3)[\[13\]](#refnote-tor-design)

## Weaknesses

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Weaknesses")\]

See also: [Tor (network) §Attacks and limitations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_(network)#Attacks_and_limitations "Tor (network)")

### Timing analysis

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Timing analysis")\]

See also: [Traffic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_analysis "Traffic analysis")

One of the reasons why the typical Internet connections are not considered anonymous is the ability of [Internet service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_service_provider "Internet service provider") to trace and log connections between computers. For example, when a person accesses a particular website, the data itself may be secured through a connection like [HTTPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTPS "HTTPS") such that the user's password, emails, or other content is not visible to an outside party, but there is a record of the connection itself, what time it occurred, and the amount of data transferred. Onion routing creates and obscures a path between two computers such that there is no discernible connection directly from a person to a website, but there still exist records of connections between computers. Traffic analysis searches those records of connections made by a potential originator and tries to match the timing and data transfers to connections made to a potential recipient. If an attacker has compromised both ends of a route, a sender may be seen to have transferred an amount of data to an unknown computer a specified amount of seconds before a different unknown computer transferred data of similar timing and traffic patterns to a particular destination.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Dingledine-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-Dingledine) Factors that may facilitate traffic analysis include nodes failing or leaving the network[&#8202;](#ref-back-Dingledine-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-Dingledine) and a compromised node keeping track of a session as it occurs when chains are periodically rebuilt.[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-16)

[Garlic routing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic_routing "Garlic routing") is a variant of onion routing associated with the [I2P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I2P "I2P") network that encrypts multiple messages together, which both increases the speed of data transfer and makes it more difficult[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17) for attackers to perform traffic analysis.[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18)

### Exit node vulnerability

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Onion_routing&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Exit node vulnerability")\]

Although the message being sent is transmitted inside several layers of encryption, the job of the exit node, as the final node in the chain, is to decrypt the final layer and deliver the message to the recipient. A compromised exit node is thus able to acquire the raw data being transmitted, potentially including passwords, private messages, bank account numbers, and other forms of personal information. Dan Egerstad, a Swedish researcher, used such an attack to collect the passwords of over 100 email accounts related to foreign embassies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19)

Exit node vulnerabilities are similar to those on unsecured wireless networks, where the data being transmitted by a user on the network may be intercepted by another user or by the router operator. Both issues can be mitigated by using authenticated end-to-end encryption such as [HTTPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTPS "HTTPS"). If there is [end-to-end encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-to-end_encryption "End-to-end encryption") between the sender and the recipient, and the sender isn't lured into trusting a false SSL certificate offered by the exit node, then not even the last intermediary can view the original message.
