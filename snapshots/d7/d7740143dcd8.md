# Marc Bloch in World War II

Marc Bloch in World War II[![Black and white photograph of Marc Bloch](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3d/Marc_Bloch_%281886%E2%80%931944%29.jpg/500px-Marc_Bloch_%281886%E2%80%931944%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Marc_Bloch_(1886%E2%80%931944).jpg) Marc Bloch

**Marc Léopold Benjamin Bloch** ([/blɒk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); French:[\[maʁkleɔpɔldbɛ̃ʒamɛ̃blɔk\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 6 July 1886 – 16 June 1944) was a [French historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_contemporary_French_historians "List of contemporary French historians"). He was a founding member of the [Annales School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annales_School "Annales School") of French social history. [Marc Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Bloch "Marc Bloch") specialised in [medieval history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_history "Medieval history") and published widely on [Medieval France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_France "Medieval France"). He worked at the [Universities of Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg") (1920 to 1936), [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") (1936 to 1939), and the [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Montpellier "University of Montpellier") (1941 to 1944).

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Bloch volunteered for service, and was a logistician during the [Phoney War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoney_War "Phoney War"). Involved in the [Battle of Dunkirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dunkirk "Battle of Dunkirk") and spending a brief time in Britain, he unsuccessfully attempted to secure passage to the United States. Returning to France, he found his ability to work curtailed by new antisemitic regulations. However, he applied for and received one of the few permits available, allowing Jews to continue working in the French university system. He had to leave Paris, and complained that the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") authorities had looted his apartment and stole his books; he was also forced to relinquish his position on the editorial board of _Annales_. Bloch worked in [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montpellier "Montpellier") until November 1942 when [Germany invaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Anton "Case Anton") [Vichy France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_France "Vichy France"). Bloch then joined the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance"), acting predominantly as a courier and translator. In 1944, he was captured in [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyon "Lyon") and [executed by firing squad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executed_by_firing_squad "Executed by firing squad"). Several of his books were published posthumously.

## Background

Born in [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyon "Lyon") to an [Alsatian Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Alsace "History of the Jews in Alsace") family, Bloch was raised in Paris, where his father—the [classical historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_historian "Classical historian") [Gustave Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Bloch "Gustave Bloch")—worked at [Sorbonne University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbonne_University "Sorbonne University"). Bloch was educated at various Parisian [lycées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e "Lycée") and the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_Normale_Sup%C3%A9rieure "École Normale Supérieure"), and from an early age was affected by the [antisemitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism "Antisemitism") of the [Dreyfus affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_affair "Dreyfus affair"). During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), he served in the French Army and fought at the [First Battle of the Marne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_the_Marne "First Battle of the Marne") and the [Somme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Somme "Battle of the Somme"). After the war, he was awarded his doctorate in 1918 and became a lecturer at the University of Strasbourg. There, he formed an intellectual partnership with [modern historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_historian "Modern historian") [Lucien Febvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Febvre "Lucien Febvre"). Together they founded the Annales School and began publishing the [journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_journal "Academic journal") _[Annales d'histoire économique et sociale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annales._Histoire,_Sciences_Sociales "Annales. Histoire, Sciences Sociales")_ in 1929.

## Outbreak of war

On 24 August 1939, at the age of 53,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199840-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199840) Bloch was mobilised for a third time,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199840-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199840) now as a fuel supply officer.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268) He was responsible for the mobilisation of the French Army's massive [motorised units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_French_divisions_in_World_War_II#Motorized_and_infantry_divisions "List of French divisions in World War II").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199845-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199845) This involved him undertaking such a detailed assessment of the French fuel supply that he later wrote he was able to "count petrol tins and ration every drop" of fuel he obtained.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199845-1)[\[3\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199845) During the first few months of the war, called the [Phoney War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoney_War "Phoney War"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007533-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007533)[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[note 1\]](#refnote-5) he was stationed in Alsace.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985188-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985188) He possessed none of the eager patriotism with which he had approached the First World War. Instead, Carole Fink suggests that because Bloch felt himself to have been discriminated against, he had "begun to distance himself intellectually and emotionally from his comrades and leaders".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199841-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199841) Back in Strasbourg, his main duty was evacuating civilians behind the [Maginot Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maginot_Line "Maginot Line").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199843-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199843) Further transfers occurred, and Bloch was re-stationed to [Molsheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molsheim "Molsheim"), [Saverne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saverne "Saverne"), and eventually to the [1st Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Army_(France) "1st Army (France)") headquarters in [Picardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picardy "Picardy"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199843-1)[\[7\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199843) where he joined the Intelligence Department, in liaison with the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Expeditionary_Force_(World_War_I) "British Expeditionary Force (World War I)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199844-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199844)[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[note 2\]](#refnote-10)

Bloch was largely bored between 1939 and May 1940 as he often had little work to do.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999164-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999164) To pass the time and occupy himself, he decided to begin writing a history of France. To this end, he purchased notebooks and began to work out a structure for the work.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199848-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199848) Although never completed, the pages he managed to write, "in his cold, poorly lit rooms",[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199849-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199849) eventually became the kernel of _The Historian's Craft_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199849-1)[\[11\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199849) At one point he expected to be invited to neutral [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium "Belgium") to deliver a series of lectures in [Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li%C3%A8ge "Liège"). These never took place, however, disappointing Bloch very much; he had planned to speak on Belgian [neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_country "Neutral country").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199849-2)[\[11\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199849) He also turned down the opportunity to travel to Oslo as an [attaché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attach%C3%A9_duty "Attaché duty") to the French Military Mission there. He was considered an excellent candidate for the position due to his fluency in Norwegian and knowledge of the country. Bloch considered it and came close to accepting; ultimately, though, it was too far from his family,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199850-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199850) whom he rarely saw enough of in any case.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199850-1)[\[12\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199850)[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[note 3\]](#refnote-15) Some academics had escaped France for [The New School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_School "The New School") in New York City, and the School also invited Bloch. He refused,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) possibly because of difficulties in obtaining [visas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_visa "Travel visa"):[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) the US government would not grant visas to every member of his family.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200948-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200948)

### Fall of France

Main article: [Fall of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_France "Fall of France")

[![street sign in Strasbourg](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Strasbourg-Plaque_Marc_Bloch.jpg/500px-Strasbourg-Plaque_Marc_Bloch.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Strasbourg-Plaque_Marc_Bloch.jpg) Plaque commemorating Bloch in the [Marc Bloch University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Bloch_University "Marc Bloch University"), Strasbourg, now part of the refounded [University of Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg")

In May 1940, the German army outflanked the French and forced them to withdraw. Facing capture in [Rennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennes "Rennes"), Bloch disguised himself in civilian clothes and lived under German occupation for a fortnight before returning to his family at their country home in [Fougères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foug%C3%A8res "Fougères").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995207-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995207)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199842-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199842)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBloch194923-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBloch194923) He fought at the [Battle of Dunkirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dunkirk "Battle of Dunkirk") in May–June 1940 and was [evacuated to England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkirk_evacuation "Dunkirk evacuation") with the [British Expeditionary Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Expeditionary_Force_(World_War_II) "British Expeditionary Force (World War II)") on the requisitioned [steamer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamship "Steamship") [MV _Royal Daffodil_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Royal_Daffodil_(1939) "MV Royal Daffodil (1939)"), which he later described as taking place "under golden skies coloured by the black and fawn smoke".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991256-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991256) Before the evacuation, Bloch ordered the immediate burning of fuel supplies.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995207-1)[\[16\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995207) Although he could have remained in Britain,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEKaye200197-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEKaye200197) he chose to return to France the day he arrived[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995207-2)[\[16\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995207) because his family was still there.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEKaye200197-1)[\[20\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEKaye200197)

Bloch felt that the French Army lacked the _[esprit de corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esprit_de_corps "Esprit de corps")_ or "fervent fraternity"[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985184-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985184) of the French Army in the First World War.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985184-1)[\[21\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985184) He saw the French generals of 1940 as behaving as unimaginatively as [Joseph Joffre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Joffre "Joseph Joffre") had in the first war.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985187-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985187) He did not, however, believe that the earlier war was an indication of how the next would progress: "no two successive wars", he wrote in 1940, "are ever the same war".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985189-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985189)

To Bloch, France collapsed because her generals failed to capitalise on the best qualities humanity possessed—character and intelligence[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967281-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967281)—because of their own "sluggish and intractable" progress since the First World War.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007533-1)[\[4\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007533) He was horrified by the defeat which, Carole Fink has suggested, he saw as being worse, for both France and the world, than her previous defeats at [Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo "Battle of Waterloo") and [Sedan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sedan "Battle of Sedan").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199839-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199839) Bloch understood the reasons for France's sudden defeat: not in the rumours of British betrayal, communist [fifth columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_column "Fifth column") or fascist plots, but in her failure to [motorise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorized_infantry "Motorized infantry"), and perhaps more importantly, her failure to understand what motorisation meant. He understood that it was the latter that allowed the French army to become bogged down in Belgium, and this had been compounded by the French army's slow retreat. He wrote in _Strange Defeat_ that a fast, motorised retreat might have saved the army.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEChirot198442-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEChirot198442)

Two-thirds of France was occupied by Germany.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHughes-Warrington201515-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHughes-Warrington201515) Bloch, one of the few elderly academics to volunteer,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199842-1)[\[17\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199842) was demobilised soon after [Philippe Pétain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_P%C3%A9tain "Philippe Pétain")'s government signed the [Armistice of 22 June 1940](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_22_June_1940 "Armistice of 22 June 1940") forming [Vichy France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_France "Vichy France") in the remaining southern-third of the country.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink199839-1)[\[25\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink199839) Bloch moved south, where in January 1941, he applied for and received[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBirnbaum2007251_n.92-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBirnbaum2007251_n.92) one of only ten exemptions to the ban on employing Jewish academics the Vichy government made.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276) This was probably due to Bloch's pre-eminence in the field of history.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-1)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) He was allowed to work[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276-1)[\[29\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276) at the "University of Strasbourg-in-exile",[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-2)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) the universities of [Clermont-Ferrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Clermont-Ferrand "University of Clermont-Ferrand"), and Montpellier.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-1)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268) The latter, further south, was beneficial to his wife's health, which was in decline.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHughes2002127-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHughes2002127) The [dean of faculty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_(education) "Dean (education)") at Montpellier was [Augustin Fliche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Fliche "Augustin Fliche"), an [ecclesiastical historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_historian "Ecclesiastical historian") of the Middle Ages, who, according to Weber, "made no secret of his antisemitism".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991253–254-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991253–254) He disliked Bloch further for having once given him a poor [review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review "Peer review").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991253–254-1)[\[31\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991253–254) Fliche not only opposed Bloch's transfer to Montpellier but made his life uncomfortable when he was there.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991254-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991254) The Vichy government was attempting to promote itself as a return to traditional French values.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELevine201015-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELevine201015) Bloch condemned this as propaganda; the rural idyll that Vichy said it would return France to was impossible, he said, "because the idyllic, docile peasant life of the French right had never existed".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEChirot198443-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEChirot198443)

## Declining relationship with Febvre

> It was during these bitter years of defeat, of personal recrimination, of insecurity that he wrote both the uncompromisingly condemnatory pages of _Strange Defeat_ and the beautifully serene passages of _The Historian's Craft_.

— [R. R. Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rees_Davies "Rees Davies")[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-2)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268)

The war also impacted Bloch's professional relationship with Febvre. The Nazis wanted French [editorial boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editorial_board "Editorial board") to be stripped of Jews in accordance with German [racial policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_policy_of_Nazi_Germany "Racial policy of Nazi Germany"); Bloch advocated disobedience, while Febvre was passionate about the survival of _Annales_ at any cost.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199443-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199443) He believed that it was worth making concessions to keep the journal afloat and to keep France's intellectual life alive.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200943-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200943) Bloch rejected out of hand any suggestion that he should, in his words, "fall into line".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200944-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200944) Febvre also asked Bloch to resign as joint-editor of the journal. Febvre feared that Bloch's involvement, as a Jew in Nazi-occupied France, would hinder the journal's distribution.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007530-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007530) Bloch, forced to accede, turned the _Annales_ over to the sole editorship of Febvre, who then changed the journal's name to [_Mélanges d'Histoire Sociale_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9langes_d'histoire_sociale "Mélanges d'histoire sociale"). Bloch was forced to write for it under the pseudonym Marc Fougères.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199443-1)[\[35\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199443) The journal's bank account was also in Bloch's name; this too had to go.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200943-1)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200943) Henri Hauser supported Febvre's position, and Bloch was offended when Febvre intimated that Hauser had more to lose than both of them. This was because, whereas Bloch had been allowed to retain his research position, Hauser had not. Bloch interpreted Febvre's comment as implying that Bloch was not a victim. Bloch, alluding to his ethnicity, replied that the difference between them was that, whereas he feared for his children because of their Jewishness, Febvre's children were in no more danger than any other man in the country.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200944-1)[\[37\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200944)

The Annalist historian [André Burguière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Burgui%C3%A8re "André Burguière") suggests Febvre did not really understand the position Bloch, or any French Jew, was in.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200945-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200945) Already damaged by this disagreement, Bloch's and Febvre's relationship declined further when the former had been forced to leave his library and papers[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-3)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) in his Paris apartment following his move to Vichy. He had attempted to have them transported to his [Creuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creuse "Creuse") residence,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200945-1)[\[39\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200945) but the Nazis—-who had made their headquarters in the hotel next to Bloch's apartment[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249)—-looted his rooms[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249-1)[\[40\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249) and confiscated his library in 1942.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276-2)[\[29\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276) Bloch held Febvre responsible for the loss, believing he could have done more to prevent it.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276-3)[\[29\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993276)

Bloch's mother had recently died, and his wife was ill; furthermore, although he was permitted to work and live, he faced daily harassment.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-4)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) On 18 March 1941, Bloch made his [will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_and_testament "Will and testament") in Clermont-Ferrand.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163) The Polish social historian [Bronisław Geremek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronis%C5%82aw_Geremek "Bronisław Geremek") suggests that this document hints at Bloch in some way foreseeing his death,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861103-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861103) as he emphasised that nobody had the right to avoid fighting for their country.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861105-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861105) In March 1942 Bloch and other French academics such as [Georges Friedmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Friedmann "Georges Friedmann") and [Émile Benveniste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Benveniste "Émile Benveniste"), refused to join or condone the establishment of the [Union Générale des Israelites des France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judenrat "Judenrat") by the Vichy government, a group intended to include all Jews in France, both of birth and immigration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBirnbaum2007248-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBirnbaum2007248)

## French resistance, capture and death

[![Colour photograph of the outside of Montluc Prison](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Cour_de_la_prison_Montluc.jpg/500px-Cour_de_la_prison_Montluc.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cour_de_la_prison_Montluc.jpg) Exterior of Montluc Prison, where Bloch and his comrades were held before their deaths; the mural is modern.

In November 1942, as part of an operation known as [Case Anton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Anton "Case Anton"), the German Army crossed the [demarcation line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demarcation_line_(France) "Demarcation line (France)") and occupied the territory previously under direct Vichy rule.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-5)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) This was the catalyst for Bloch's decision to join the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance")[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-3)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268) sometime between late 1942[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531) and March 1943.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-4)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268) Bloch was careful not to join simply because of his ethnicity or the laws that were passed against it. As Burguière has pointed out, and Bloch would have known, taking such a position would effectively "indict all Jews who did not join".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947) Burguière has pinpointed Bloch's motive for joining the Resistance in his characteristic refusal to mince his words or play half a role.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200948-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200948) Bloch had previously expressed the view that "there can be no salvation where there is not some sacrifice".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-5)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268) He sent his family away and returned to Lyon to join the underground.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-6)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208)

Despite knowing a number of [_francs-tireurs_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francs-Tireurs_et_Partisans "Francs-Tireurs et Partisans") around Lyon, Bloch still found it difficult to join them because of his age.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947-1)[\[46\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947) Although the Resistance recruited heavily among university lecturers[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861104-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861104)—and indeed, Bloch's alma mater, the École Normale Superieure, provided it with many members[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESmith1982268-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESmith1982268)—he commented in exasperation to [Simonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simonne_Vidal "Simonne Vidal") that he "didn't know it is so difficult to offer one's life".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947-2)[\[46\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200947) The French historian and philosopher [François Dosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Dosse "François Dosse") quotes a member of the _franc-tireurs_ active with Bloch as later describing how "that eminent professor came to put himself at our command simply and modestly".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-1)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) Bloch used his professional and military skills on their behalf, writing propaganda for them and organising their supplies and [materiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materiel "Materiel"), becoming a regional organiser.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-7)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) Bloch also joined the _[Mouvements Unis de la Résistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouvements_Unis_de_la_R%C3%A9sistance "Mouvements Unis de la Résistance")_ (Unified Resistance Movement, or MUR),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-2)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) section R1,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWieviorka2016102-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWieviorka2016102) and edited the underground newsletter, _Cahiers Politiques_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-8)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208) He went under various pseudonyms: Arpajon, Chevreuse, Narbonne.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995208-9)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995208)[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[note 4\]](#refnote-54) Often on the move, Bloch used [archival research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archival_research "Archival research") as his excuse for travelling.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991256-1)[\[19\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991256) The journalist-turned-resistance fighter [Georges Altman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Altman "Georges Altman") later told how he knew Bloch as, although originally "a man, made for the creative silence of gentle study, with a cabinet full of books" was now "running from street to street, deciphering secret letters in some Lyonaisse Resistance garret";[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861104-1)[\[47\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGeremek19861104) all Bloch's notes were kept in code.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249-2)[\[40\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991249) For the first time, suggests Lyon, Bloch was forced to consider the role of the individual in history, rather than the collective; perhaps by then even realising he should have done so earlier.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985186-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985186)[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[note 5\]](#refnote-57)

### Capture and execution

Bloch was arrested at the Place de Pont, Lyon,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) during a major [roundup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundup_(history) "Roundup (history)") by the Vichy _[milice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milice "Milice")_ on 8 March 1944, and handed over to the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFreire2015170_n.60-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFreire2015170_n.60) Bloch was using the pseudonym "Maurice Blanchard", and in appearance was "an ageing gentleman, rather short, grey-haired, bespectacled, neatly dressed, holding a briefcase in one hand and a cane in the other".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-1)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) He was renting a room above a dressmaker's shop on rue des Quatre Chapeaux; the Gestapo raided the place the following day. It is possible Bloch had been [denounced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denunciation "Denunciation") by a woman working in the shop.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-2)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) In any case, they found a radio transmitter and many papers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-3)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) Bloch was imprisoned in [Montluc prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montluc_prison "Montluc prison"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-3)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) during which time his wife died.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163-1)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163) While imprisoned, he was tortured, suffering beatings and ice-baths. On occasion, his torturers broke his ribs and wrists, which led to his being returned to his cell unconscious. He eventually caught [bronchopneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchopneumonia "Bronchopneumonia")[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-4)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) and fell seriously ill. It was later claimed that he gave away no information to his interrogators, and while incarcerated taught French history to other inmates.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209)

In the meantime, the allies had [invaded Normandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Normandy "Invasion of Normandy") on 6 June 1944.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-1)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209) As a result, the Nazi regime was keen to evacuate and "liquidate their holdings"[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-5)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) in France; this meant disposing of as many prisoners as they could.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-2)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209) Between May and June 1944 the Nazi occupying forces shot around 700 prisoners in scattered locations to avoid the risk of this becoming common knowledge, thus inviting Resistance reprisals around southern France.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-3)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209) Among those killed was Bloch,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-4)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) one of a group of 26 Resistance prisoners[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-4)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531-1)[\[45\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531) picked out in Montluc[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200939-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200939) and driven along the [Saône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%C3%B4ne "Saône") towards [Trévoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%C3%A9voux "Trévoux")[&#8202;](#ref-back-62-0)[\[note 6\]](#refnote-62) on the night of[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-5)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209) 16 June 1944.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531-2)[\[45\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531) Driven to a field near [Saint-Didier-de-Formans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Didier-de-Formans "Saint-Didier-de-Formans"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEFink1995209-6)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEFink1995209) they were shot by the Gestapo in groups of four.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-6)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) According to Lyon, Bloch spent his last moments comforting a 16-year-old beside him who was worried that the bullets might hurt.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELyon1985186-1)[\[51\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELyon1985186) Bloch fell first, reputedly shouting " _Vive la France_"[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-5)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) before being shot. A [coup de grâce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup_de_gr%C3%A2ce "Coup de grâce") was delivered. One man managed to crawl away and later provided a detailed report of events; the bodies were discovered on 26 June.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-7)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244) For some time Bloch's death was merely a "dark rumour" until it was confirmed to Febvre.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurguière200939-1)[\[56\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurguière200939)

At his burial, his own words were read at the graveside. With them, Bloch proudly acknowledged his Jewish ancestry while identifying foremost as a Frenchman.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967282-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967282)[&#8202;](#ref-back-64-0)[\[note 7\]](#refnote-64) He described himself as "a stranger to any formal religious belief as well as any supposed racial solidarity, I have felt myself to be, quite simply French before anything else".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDosse199444-6)[\[13\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDosse199444) According to his instructions, no orthodox prayers were said over his grave,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163-2)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999163) and on it was to be carved his epitaph _dilexi veritatem_ ("I have loved the truth").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999174-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999174) In 1977, his ashes were transferred from St-Didier to Fougeres and the gravestone was inscribed as he requested.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991258-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991258)

## Later events

Febvre had not approved of Bloch's decision to join the Resistance, believing it to be a waste of his brain and talents,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv) although, as Davies points out, "such a fate befell many other French intellectuals".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268-6)[\[2\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967268)[&#8202;](#ref-back-71-0)[\[note 8\]](#refnote-71) Febvre continued publishing _Annales_, ("if in a considerably modified form" comments Beatrice Gottlieb),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv-1)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv)[&#8202;](#ref-back-73-0)[\[note 9\]](#refnote-73) dividing his time between his country [château](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau "Château") in the [Franche-Comté](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franche-Comt%C3%A9 "Franche-Comté")[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv-2)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEGottlieb1982xv) and working at the École Normale in Paris. This caused some outrage, and, after [liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberation_of_Paris "Liberation of Paris"), when classes were returning to a degree of normality, he was booed by his students at the Sorbonne.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWieviorka2016102-1)[\[49\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWieviorka2016102)

[Intellectual historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history "Intellectual history") [Peter Burke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Burke_(historian) "Peter Burke (historian)") named Bloch the leader of what he called the "French Historical Revolution",[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBurke19907-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBurke19907) and Bloch became an icon for the post-war generation of new historians.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESreedharan2004259-0)[\[66\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESreedharan2004259) Although he has been described as being, to some extent, the object of a [cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult "Cult") in both England and France[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDavies1967265-0)[\[67\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDavies1967265)—"one of the most influential historians of the twentieth century"[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007525-0)[\[68\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007525) by Stirling, and "the greatest historian of modern times" by [John H. Plumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_H._Plumb "John H. Plumb")[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244-8)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWeber1991244)—this is a reputation mostly acquired postmortem.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993273-0)[\[69\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993273) Henry Loyn suggests it is also one which would have amused and amazed Bloch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999165-0)[\[70\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999165) According to Stirling, this posed a particular problem within French historiography when Bloch effectively had [martyrdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr "Martyr") bestowed upon him after the war, leading to much of his work being overshadowed by the last months of his life.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007526-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007526) This led to "indiscriminate heaps of praise under which he is now almost hopelessly buried".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531-3)[\[45\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007531) His legacy has been further complicated by the fact that the second generation of Annalists led by [Fernand Braudel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernand_Braudel "Fernand Braudel") has "co-opted his memory",[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEStirling2007526-1)[\[71\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEStirling2007526)[&#8202;](#ref-back-82-0)[\[note 10\]](#refnote-82) combining Bloch's academic work and Resistance involvement to create "a founding myth".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993282-0)[\[73\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEEpstein1993282) The aspects of his life which made Bloch easy to beatify have been summed up by [Henry Loyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Loyn "Henry Loyn") as "Frenchman and Jew, scholar and soldier, staff officer and Resistance worker ... articulate on the present as well as the past".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELoyn1999162–163-0)[\[74\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELoyn1999162–163)
