# A guide to the Queen Elizabeth Class aircraft carriers

The Queen Elizabeth-class carriers are the largest and potentially most powerful warships ever built for the Royal Navy. This guide examines the origins, design, systems, aircraft, operational role and future development of HMS Queen Elizabeth and HMS Prince of Wales.

The Queen Elizabeth class restored the UK’s fixed-wing carrier strike role after the Harrier force was withdrawn in 2010. The ships provide a sovereign mobile airbase, allowing the UK to deploy combat aircraft without relying entirely on access to overseas airfields. The carriers are also command platforms, bases for helicopters and uncrewed systems, and visible instruments of diplomacy and deterrence. Their value does not rest solely on launching air strikes. They can support anti-submarine warfare, air defence, amphibious operations, disaster relief, evacuation missions and multinational task groups.

**Why these ships matter**

Aircraft carriers are key to naval operations. They provide the UK with a mobile airbase that is hard to target, which can be rapidly moved between regions and carry out operations without requiring permission from a host nation. Their effectiveness, however, depends on the availability of aircraft, escorts, support ships, weapons and trained personnel.

### Rebuilding carrier strike

The two ships emerged from a requirement formulated in the late 1990s to replace the three much smaller Invincible-class aircraft carriers. Although these vessels had served effectively, their limited flight decks, hangars and aviation fuel capacity constrained the number and range of aircraft they could operate.

Early studies examined ships of several sizes and considered Short Take-Off and Vertical Landing (STOVL), Short Take-Off But Arrested Recovery (STOBAR) and conventional catapult-launched aircraft. Operational analysis concluded that a substantial air group would be required for a sustained campaign, pushing the design towards a ship far larger than the Invincible class.

The programme was initially known as the Future Aircraft Carrier and later Carrier Vessel Future (CVF). Competing teams led by BAE Systems and Thales developed concepts before their work was brought together under the Aircraft Carrier Alliance. The resulting design evolved into a conventionally powered carrier of around 65,000 tonnes, optimised for the F-35B Lightning.

The ships were ordered in July 2007, although political indecision and attempts to defer expenditure added delay and cost. Construction was distributed around the UK, with large hull sections built at six shipyards before being assembled at Rosyth. HMS Queen Elizabeth was floated out in 2014 and commissioned in December 2017. HMS Prince of Wales followed in December 2019. Together they restored a fixed-wing carrier strike role lost when the Harrier force was withdrawn.

The long and sometimes torturous evolution of the ships is examined in more detail in [the design history of the Queen Elizabeth class](https://www.navylookout.com/development-of-the-queen-elizabeth-class-aircraft-carrier-a-design-history/).

### Big decks

At 284 metres long, with a flight deck approximately 70 metres across at its widest point, the Queen Elizabeth class is considerably larger than any previous RN warship. Official displacement is 65,000 tonnes, although the figure rises when the ships are fully loaded with fuel, stores, aircraft, weapons and personnel.

The size was not chosen simply for prestige but to support a mixed airwing built around fifth-generation F-35B jets which are more than double the size of the Harriers. Aviation at sea demands space. A larger deck allows aircraft to be launched, recovered, refuelled, rearmed and repositioned with fewer movements and less congestion. It also provides room for helicopters and visiting allied aircraft without bringing fast-jet operations to a halt.

The flight deck covers about 16,000 square metres and includes a 13-degree ski-jump at the bow. The ramp gives an F-35B additional upward momentum during take-off, allowing it to launch with more fuel or weapons than would be possible from a flat deck under the same conditions. The development and wider adoption of this British invention is covered in [the history of RN carrier ski-jumps](https://www.navylookout.com/royal-navy-aircraft-carrier-ski-jumps-a-history/).

Below the flight deck is a hangar measuring about 155 metres long, 33 metres wide and between six and ten metres high. It can accommodate around 20 F-35Bs, depending on how closely they are parked, or a larger number of helicopters and uncrewed aircraft. Two large aircraft lifts connect the hangar with the flight deck. Each can carry two F-35Bs simultaneously and is positioned at the deck edge so aircraft movements do not obstruct the principal runway.

Weapons and stores are moved by a highly automated handling system intended to reduce manpower and accelerate the preparation of aircraft. [Munitions handling aboard the carriers](https://www.navylookout.com/munitions-handling-on-the-royal-navys-aircraft-carriers/) involves protected magazines, mechanical handling equipment and dedicated weapon preparation areas.

A full air group and embarked command staff can take the total complement to around 1,600, although the core ship’s company is about 700. This relatively modest figure for a ship of this size is achieved through extensive automation, particularly in machinery control, stores movement and weapons handling.

### Two islands

The most distinctive feature of the class is the pair of separate islands on the starboard side of the flight deck. The forward island contains the navigation bridge, while the aft island houses flying control. The arrangement was driven principally by the propulsion system. Each island contains the uptakes and exhausts for one of the ship’s two Rolls-Royce MT30 gas turbines. Separating the machinery into forward and aft groups improves survivability because damage to one complex should not disable the other.

The twin islands also reduce the amount of large exhaust trunking that would otherwise pass through the hangar, create less turbulence over the flight deck and provide good separation between the principal radars. The forward position gives the bridge a clear view over the bow, while the aft flying-control position offers a commanding view of aircraft approaching the deck. Both islands contain facilities from which the ship can be controlled if the other is damaged. The engineering and operational reasoning is considered in the article: [why HMS Queen Elizabeth has two islands](https://navylookout.com/the-reasons-hms-queen-elizabeth-has-two-islands/).

### Propulsion and machinery

The carriers use integrated full electric propulsion. Two Rolls-Royce MT30 gas turbines and four Wärtsilä diesel generator sets produce electricity for the propulsion motors, ship systems and domestic load. Four large electric motors drive two shafts and fixed-pitch propellers.

The ships can exceed 25 knots and have a quoted range of about 10,000 nautical miles, although practical endurance is governed by aviation fuel, food, weapons and other stores rather than propulsion fuel alone. A carrier task group depends heavily on Royal Fleet Auxiliary tankers and solid-support ships to remain at sea for extended periods.

Conventional propulsion was selected because nuclear power would have greatly increased construction, infrastructure, training and through-life costs. Even a nuclear-powered carrier must regularly replenish aviation fuel, munitions and provisions, reducing the operational value of unlimited propulsion endurance.

The electric architecture also provides significant generating capacity for future sensors and equipment. Its distributed machinery arrangement gives the ships a measure of propulsion redundancy, although both carriers have experienced serious shaft-line defects during their early service.

### Weapons and protection

The carriers’ fitted hard-kill defences are three Phalanx close-in weapon systems. These radar-directed 20mm Gatling guns are intended as a last line of protection against missiles and aircraft. Heavy and general-purpose machine guns provide defence against small craft and other close threats.

The ships do not carry a surface-to-air missile system. Area defence is principally provided by Type 45 destroyers equipped with Sea Viper, with Sea Ceptor-armed frigates adding another defensive layer. Aircraft, helicopters, electronic warfare, decoys and manoeuvre are all parts of the wider protection scheme.

Reliance on escorts allows the carrier to concentrate on aviation, but it also creates a potential vulnerability if too few suitable warships are available. A missile such as Sea Ceptor would add another layer against weapons that penetrate the outer screen, although installation would consume space, require integration work and potentially interfere with flight-deck operations. The arguments are set out in whether the carriers should receive their own missile defences.

Protection is not limited to weapons. The ships were designed with extensive redundancy, subdivision and resistance to blast, flooding and splinter damage. Machinery is separated, critical systems are duplicated and the hangar can be divided by fire curtains into three sections.

Their large size provides reserve buoyancy and space for damage-control activity, making them difficult to disable with a single hit. The survivability built into HMS Queen Elizabeth is more substantial than descriptions of the ships as fragile floating airfields suggest. No surface warship is invulnerable. Long-range missiles, quiet submarines, mines, cyber attack and increasingly numerous uncrewed systems all present serious threats. Carriers are intended to survive by operating as part of a layered and highly mobile task group, not by absorbing punishment while remaining stationary.

### Sensors and communications

The forward island carries the Type 1046 S1850M long-range air-surveillance radar, while the aft island supports the Type 997 Artisan 3D radar. The S1850M provides broad air-picture surveillance at long range, while Artisan is optimised for tracking air and surface contacts in more cluttered environments.

The ships also carry navigation radars, electro-optical sensors, electronic-support measures, secure satellite communications and extensive command facilities. They are intended to act as the headquarters of a carrier strike group rather than merely as aviation platforms.

Much of their fighting value comes from information gathered and shared by other assets. F-35Bs, Merlin helicopters, escorts, submarines, maritime patrol aircraft, satellites and allied units contribute to a networked picture that may extend hundreds of miles beyond the carrier.

### Aircraft and air groups

The primary combat aircraft is the F-35B Lightning, jointly operated by the Fleet Air Arm and Royal Air Force. Its short take-off and vertical landing system allows it to operate without catapults or arrestor wires.

The selection of STOVL aircraft avoided the cost and technical risk of fitting catapults and arresting gear, reduced the number of specialist deck crew required and allows aircraft to land independently rather than relying on a single arresting system. The disadvantages are shorter range and lower weapons capacity than the carrier-capable F-35C, together with the inability to operate conventional fixed-wing airborne early warning and tanker aircraft. The decision, including the abandoned 2010 proposal to convert the ships for catapult operations, is explored in [why the RN’s carriers operate STOVL aircraft](https://www.navylookout.com/cats-traps-and-claptrap-why-the-royal-navys-new-aircraft-carriers-operate-vstol-aircraft/).

The ships were designed to embark up to 36 F-35Bs for major operations, although normal deployments have generally carried fewer. Operation HIGHMAST in 2025 embarked 24 British jets, the largest concentration of UK carrier-based fast jets since the 1970s. During Exercise Falcon Strike, the air group generated 36 fixed-wing sorties in one day.

Merlin HM2 helicopters provide anti-submarine warfare and airborne surveillance. Selected aircraft carry the Crowsnest radar system, which provides early warning against aircraft, missiles and surface contacts. Merlin HC4s can transport Royal Marines, equipment and supplies, while Wildcat HMA2 helicopters provide reconnaissance and can attack small vessels using Martlet and Sea Venom missiles.

The flight deck can also support Chinook, Apache and allied helicopters. US Marine Corps F-35Bs operated from HMS Queen Elizabeth during the 2021 deployment, and British aircraft have since demonstrated interoperability with US, Italian and Japanese ships.

Over time, the air group is expected to become a mixture of crewed and uncrewed aircraft. The RN’s Maritime Aviation Transformation programme aims to add surveillance, logistics, communications, electronic warfare and potentially combat systems without relying exclusively on scarce and expensive crewed aircraft. The developing concept is described in [the plan for a hybrid carrier air wing](https://www.navylookout.com/maritime-aviation-transformation-the-royal-navys-plan-for-a-hybrid-air-wing/).

### HMS Queen Elizabeth

HMS Queen Elizabeth is the first ship of the class and commissioned in December 2017. She conducted the first British F-35B trials at sea in 2018 and led the [Carrier Strike Group 2021 deploymen](/tag/CSG21)t to the Indo-Pacific.

In February 2024, a defect was discovered in her starboard propeller shaft coupling shortly before she was due to lead Exercise Steadfast Defender. HMS Prince of Wales was prepared at short notice and sailed in her place, demonstrating the principal strategic justification for purchasing two carriers. HMS Queen Elizabeth subsequently underwent planned work at Rosyth, including attention to her propulsion system. Her second docking period ran from August 2025 until spring 2026, after which she returned to Portsmouth for trials, training and maintenance.

HMS Queen Elizabeth is in the regeneration and readiness cycle following this docking period. She provides the alternative carrier while her sister is undertaking front-line tasking.

### HMS Prince of Wales

HMS Prince of Wales commissioned in December 2019. Her early service was interrupted by [flooding incidents](https://www.navylookout.com/one-of-the-reasons-the-royal-navy-needs-two-aircraft-carriers-a-setback-for-hms-prince-of-wales/ "One of the reasons the Royal Navy needs two aircraft carriers – a setback for HMS Prince of Wales") and a major starboard propeller shaft failure shortly after sailing for the United States in August 2022. Repairs at Rosyth kept her out of service until 2023. The ship returned to operations with a notably rapid response in February 2024, replacing HMS Queen Elizabeth on Exercise Steadfast Defender after being prepared to sail in little more than a week.

In 2025, HMS Prince of Wales led [Operation HIGHMAST](/tag/CSG25), a 222-day deployment covering almost 40,000 nautical miles. The Carrier Strike Group operated in the Mediterranean, Indo-Pacific and Red Sea, embarked 24 British F-35Bs and worked extensively with allied ships and aircraft.

The deployment culminated in the declaration of Full Operating Capability for UK Carrier Strike in November 2025. This signified that the UK had demonstrated the personnel, equipment and procedures needed to generate a deployable carrier group, although it did not mean every supporting element was available in ideal numbers.

In 2026, HMS Prince of Wales deployed as the RN flagship and is deployed on Operation FIRECREST in the North Atlantic and High North. She has supported NATO anti-submarine exercises, and embarked F-35Bs for air-policing activity around Iceland.

### Two ships, one available

The purpose of buying two carriers was never to operate two full strike groups continuously. It was to ensure that one carrier could usually be made available while the other underwent maintenance, training or regeneration.

In practice, the distinction between availability and immediate readiness is important. A ship alongside may require weeks or months to assemble its air group, escorts, support ships, stores and trained personnel. The ability of HMS Prince of Wales to replace her sister in 2024 was impressive, but also depended on favourable circumstances and significant effort.

The absence of a large [carrier-compatible dry dock](https://www.navylookout.com/dry-docking-the-royal-navys-aircraft-carriers-what-are-the-options/ "Dry docking the Royal Navy’s aircraft carriers – what are the options?") at Portsmouth is another constraint. Major underwater work requires the ships to return to Rosyth, where entry is affected by tides and the availability of the basin. The carriers’ size provides major operational advantages, but imposes equally large maintenance and infrastructure demands.

### Future development

The ships were built with space, electrical power and weight margins intended to support adaptation over a service life extending into the second half of the century. Future changes are likely to focus on the air group, communications, sensors, defensive weapons and the ability to operate uncrewed aircraft.

The most significant development will be the move towards a hybrid air wing. Uncrewed aircraft could undertake persistent surveillance, communications relay, electronic warfare, logistics and eventually strike missions. This would expand the reach of the carrier without requiring every task to be performed by an F-35B or crewed helicopter.

Launching larger fixed-wing uncrewed aircraft may require assisted take-off and recovery equipment. The RN has explored lightweight catapults, arrestor systems and alternative recovery methods, although no firm programme to fit them has yet been confirmed.

There is also a continuing case for strengthening the carriers’ own missile defences. The growth of anti-ship ballistic missiles, hypersonic weapons and one-way attack aircraft has increased the importance of layered protection. Any installation would need to be balanced against cost, deck space and the risk of interfering with aviation operations.

### Strengths and limitations

The Queen Elizabeth class provides the UK with a mobile airfield, command centre and strategic signalling tool that can be moved without requiring permission from a foreign government. A carrier can alter an opponent’s calculations simply by being positioned within reach of a crisis, while retaining the option to withdraw or escalate.

The ships are large enough to support meaningful aviation, absorb future equipment and operate a varied air group. Their low core manpower, efficient deck arrangement and interoperability with allied aircraft are considerable achievements.

Claims that smaller carriers would necessarily have been cheaper or more useful often overlook the fixed costs of aviation systems, reduced sortie generation and the need for more hulls to provide comparable capacity. [The case for and against their size](https://www.navylookout.com/are-the-royal-navys-aircraft-carriers-too-big/) is more nuanced than the familiar description of them as oversized prestige projects.

Their full value nevertheless depends on assets that are in short supply. The UK has fewer F-35Bs than originally envisaged, limited airborne early warning capacity, a small escort fleet and no currently operational Fleet Solid Support ship dedicated to sustaining a carrier group.

Long deployments consume aircraft hours, engineering capacity, ammunition, spares and personnel at a rate that is difficult for a small fleet to absorb. Operation HIGHMAST proved that the UK can deploy a credible carrier group over global distances, but also reinforced its reliance on allied escorts and support.

This is a strength when partners are available and politically aligned, but a limitation when the UK needs to act independently or allies have competing priorities. [The lessons identified after the 2025 deployment](https://www.navylookout.com/royal-navy-considers-lessons-from-2025-carrier-strike-group-deployment/) include the pressure imposed on people, ships and aircraft by sustained high-tempo operations.

The future of the class will therefore be determined less by the ships themselves than by whether the UK can provide enough aircraft, escorts, weapons, support vessels and trained personnel to exploit them. The hulls have space, electrical power and service life for substantial evolution. Filling that potential remains the harder task.

### Frequently asked questions

**How many aircraft can HMS Queen Elizabeth carry?**

The official maximum figure is up to 36 F-35Bs, although the precise air group depends on the mission and the number of helicopters also embarked. Normal peacetime deployments have carried fewer aircraft, while Operation HIGHMAST in 2025 [embarked 24](https://www.navylookout.com/24-british-f-35-jets-now-embarked-on-hms-prince-of-wales/ "24 British F-35 jets now embarked on HMS Prince of Wales") British F-35Bs.

**Why do the Queen Elizabeth-class carriers have two islands?**

The forward island is principally used for navigation and the aft island for flying control. The arrangement also separates the exhaust systems and machinery groups, improves radar positioning, reduces turbulence and limits the amount of exhaust trunking passing through the hangar.

**Why do the carriers use a ski-jump?**

The [ski-jump](https://www.navylookout.com/royal-navy-aircraft-carrier-ski-jumps-a-history/ "Royal Navy aircraft carrier ski jumps – a history") helps the F-35B gain height and remain airborne during a short take-off. This allows the aircraft to launch with a greater combination of fuel and weapons than would normally be possible from a flat deck of the same length.

**Why do the carriers not have catapults and arrestor wires?**

The ships were designed around the STOVL F-35B, which does not require either system. There was a brief reversal of this decision made in 2010 with the intention of fitting catapults, but [ultimately this was not affordable](https://www.navylookout.com/cats-traps-and-claptrap-why-the-royal-navys-new-aircraft-carriers-operate-vstol-aircraft/ "Cats, traps and claptrap. Why the Royal Navy’s new aircraft carriers operate VSTOL aircraft"). Vertical take-off reduces carrier cost, complexity and crew requirements, although it prevents operation of more capable conventional carrier aircraft, fixed-wing airborne early warning and tanker aircraft.

**Can US aircraft operate from the ships?**

US Marine Corps F-35Bs operated extensively from HMS Queen Elizabeth during the 2021 Carrier Strike Group deployment. The ships can also support V-22 Osprey tilt-rotor aircraft and a range of allied helicopters and were designed with NATO interoperability in mind.

**Why did the UK build two aircraft carriers?**

Two ships make it more likely that one carrier will be available while the other undergoes maintenance, repair or training. This does not mean that two complete carrier strike groups can be maintained continuously.

**Are the Queen Elizabeth class carriers nuclear-powered?**

No. They use gas turbines and diesel generators to produce electricity for propulsion and ship systems. Conventional propulsion is [far more affordable](https://www.navylookout.com/the-reasons-hms-queen-elizabeth-is-not-nuclear-powered/ "The reasons HMS Queen Elizabeth is not nuclear powered") and avoids the infrastructure and personnel requirements associated with nuclear-powered surface ships.

**How well protected are the carriers?**

The ships rely on escorts, aircraft, sensors, electronic warfare and close-range weapons, but they also have extensive subdivision, redundancy and damage-control provisions. Unlike most of the world’s carriers, they [do not have self-defence missiles](https://www.navylookout.com/should-the-royal-navy-aircraft-carriers-be-fitted-with-their-own-missile-defences/ "Should HMS Queen Elizabeth be fitted with her own missile defences?"), which is considered a significant omission. Their close-range hard-kill defences are three [Phalanx](https://www.navylookout.com/app/uploads/2020/08/Last-Ditch-Defence-Phalanx-in-focus-1.jpg "Last-Ditch-Defence-Phalanx-in-focus") Close-In Weapon Systems and crew-served machine guns.

**Are the Queen Elizabeth class carriers too large?**

Their size increases construction, maintenance and infrastructure demands, but also provides a larger flight deck, greater aviation capacity, more space for future equipment and improved damage tolerance. [See detailed discussion on their size here.](https://www.navylookout.com/are-the-royal-navys-aircraft-carriers-too-big/ "Are the Royal Navy’s aircraft carriers too big?")

**How will the carrier air group change?**

The RN intends to introduce more uncrewed aircraft for surveillance, logistics, communications, electronic warfare and potentially strike missions.

**How long will the ships remain in service?**

The carriers were designed for a service life of about 50 years, potentially taking them into the 2060s. Their size and electrical generating capacity provide room for new sensors, weapons and aircraft as technology develops.

### Summary

The Queen Elizabeth class restored British carrier strike on a scale not seen for decades and provides the RN with two highly adaptable aviation platforms designed to serve into the second half of the century. The ships are neither obsolete white elephants nor self-contained instruments of war. Properly supported, they are powerful, flexible and survivable. Without sufficient aircraft, escorts, logistics and people, their impressive size cannot by itself generate combat power.

The carriers were borne out of the 1998 SDR heralding a switch to expeditionary warfare. Their size and design followed seemingly logically from that- a continuation of joint force Harrier but with a STOVL variant of the JSF replacing Harrier on a one for one basis. Scale was determined by the ambition to match sortie rates of a US supercarrier, something the USN would find very useful according to Admiral Wests evidence to the defence select committee in 2004. Supposed to have flexibility in their design to allow the easy retro fitting of catapults, they didn’t. The appalling performance of LM in delivering full mission capable aircraft and the high costs of supporting the aircraft has forced the UK to reduce its planned numbers.  
 The result is that we have bought two HGVs to do courier work.  
 We are stuck with them so the real question is what to do to make them more useful. Switching to CATOBAR would not only be expensive but would undermine the whole idea of a joint RN/RAF force that can scale up on the carriers when needed.  
 The RN is pinning its hopes for increased mass on UCAVs. But that ambition hits the problem, inherent in the design, of the likely need for EM launch plus arrestor systems to operate aircraft size drones.  
 One option, planned in 2017 but quietly dropped, was to enhance the amphibious capabilities of one carrier. But with the downsizing of the RM role, this no longer seems relevant.  
 In the longer term, the likely range of Tempest will make the carriers even less useful to the direct defence of the UK ( that was never their purpose) and the pressure to mothball one of them will only increase.  
 With so many other capabilities needing urgent improvement, the best answer may be to stop spending more on the carriers and accept that we got it wrong.
