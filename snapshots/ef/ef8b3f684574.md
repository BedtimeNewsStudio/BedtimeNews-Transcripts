Saturn V

The **Saturn V**[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[f\]](#refnote-10) is a retired American [super heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle "Super heavy-lift launch vehicle") developed by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") under the [Apollo program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program "Apollo program") for human [exploration of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon "Exploration of the Moon"). The rocket was [human-rated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human-rated "Human-rated"), had [three stages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket "Multistage rocket"), and was [liquid-fueled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket "Liquid-propellant rocket"). Thirteen Saturn V vehicles were launched, from 1967 to 1973, all from [Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39 "Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39"), nine of which launched [24 astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Apollo_astronauts "List of Apollo astronauts") toward the Moon from [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8 "Apollo 8") to [Apollo 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_17 "Apollo 17"). Its final launch was [Skylab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab "Skylab"), the first American [space station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_station "Space station"), converted from its own third stage.

The Saturn V was the first launch vehicle to carry humans beyond [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") (LEO), with the only other to do so being the [Space Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Launch_System "Space Launch System") (SLS) for the [Artemis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_II "Artemis II") lunar flyby in 2026. The Saturn V holds the record for the [largest payload capacity to low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_of_retired_orbital_launch_systems "Comparison of retired orbital launch systems"), 140,000kg (310,000lb), which included the [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") third stage with remaining [propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant "Propellant") to send the [Apollo command and service module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_command_and_service_module "Apollo command and service module") and [Lunar Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Module "Apollo Lunar Module") to the Moon. At 34.5 [meganewtons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meganewton "Meganewton"), it is the fourth most powerful rocket by [thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust "Thrust") at launch and the second most powerful to carry humans, after SLS at 39 meganewtons.

The largest production model of the [Saturn family of rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_(rocket_family) "Saturn (rocket family)"), the Saturn V was designed at the [Marshall Space Flight Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Space_Flight_Center "Marshall Space Flight Center") (MSFC) in [Huntsville, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntsville,_Alabama "Huntsville, Alabama"). The program was managed by American [George Mueller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mueller_(engineer) "George Mueller (engineer)"); technical design was led by [scientists relocated from Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Paperclip "Operation Paperclip"), most notably [Wernher von Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernher_von_Braun "Wernher von Braun"), as well as [Kurt Debus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Debus "Kurt Debus") and [Arthur Rudolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rudolph "Arthur Rudolph"). This group had developed the first US launch vehicles, the [Redstone rocket family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redstone_(rocket_family) "Redstone (rocket family)"), under the [Army Ballistic Missile Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Ballistic_Missile_Agency "Army Ballistic Missile Agency"). All engines were built by [Rocketdyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne "Rocketdyne"). [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing "Boeing") built the [kerolox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerolox "Kerolox") [S-IC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IC "S-IC") first stage powered by five [F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_F-1 "Rocketdyne F-1") engines; these remain the most powerful [single chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_chamber "Combustion chamber") [liquid-fuelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket "Liquid-propellant rocket") engines ever built. [North American Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Aviation "North American Aviation") the [hydrolox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolox "Hydrolox") [S-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-II "S-II") second stage, and [Douglas Aircraft Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Aircraft_Company "Douglas Aircraft Company") the hydrolox [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") third stage, powered by five and one [J-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_J-2 "Rocketdyne J-2") engines respectively. [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") and MSFC designed [the rocket's instrument unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_instrument_unit "Saturn V instrument unit").

The rocket first launched in the November 1967 [Apollo 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_4 "Apollo 4") test flight. Two unused Saturn Vs were built, originally designated for [Apollo 19 and 20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canceled_Apollo_missions "Canceled Apollo missions"); [three ground test articles](#Launch_history) were also built. Unrealized follow-on concepts included the [Saturn II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_II "Saturn II"), [Saturn MLV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_MLV "Saturn MLV"), and [Saturn V ELV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_ELV "Saturn V ELV") vehicles, which could include [UA120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UA120 "UA120") [solid rocket boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_rocket_booster "Solid rocket booster"), the [Saturn C-5N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_C-5N "Saturn C-5N") using a [NERVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NERVA "NERVA") [nuclear thermal rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_thermal_rocket "Nuclear thermal rocket") third stage, and the [Saturn-Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn-Shuttle "Saturn-Shuttle"). Further flights were proposed for the [Skylab B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab_B "Skylab B") space station, [Voyager Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program_(Mars) "Voyager program (Mars)") [orbiters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Mars_orbiters "List of Mars orbiters") and [landers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_landing "Mars landing"), [Prospector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospector_(spacecraft) "Prospector (spacecraft)") [lunar rover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_rover "Lunar rover"), and a [manned Venus flyby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manned_Venus_flyby "Manned Venus flyby").

## History

See also: [Space Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Race "Space Race")

### Background

In September 1945 German rocket technologist [Wernher von Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernher_von_Braun "Wernher von Braun") was brought, under contract, to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") during [Operation Paperclip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Paperclip "Operation Paperclip").[&#8202;](#ref-back-vonBraun-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-vonBraun) Operation Paperclip, authorized by President [Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman "Harry S. Truman"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEJacobsen2014260-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEJacobsen2014260) brought in over 1,600 German rocket engineers and technicians from former [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") to the United States for government employment.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEJacobsen2014Prologue-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEJacobsen2014Prologue) Von Braun, who had helped create the German [V-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2 "V-2") rocket,[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-14) was assigned to the [United States Army Ordnance Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Ordnance_Corps "United States Army Ordnance Corps") at [Fort Strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Strong "Fort Strong"), [Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts "Massachusetts"), then at [Fort Bliss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Bliss "Fort Bliss"), [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas "Texas").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESaurmaWiesman199622–23-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESaurmaWiesman199622–23) During his time at Fort Bliss, von Braun and his team were not given much to work with. In the first couple of months, the Germans were only given "primitive or aged"[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007219-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007219) wooden workshops and were not allowed to leave Fort Bliss without a military escort. In 1950, von Braun remarked to [Daniel Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Lang_(writer) "Daniel Lang (writer)"), a reporter at _[The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker"),_ "At [Peenemünde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peenem%C3%BCnde_Army_Research_Center "Peenemünde Army Research Center") we had been coddled, here you were counting pennies."[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007218–219-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007218–219) However, he wrote books and articles in popular magazines, such as _[Collier's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collier's "Collier's")_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007256–257-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENeufeld2007256–257)[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-19)

This approach changed in 1957, when the Soviets launched [Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1 "Sputnik 1") atop an [R-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-7_(rocket_family) "R-7 (rocket family)") ICBM, which could carry a [thermonuclear warhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon "Thermonuclear weapon") to the U.S.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHuntressMarov201136-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHuntressMarov201136)[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-21)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198018-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198018) The American Army and government began putting more effort towards sending Americans into space before the Soviets.[&#8202;](#ref-back-time58-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-time58) The Army turned to von Braun's team, who had created the [Jupiter series of rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter-C "Jupiter-C").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrzezinski2007248-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrzezinski2007248) The [Juno I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_I "Juno I") rocket launched the [first American satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer_1 "Explorer 1") in January 1958.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBoehmFichtnerHoberg1962163-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBoehmFichtnerHoberg1962163) Von Braun considered the Jupiter series of rockets to be a prototype of the upcoming [Saturn series of rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_(rocket_family) "Saturn (rocket family)"), and referred to it as "an infant Saturn".[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198028-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198028)

### Design process

Main article: [Saturn (rocket family)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_(rocket_family) "Saturn (rocket family)")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Saturn_V_vehicle_configurations.jpg/500px-Saturn_V_vehicle_configurations.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_V_vehicle_configurations.jpg) [Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_Dynamic_Test_Vehicle "Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle") and flight vehicle configurations[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/S-IC_engines_and_Von_Braun.jpg/500px-S-IC_engines_and_Von_Braun.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:S-IC_engines_and_Von_Braun.jpg) [Von Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernher_von_Braun "Wernher von Braun") with the [F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_F-1 "Rocketdyne F-1") engines of the Saturn V first stage at the [U.S. Space and Rocket Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Space_and_Rocket_Center "U.S. Space and Rocket Center")

The Saturn rocket family was named after the planet [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn "Saturn") because the design of the various Saturn rockets evolved from the earlier Jupiter vehicles, which were named after the planet [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197541-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197541) Between 1960 and 1962, the [Marshall Space Flight Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Space_Flight_Center "Marshall Space Flight Center") (MSFC) designed a series of Saturn rockets that could be deployed for Earth orbit and lunar missions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDunarWaring199954-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDunarWaring199954) NASA planned to use a Saturn vehicle as part of the [Earth orbit rendezvous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_orbit_rendezvous "Earth orbit rendezvous") (EOR) method for a lunar mission.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063) Development on the [Saturn C-3 rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_C-3 "Saturn C-3") was just beginning when the MSFC planned an even bigger rocket, the [Saturn C-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_C-4 "Saturn C-4"), which would use four [F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_F-1 "Rocketdyne F-1") engines in its first stage and five [J-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_J-2 "Rocketdyne J-2") engines in its second stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978110-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978110)

On January 25, 1962, NASA gave its approval to build the C-5. The three-stage rocket would consist of the [S-IC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IC "S-IC") first stage, with five F-1 engines; the [S-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-II "S-II") second stage with five J-2 engines; and the [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") third stage, with a single J-2 engine.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198058–59-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198058–59) The C-5 would undergo component testing even before the first model was constructed. The S-IVB third stage would be used as the second stage for the C-1B,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160) which would serve both to demonstrate proof of concept and feasibility for the C-5 and to provide flight data critical to the development of the C-5.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161) Rather than undergoing testing for each major component, the C-5 would be tested in an "all-up" fashion, meaning that the first test flight of the rocket would include complete versions of all three stages. By testing all components at once, far fewer test flights would be required before a crewed launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197550-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197550)
The C-5 was confirmed as NASA's choice for the Apollo program in mid-1962,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161-1)[\[27\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161) and was named the SaturnV in February 1963.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWellsWhiteleyKaregeannes197620-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWellsWhiteleyKaregeannes197620) In the same month, the C designations were dropped; the C-1 became the Saturn I and C-1B became Saturn IB.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161-2)[\[27\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980161)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEWellsWhiteleyKaregeannes197619-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEWellsWhiteleyKaregeannes197619) By November 1962, NASA had switched to and confirmed a [lunar orbit rendezvous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_orbit_rendezvous "Lunar orbit rendezvous") (LOR) method for a lunar mission.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198068-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198068) The outside contractors that were chosen for the construction were: Boeing (S-IC),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192) North American Aviation (S-II),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192-1)[\[32\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192) Douglas Aircraft (S-IVB),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160-1)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160) and IBM ( [S-IU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_instrument_unit "Saturn V instrument unit")).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980245-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980245)

### Selection for Apollo lunar landing

See also: [Project Apollo §Choosing a mission mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Apollo#Choosing_a_mission_mode "Project Apollo")

Early in the planning process, NASA considered three methods for the Moon mission: Earth orbit rendezvous (EOR), [direct ascent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_ascent "Direct ascent"), and lunar orbit rendezvous (LOR). A direct ascent configuration would require an extremely large rocket to send a three-man spacecraft to land directly on the lunar surface with enough fuel to fly back to Earth. NASA proposed the [Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_(NASA_rocket) "Nova (NASA rocket)") for this method.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197961-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197961)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197542-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197542) An EOR would launch the direct-landing spacecraft in two smaller parts which would combine in Earth orbit. A LOR mission would involve a single rocket launching two spacecraft: a [mother ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_ship "Mother ship"), and a smaller, two-man landing module which would rendezvous back with the main spacecraft in lunar orbit. The lander would be discarded and the mother ship would return home.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197542-1)[\[35\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEvon_Braun197542)

Originally, in the early 1960s, when the Saturn project was transferred to NASA from the U.S. Army, direct ascent was the preferred method.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197961-1)[\[34\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197961) At the same time, the Air Force had been developing of a lunar mission named [Lunex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunex_Project "Lunex Project"), which would use the direct ascent method. The [Space Systems Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Systems_Command "Space Systems Command") estimated that a mission to the Moon using direct ascent could be done by 1967 at an estimated cost of $7.5 billion (equivalent to $60.1billion in 2024). NASA dismissed both of the rendezvous methods as "dangerous and impractical." Von Braun's team had shown an interest in using the EOR method as early as 1958, arguing that smaller vehicles could be used.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197962-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197962) Around the same time, [Thomas Dolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Dolan_(engineer) "Tom Dolan (engineer)") and his team from the [Vought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ling-Temco-Vought "Ling-Temco-Vought") Astronautics Division became the first to study the LOR method. Although his team presented their ideas to NASA, nothing came from his proposal.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197966–67-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197966–67)

In 1960, several NASA officials, including [Langley Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Research_Center "Langley Research Center") engineer [John Houbolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Houbolt "John Houbolt"), argued that a lunar orbit rendezvous provided the simplest landing on the Moon with the most cost–efficient launch vehicle, and the best chance to accomplish the lunar landing within the decade.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197967–68-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197967–68)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063-1)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063) Throughout 1961, Houbolt and his team went around convincing other research teams to pursue LOR,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197969–70-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197969–70) catching the attention of the chief of the Engineering Division, [James Chamberlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Chamberlin "Jim Chamberlin"). Although in charge of what would later become [Project Gemini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Gemini "Project Gemini"), he proposed using a two-man spacecraft using LOR to send a one-man lunar lander to the surface of the Moon. Although Chamberlin's plan stalled, it did mark a shift towards LOR.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197973–75-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBrooksGrimwoodSwenson197973–75) Houbolt also managed to convince NASA official [George Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Low "George Low").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063-2)[\[23\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198063) In June 1962, von Braun announced LOR would be MSFC's choice.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198066-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198066) As more NASA officials became convinced, LOR was officially selected and announced as the mission configuration for the Apollo program on November 7, 1962 by NASA administrator [James E. Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_E._Webb "James E. Webb").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein198068-1)[\[31\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein198068)

### Development

Main articles: [Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle §Development of the test article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_Dynamic_Test_Vehicle#Development_of_the_test_article "Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle"), and [SA-500F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-500F "SA-500F")

A [boilerplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilerplate_(spaceflight) "Boilerplate (spaceflight)") [Apollo spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_(spacecraft) "Apollo (spacecraft)"), BP-027 was used for all configurations of dynamic testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a2-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a2) The boilerplate took the place of actual flight hardware. Boilerplate size, shape, mass, and center of gravity were the same, but it was not necessary for the entire Apollo spacecraft to be completed to commence dynamic testing. The boilerplate was outfitted with instrumentation to record data for engineering study and evaluation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19754-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19754) BP-27 was accepted at the Marshall Space Flight Center in late September 1964.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEErtelMorse1973248-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEErtelMorse1973248)

The third stage, the S-IVB-D arrived at MSFC before any other Saturn V stage because it was first needed for dynamic testing of the [Saturn IB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_IB "Saturn IB") rocket.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160-2)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160) The third stage arrived on January 4, 1965.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens197199–100-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens197199–100) Next, the instrument unit, S-IU-200D/500D, was built. Unlike the other major components of the rocket, the instrument unit was built in Huntsville, Alabama, where the MSFC is located.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980245-1)[\[33\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980245) The ring was completed in January 1965 and electronic components from IBM installed by February 1. Like the third stage, it arrived before the other stages because it was needed for dynamic testing in the Saturn IB first.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971100–101-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971100–101)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980247-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980247)

The first stage of the Saturn V rocket, S-IC-D, set out on the maiden voyage of NASA barge _Poseidon_ to Marshall Space Flight Center on October 6, 1965, and arrived on October 13.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971122-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971122) While the first stage was on its way, dynamic testing for the Saturn V program using the test rocket [SA-500D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_dynamic_test_vehicle "Saturn V dynamic test vehicle") began on October 8.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971122-1)[\[48\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971122) The first stage was lifted into place in the dynamic test stand January 13, 1966.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971129-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971129)

The second stage of the SA-500D had a complex history. Originally, NASA wanted to use the S-II-D stage for its dynamic testing. However, in the spring of 1965, NASA canceled the production of the S-II-D stage and instead opted to use the S-II-S stage for its dynamic tests.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980223-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980223) The S-II-S stage, which [North American Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Aviation "North American Aviation")'s Space and Information Systems Division (S&ID) at [Seal Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_Beach,_California "Seal Beach, California") had completed by January 31, 1965,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971101-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971101) was re-designated as S-II-S/D to be used for dynamic testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980224-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980224) The S-II-S/D would rupture and be destroyed during a test on September 29, 1965 at Seal Beach. It was discovered that the test was exercising a considerable margin above the structural limits required for flight, approximately 144 percent of its designed load limit.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980224-1)[\[52\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980224)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971120-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971120) Because of this, NASA was forced to substitute the S-II-T stage for testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971120–121-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971120–121) In early 1966, the S-II-T was re-designated S-II-T/D, so it could be used for dynamic testing as well as engine firing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971131-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971131) On May 28, 1966, S-II-T/D was undergoing a pressure test to find a hydrogen leak, but the hydrogen pressure sensors and switches had been disconnected unbeknownst to the second-shift crew. As a result, the crew, believing that a valve was leaking liquid hydrogen, began closing valves. This caused the liquid hydrogen tank to over pressurize and explode, injuring five men and hospitalizing two others.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971142–143-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971142–143)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980229-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980229)

After the S-II-T/D was destroyed, the S-II-F stage was assigned to dynamic test duties. The S-II-F stage was at the Kennedy Space Center being used as a non-flight version of the stage. Being shipped from Seal Beach, California, to [Kennedy Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center "Kennedy Space Center") where it arrived March 4.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971133,_135-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971133,_135) The S-II-F, now designated S-II-F/D, arrived at the MSFC on November 10, 1966.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971155-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971155)

### Testing

Main article: [Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle §Dynamic testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_Dynamic_Test_Vehicle#Dynamic_testing "Saturn V Dynamic Test Vehicle")

Dynamic testing examined "the vehicle's response to lateral, longitudinal, and torsional excitation, simulating those that would be experienced in flight. The vehicle was "mounted on a hydrodynamic support system made up of four hydraulic/pneumatic pedestals to permit a simulated unrestrained reaction." Engineers tested vibrations in one plane at a time with different amounts of ballast simulating "fuel load at critical time points in the flight trajectory."[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/SA-500D_in_Dynamic_Test_Stand_Configuration_I.jpg/500px-SA-500D_in_Dynamic_Test_Stand_Configuration_I.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SA-500D_in_Dynamic_Test_Stand_Configuration_I.jpg) In Configuration One, SA-500D was fully assembled inside the dynamic test facility.

Dynamic tests were conducted in three configurations, one for each phase of Saturn V-powered flight. Configuration I tested the entire stack for its bending and vibration characteristics, as if the vehicle had just been launched. Configuration II tested the stack as if the first stage had jettisoned and the second stage were firing,[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-:0) and configuration III tested only the third stage and Apollo spacecraft.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a4-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a4) Tests began with Configuration III in the Saturn IB dynamic test facility.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5-1)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5) Configuration III testing took place in late 1965.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a6-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a6) Configuration I testing followed in the Saturn V dynamic test stand, then Configuration II in the same place.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5-2)[\[60\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965a5) With all the components at MSFC as of November 10, 1966,[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-1)[\[61\]](#refnote-:0)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971155-1)[\[59\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971155) the second stage was stacked atop the first inside the dynamic test stand on November 23. The third stage was added to the stack November 30, and the Instrument Unit and boilerplate Apollo were installed in December. The rocket was stacked and ready for "Configuration One" testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-2)[\[61\]](#refnote-:0)

Configuration One Testing finished on March 11.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971169-0)[\[64\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971169) Testing produced "several minor irregularities indicating the need for possible engineering changes" Configuration Two testing followed; in which the first stage was removed from the stack to simulate conditions after the first stage had jettisoned.[&#8202;](#ref-back-71-0)[\[65\]](#refnote-71) On August 3, 1967, MSFC announced the successful completion of the dynamic test program, thereby declaring the dynamics and structures of the Saturn V ready for its first launch later in the year. The dynamic testing resulted in "several slight modifications" to the final flight vehicle.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEAkens1971192-0)[\[66\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEAkens1971192)

## Specifications

[![Drawing of a Saturn V rocket, showing all the stages of the rocket with brief descriptions and two tiny people to show relative size.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Saturn_v_schematic.svg/500px-Saturn_v_schematic.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_v_schematic.svg) Saturn V diagram

The size and payload capacity of the Saturn V dwarfed those of all other previous rockets successfully flown at that time. With the [Apollo spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_(spacecraft) "Apollo (spacecraft)") on top, the Saturn V stood 363ft (111m) tall, and, ignoring the fins, had a diameter of 33ft (10m) at its base.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681-0)[\[90\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681) Fully fueled, the Saturn V had a mass of 2,822,171 to 2,965,241kg (6,221,823 to 6,537,238lb),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEOrloff2001284-0)[\[91\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEOrloff2001284) with a low Earth orbit (LEO) payload capacity of about 140,000kg (310,000lb),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESynthesis_Group199131-0)[\[92\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESynthesis_Group199131) and could send about 43,500kg (95,900lb) to the Moon.[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto1-0)[\[93\]](#refnote-auto1)

The Saturn V was primarily designed by the [Marshall Space Flight Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Space_Flight_Center "Marshall Space Flight Center") in Huntsville, Alabama.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160-3)[\[26\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980160) The rocket used the powerful [F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-1_(rocket_engine) "F-1 (rocket engine)") and [J-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J-2_(rocket_engine) "J-2 (rocket engine)") [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine "Rocket engine").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681-1)[\[90\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681) When all five F-1 engines of the first stage were tested together at the [Stennis Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stennis_Space_Center "Stennis Space Center"), their low-frequency roar shattered the plate-glass window of a bank building in [Picayune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picayune,_Mississippi "Picayune, Mississippi"), 15 miles away.[&#8202;](#ref-back-100-0)[\[94\]](#refnote-100) Designers decided early on to attempt to use as much technology from the [Saturn I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I "Saturn I") program as possible for the Saturn V. Consequently, the S-IVB third stage of the Saturn V was based on the [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") second stage of the Saturn I.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980157-0)[\[95\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980157) The Saturn V was primarily constructed of [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum "Aluminum"), [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium "Titanium"), [polyurethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyurethane "Polyurethane"), [cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_(material) "Cork (material)") and [asbestos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asbestos "Asbestos").[&#8202;](#ref-back-NPS-construction-0)[\[96\]](#refnote-NPS-construction) Blueprints and other plans of the rocket are available on [microfilm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfilm "Microfilm") at the Marshall Space Flight Center.[&#8202;](#ref-back-MSFC-plans-0)[\[97\]](#refnote-MSFC-plans)

The Saturn V consisted of three stages—the S-IC first stage, S-II second stage, S-IVB third stage, and the [instrument unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_instrument_unit "Saturn V instrument unit").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681-2)[\[90\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681) All three stages used [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") (LOX) as the [oxidizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agent "Oxidizing agent"). The first stage used [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1 "RP-1") for fuel,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-0)[\[98\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684) while the second and third stages used [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen "Liquid hydrogen") (LH2).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-5,_6-0)[\[99\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-5,_6) LH2 has a higher [specific energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_energy "Specific energy") (energy per unit mass) than RP-1, which makes it more suitable for higher-energy orbits, such as the [trans-lunar injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-lunar_injection "Trans-lunar injection") required for Apollo missions. Conversely, RP-1 offers higher [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density "Energy density") (energy per unit volume) and higher thrust than LH2, which makes it more suitable for reducing aerodynamic drag and gravity losses in the early stages of launch. If the first stage had used LH2, the volume required would have been greater, which would have been aerodynamically infeasible at the time.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192-2)[\[32\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980192) The second and third stages also used small solid-propellant [ullage motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ullage_motor "Ullage motor") that helped to separate the stages during the launch to ensure proper positioning of the liquid propellants for pump intake.[&#8202;](#ref-back-106-0)[\[100\]](#refnote-106)

### S-IC first stage

Main article: [S-IC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IC "S-IC")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Apollo_8_first_stage_in_the_Vehicle_Assembly_Building.jpg/500px-Apollo_8_first_stage_in_the_Vehicle_Assembly_Building.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Apollo_8_first_stage_in_the_Vehicle_Assembly_Building.jpg) The first stage of [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8 "Apollo 8") Saturn V being erected in the [VAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_Assembly_Building "Vehicle Assembly Building") on February 1, 1968

The S-IC was built by the Boeing Company at the [Michoud Assembly Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michoud_Assembly_Facility "Michoud Assembly Facility"), New Orleans, and the [Mississippi Test Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stennis_Space_Center "Stennis Space Center") (now known as the Stennis Space Center), [Hancock County, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hancock_County,_Mississippi "Hancock County, Mississippi").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825-0)[\[101\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825) Most of its launch mass was propellant: RP-1 fuel with liquid oxygen as the oxidizer.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTELennick200646-0)[\[102\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTELennick200646) The stage was 42m (138ft) tall and 10m (33ft) in diameter. It provided 33,000 [kN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit) "Newton (unit)") (7,500,000 [lbf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_(force) "Pound (force)")) of thrust at sea level. The S-IC had a dry mass of about 137,000kg (303,000lb). When fully fueled at launch, it had a total mass of 2,214,000kg (4,881,000lb).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825-1)[\[101\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825) The S-IC was powered by five [Rocketdyne F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_F-1 "Rocketdyne F-1") engines arrayed in a [quincunx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quincunx "Quincunx"). The center engine was fixed, while the four outer engines were [hydraulically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic "Hydraulic") turned with [gimbals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal "Gimbal") to steer the rocket.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-1)[\[103\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684) The S-IC had a burn time of approximately 150 seconds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825-2)[\[101\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196825)

#### Structure

The S-IC structure design reflects the requirements of the F-1 engines, [propellants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant "Propellant"), control, [instrumentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumentation "Instrumentation"), and interfacing systems. The stage is primarily built of [aluminum alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_alloy "Aluminium alloy"), specifically [7075](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7075_aluminium_alloy "7075 aluminium alloy") and [2219](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2219_aluminium_alloy "2219 aluminium alloy") aluminum alloys. The major components are the forward skirt, oxidizer tank, intertank section, fuel tank, and thrust structure. The [aft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aft "Aft") end of the forward skirt is attached to the oxidizer (liquid oxygen) tank and the forward end interfaces with the [S-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-II "S-II"). The skin panels, fabricated from 7075 aluminum, are stiffened and strengthened by ring frames and stringers.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-2)[\[104\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

The 1,310,000L (345,000U.S.gal) liquid oxygen tank is the structural link between the forward skirt and the intertank section. Ring baffles attached to the skin stiffeners stabilize the tank wall and serve to reduce liquid oxygen sloshing. The tank is made of 2219 aluminum alloy and is a cylinder with [ellipsoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoid "Ellipsoid") upper and lower [bulkheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulkhead_(partition) "Bulkhead (partition)"). The skin thickness is tapered in eight steps from 0.65cm (0.254in) at the aft section to 0.48cm (0.190in) at the forward section. The intertank structure helps provide structural continuity between the liquid oxygen and fuel tanks. The skin panels and ring frames are fabricated from 7075 aluminum.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-3)[\[104\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

The 820,000L (216,000U.S.gal) fuel tank provides the structural link between the thrust and intertank structures. It is [cylindrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder "Cylinder") with ellipsoidal upper and lower bulkheads. Anti-slosh ring baffles are located on the inside wall of the tank and anti- [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex "Vortex") [cruciform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruciform "Cruciform") [baffles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baffle_(heat_transfer) "Baffle (heat transfer)") are located in the lower bulkhead area. Five liquid oxygen ducts run from the liquid oxygen tank, through the RP-1 tank, and terminate at the F-1 engines. The 2219 aluminum skin thickness is decreased in four steps from 0.49cm (0.193in) at the aft section to 0.43cm (0.170in) at the forward section.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-4)[\[104\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/Saturn_V_S-IC_diagram.png/500px-Saturn_V_S-IC_diagram.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_V_S-IC_diagram.png) Cutaway diagram of the S-IC

The thrust structure assembly redistributes the loads of the five F-1 engines to the periphery of the fuel tank. It also provides support for the engine accessories, base [heat shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield "Heat shield"), engine [fairings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_fairing "Aircraft fairing") and [fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin "Fin"), propellant lines, [retrorockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrorocket "Retrorocket"), and environmental control ducts. The lower thrust ring has four holddown points, which support the fully loaded rocket and, as necessary, restrain the vehicle from lifting off at full F-1 engine thrust. The skin segments are fabricated from 7075 aluminum alloy.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-5)[\[104\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

#### Electrical and instrumentation systems

The electrical power system of the S-IC is divided into three basic subsystems: an operational power subsystem, a measurement power subsystem, and a visual instrumentation power subsystem. On-board power is supplied by five 28-volt batteries, one each for the operational and measurement power systems. The operational power system battery supplies power to operational loads such as [valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valve "Valve") controls, purge and venting systems, [pressurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurization "Pressurization") systems, sequencing systems, and flight control. The measurement power system battery supplies power to measurement loads such as [telemetry systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemetry "Telemetry"), [transducers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transducer "Transducer"), [multiplexers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplexer "Multiplexer"), and [transmitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmitter "Transmitter"). Both batteries supply power to their loads through a common [main power distributor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_distribution "Electric power distribution"), but each system is completely [isolated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanic_isolation "Galvanic isolation") from the others. In the visual instrumentation system, two batteries provide power for the liquid-oxygen tank strobe lights, while a third battery energizes the control circuits, camera motors, and thrusters of the film-camera portion of the visual instrumentation system.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-6)[\[105\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

The instrumentation system monitors functional operations of stage systems and provides signals for vehicle tracking during the S-IC [burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_maneuver "Orbital maneuver"). Prior to liftoff, measurements were telemetered by [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable "Coaxial cable") to ground support equipment. During flight, data is transmitted to ground stations over [radio frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_frequency "Radio frequency") (RF) links. The offset Doppler (ODOP) system uses the [Doppler principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect "Doppler effect") to provide vehicle position and acceleration data during flight.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684-7)[\[106\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19684)

### S-II second stage

Main article: [S-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-II "S-II")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Saturn_V%2C_S-II_Stage_is_Lifted_into_Test_Stand_-_GPN-2000-000540.jpg/500px-Saturn_V%2C_S-II_Stage_is_Lifted_into_Test_Stand_-_GPN-2000-000540.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_V,_S-II_Stage_is_Lifted_into_Test_Stand_-_GPN-2000-000540.jpg) An S-II stage hoisted onto the A-2 test stand at the [Mississippi Test Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_Test_Facility "Mississippi Test Facility")

The S-II was built by [North American Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Aviation "North American Aviation") at Seal Beach, California. Using liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, it had five Rocketdyne J-2 engines arranged similarly to the S-IC,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863-0)[\[107\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863) and also used the four outer engines for control.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-0)[\[108\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685) The S-II was 24.87m (81ft 7in) tall with a diameter of 10m (33ft), identical to the S-IC. The S-II had a dry mass of about 43,000kg (95,000lb); when fully fueled, it weighed 470,000kg (1,037,000lb). The second stage accelerated the Saturn V through the upper atmosphere with 4,400kN (1,000,000lbf) of thrust in a vacuum. The S-II had a burn time of 395 seconds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196861-0)[\[109\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196861) When loaded with fuel, more than 90 percent of the mass of the stage was propellant.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863-1)[\[107\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863)

#### Structure

The S-II consisted of a body shell structure (forward and aft skirts and interstage), a propellant tank structure (liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen tanks), and a thrust structure. The three are of the same basic design except that the aft skirt and interstage were generally of heavier construction because of higher structural loads placed on them. Each unit is a cylindrical shell of [semi-monocoque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-monocoque "Semi-monocoque") construction, built of 7075 aluminum alloy material, stiffened by external hat-section stringers and stabilized internally by circumferential ring frames. The forward skirt has a basic skin thickness of 0.10cm (0.040in), while the aft skirt and interstage both have basic skin thicknesses of 0.18cm (0.071in).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-1)[\[110\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

The thrust structure, like the body shell structure, is of semi-monocoque construction but in the form of a [truncated cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_cone "Truncated cone") increasing in size from approximately 5.5 metres (18ft) to 10 metres (33ft) in diameter. It is stiffened by circumferential ring frames and hat-section stringers like the body shell structure. Four pairs of thrust [longerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longeron "Longeron") (two at each outboard engine location) and a center engine support beam distribute the thrust loads of the J-2 engines. The shell structure is of 7075 aluminum alloy. A fiberglass honeycomb heat shield, supported from the lower portion of the thrust structure, protects the stage base area from excessive temperatures.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-2)[\[110\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

The liquid hydrogen tank consists of a long cylinder with a [concave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concave_polygon "Concave polygon") modified ellipsoidal bulkhead forward and a convex modified ellipsoidal bulkhead aft. The aft bulkhead is also used by the liquid oxygen tank. The liquid hydrogen tank wall is composed of six cylindrical sections. Wall sections and bulkheads are all fabricated from [2014 aluminum alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_aluminium_alloy "2014 aluminium alloy") and are joined together by [fusion welding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_welding "Fusion welding"). The forward bulkhead has an 11m (36ft) diameter wide access [manhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhole "Manhole") built into its center. The common bulkhead is an adhesive-bonded sandwich assembly employing facing sheets of 2014 aluminum alloy and [fiberglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiberglass "Fiberglass")/ [phenolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenols "Phenols") [honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_(geometry) "Honeycomb (geometry)") core to prevent heat transfer and retain the [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenics "Cryogenics") properties of the two fluids to which it was exposed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-3)[\[110\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Saturn_V_S-II_diagram.png/500px-Saturn_V_S-II_diagram.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_V_S-II_diagram.png) Cutaway diagram of the S-II

The liquid oxygen tank consists of ellipsoidal fore and aft halves. The tank is fitted with three ring-type slosh baffles to control propellant sloshing and minimize surface disturbances and cruciform baffles to prevent the generation of vortices at the tank outlet ducts and to minimize residuals. A six-port sump assembly located at the lowest point of the tank provides a fill and drain opening and openings for five engine feed lines.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-4)[\[111\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

#### Electrical and instrumentation systems

The S-II electrical system consists of the electrical power and electrical control subsystems. The electrical power system provides the stage with the electrical power source and distribution. The electrical power system consists of six [DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current "Direct current") bus systems and a ground supplied [AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current "Alternating current") bus system. In flight, the electrical power system busses are energized by four [zinc-silver oxide batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_zinc_battery "Silver zinc battery"). The electrical control system interfaces with the instrument unit (IU) to accomplish the mission requirements of the stage. The [Launch Vehicle Digital Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_Vehicle_Digital_Computer "Launch Vehicle Digital Computer") (LVDC) in the IU controls in-flight sequencing of stage functions through the stage switch selector. The stage switch selector can provide up to 112 individual outputs in response to the appropriate commands. These outputs are routed through the stage electrical sequence controller or the separation controller to accomplish the directed operation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-5)[\[112\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

The S-II instrumentation system consists of both operational and [R&D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development "Research and development") measurement and telemetry systems. The measurement system monitors and measures conditions on the stage while the telemetry system transmits this information to ground stations. The measurement system consists of transducers, [signal conditioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_conditioning "Signal conditioning"), and distribution equipment necessary to provide the required measurement ranges and to present suitably scaled signals to the telemetry system. The measurement system monitors numerous stage conditions and characteristics. This data is processed and conditioned into a form acceptable to the telemetry systems. The telemetry system accepts the signals produced by the measuring portion of the instrumentation system and transmits them to the ground stations. Telemetry equipment includes signal multiplexers, [subcarrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcarrier "Subcarrier") [oscillators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_oscillator "Electronic oscillator"), [amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplifier "Amplifier"), modulators, transmitters, [RF power amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RF_power_amplifier "RF power amplifier"), RF multiplexers and an omni-directional system of four [antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_(radio) "Antenna (radio)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685-6)[\[113\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19685)

### S-IVB third stage

Main article: [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/Saturn_IB_S-IVB-206.jpg/500px-Saturn_IB_S-IVB-206.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_IB_S-IVB-206.jpg) An S-IVB being moved out of NASA's [Vehicle Assembly Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_Assembly_Building "Vehicle Assembly Building")

The S-IVB stage was built by [Douglas Aircraft Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Aircraft_Company "Douglas Aircraft Company") at Huntington Beach, California.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196820-0)[\[114\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196820) It had one Rocketdyne J-2 engine and used liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879) The S-IVB used a common bulkhead to separate the two tanks.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-0)[\[115\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686) It was 17.86m (58ft 7in) tall with a diameter of 6.60m (21ft 8in) and was also designed with high mass efficiency, though not quite as aggressively as the S-II. The S-IVB had a dry mass of about 15,200kg (33,600lb) and, when fully fueled, weighed about 120,500kg (265,600lb).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879-2)[\[1\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879) The S-IVB had a burn time of 165 seconds the first burn, and 312 seconds for the second burn. Its single J-2 engine produced 1,000kN (225,000lbf) of thrust.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879-3)[\[1\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196879)

#### Structure

The S-IVB consists of the following structural assemblies: the forward skirt, propellant tanks, aft skirt, thrust structure, and aft interstage. These assemblies, with the exception of the [propellant tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant_tank "Propellant tank"), are all of a skin/ [stringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longeron "Longeron") type aluminum alloy [airframe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airframe "Airframe") construction. In addition, there are two longitudinal tunnels which house wiring, pressurization lines, and propellant dispersion systems. The tunnel covers are made of aluminum stiffened by [internal ribs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rib_(aeronautics) "Rib (aeronautics)"). The forward skirt, cylindrical in shape, extends forward from the intersection of the liquid hydrogen tank sidewall and the forward dome, providing a hard attach point for the instrument unit (IU). It is the [load supporting member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_support "Structural support") between the liquid hydrogen tank and the IU. An access door in the IU allows servicing of the equipment in the forward skirt.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-1)[\[115\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686)

The thrust structure assembly is an inverted, truncated cone attached at its large end to the [aft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ship_directions "List of ship directions") dome of the liquid oxygen tank and attached at its small end to the engine mount. It provides the attach point for the J-2 engine and distributes the engine thrust over the entire tank circumference. Attached externally to the thrust structure are the engine piping, wiring and interface panels, eight ambient helium spheres, [hydraulic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics "Hydraulics") system, oxygen/hydrogen burner, and some of the engine and liquid oxygen tank instrumentation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-2)[\[115\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686)

The propellant tank is cylindrical with a hemispherical dome at each end, and a common bulkhead to separate the liquid oxygen from the liquid hydrogen. This bulkhead is of sandwich type construction consisting of two parallel hemispherical shaped 2014 aluminum alloy domes bonded to and separated by a fiberglass-phenolic honeycomb core. The internal surface of the liquid hydrogen tank is machine- [milled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milling_(machining) "Milling (machining)") in a waffle pattern to obtain required tank stiffness with minimum structural weight. Attached to the inside of the liquid hydrogen tank are: a 10m (34ft) propellant utilization probe, nine cold [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium") spheres, brackets with temperature and level sensors, a chill-down pump, a [slosh baffle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slosh_baffle "Slosh baffle"), a slosh deflector, and fill, pressurization and vent pipes. Attached to the inside of the liquid oxygen tank are slosh baffles, a chill-down pump, a 4.1m (13.5ft) propellant utilization probe, temperature and level sensors, and fill, pressurization, and vent pipes. Attached externally to the propellant tank are helium pipes, propellant dispersion components, and wiring which passes through two tunnel fairings.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-3)[\[115\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Saturn_V_S-IVB_diagram.png/500px-Saturn_V_S-IVB_diagram.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_V_S-IVB_diagram.png) Cutaway diagram of the S-IVB stage

#### Electrical and instrumentation systems

The electrical system of the S-IVB consists of two major subsystems: the electrical power subsystem which consists of all power sources on the stage; and the electrical control subsystem which distributes power and control signals to various loads throughout the stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-4)[\[116\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686) On-board power is supplied by four zinc/silver-oxide batteries. Two are located in the forward equipment area and two in the aft equipment area. These batteries are activated during the final pre-launch preparations. Heaters and instrumentation probes are an integral part of each battery.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-5)[\[117\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686) The electrical control subsystem function is to distribute the command signals required to control the electrical components of the stage. The major components of the electrical control subsystem are the power and control distributors, the sequencer assemblies, and the pressure sensing and control devices.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-6)[\[118\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686)

The S-IVB instrumentation monitors functional operations of stage systems. Before liftoff, measurements are telemetered by coaxial cable to ground support equipment. During flight, radio frequency antennae convey data to ground stations, similar to the other two stages. The telemetry system consists of a [pulse-code-modulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse-code_modulation "Pulse-code modulation") (PCM) digital [data acquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_acquisition "Data acquisition") system (DDAS) for pre-launch checkout. The stage also contains a PCM frequency modulated (PCM/ [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_broadcasting "FM broadcasting")) system, a FM/FM system, and a [single sideband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-sideband_modulation "Single-sideband modulation") (SS/FM) system for launch information. The radio frequency (RF) subsystem consists of a PCM-RF assembly, bi-directional coupler, [RF detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RF_probe "RF probe"), [DC amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-coupled_amplifier "Direct-coupled amplifier"), coaxial switch, [dummy load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dummy_load "Dummy load"), RF [power divider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_dividers_and_directional_couplers "Power dividers and directional couplers"), and associated cabling. Omnidirectional antenna pattern coverage is provided by the folded-sleeve [dipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole_antenna "Dipole antenna"). The [effective radiating power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_radiated_power "Effective radiated power") of the system is 20 [watts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt "Watt") nominal and 16 watts minimum.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686-7)[\[119\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19686)

### Instrument unit

Main article: [Saturn V Instrument Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_Instrument_Unit "Saturn V Instrument Unit")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/IU-501_From_Below.jpg/500px-IU-501_From_Below.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:IU-501_From_Below.jpg) The [instrument unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V_Instrument_Unit "Saturn V Instrument Unit") for the [Apollo 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_4 "Apollo 4") Saturn V

The Instrument Unit (IU) is a cylindrical structure installed on top of the S-IVB. The IU contains the guidance, navigation, and control equipment. In addition, it contains telemetry, communications, tracking, and crew safety systems, along with their supporting electrical power and environmental control systems.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19687-0)[\[120\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19687) Developed from the [Saturn I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I "Saturn I") IU, the Saturn V's IU was designed by the Marshall Space Flight Center and built by [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") at their Huntsville, Alabama facility.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968111–112-0)[\[121\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968111–112) The basic IU structure is a short cylinder fabricated of an aluminum alloy honeycomb sandwich material. The structure is fabricated from three honeycomb sandwich segments of equal length. The top and bottom edges are made from extruded aluminum channels bonded to the [honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_structure "Honeycomb structure") sandwich. This type of construction was selected for its high strength-to-weight ratio, [acoustical insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soundproofing "Soundproofing"), and thermal conductivity properties. The cylinder is manufactured in three 120-degree segments, which are joined by splice plates into an integral structure. The access door segment has an umbilical door, as well as an equipment/personnel access door. The access door has the requirement to carry flight loads and still be removable at any time prior to flight.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19687-1)[\[122\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19687) The IU has a diameter of 6.6m (260in), a height of 0.91m (36in), and a weight of around 2,000kg (4,500lb).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968109-0)[\[123\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968109)

The first stage was then held in place by four support arms, and the rest of the stages stacked vertically in order. After assembly and testing were completed, the entire stack was moved from the Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB) to the launch pad using the [Crawler Transporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crawler_Transporter "Crawler Transporter") (CT).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968143-1)[\[131\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation1968143) Built by the [Marion Power Shovel Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marion_Power_Shovel_Company "Marion Power Shovel Company"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978326-0)[\[134\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978326) the CT ran on four double-tracked treads, each with 57 "shoes". Each shoe weighed around 900kg (2,000lb). Each CT had a length of 40m (130ft) and a width of 35m (115ft). This transporter was also required to keep the rocket level within 10 [minutes of arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes_of_arc "Minutes of arc") (0.16 degrees), as it traveled the 5.535km (3.439mi) to the launch site,[&#8202;](#ref-back-NASA-cways-0)[\[135\]](#refnote-NASA-cways) especially at the 5 percent [grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_(slope) "Grade (slope)") encountered at the launch pad.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NASAsci-0)[\[136\]](#refnote-NASAsci)

## Cost

From 1964 until 1973, $6.417 billion (equivalent to $34.5billion in 2024) was appropriated for the [Research and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_Development "Research and Development") and flights of the Saturn V, with the maximum being in 1966 with $1.2 billion (equivalent to $8.84billion in 2024).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEOrloff2001281-0)[\[137\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEOrloff2001281) That same year, NASA received its largest total budget of $4.5 billion, about 0.5 percent of the [gross domestic product (GDP) of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_States "Economy of the United States") at that time.[&#8202;](#ref-back-inflation-USGDP-0)[\[138\]](#refnote-inflation-USGDP) In the time frame from 1969 to 1971 the cost of launching a Saturn V Apollo mission was $185 million (equivalent to $995million in 2024).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEHeppenheimer1999247-0)[\[139\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEHeppenheimer1999247)

## Mission profile

### Launch preparation

The [Mobile Service Structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Service_Structure "Mobile Service Structure") (MSS), on which the Saturn V sat on, was moved to the launch pad at [Launch Complex 39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39 "Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39") around two months before launch. Moving the vehicle let technicians access parts normally inaccessible and allowed testing and other preparations to begin. Around 21 days before launch, technicians fueled the first stage up with [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1 "RP-1"). This was the only fuel that could be loaded this far from the launch date, as [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") and [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen "Liquid hydrogen") are [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic_fuel "Cryogenic fuel") and could only be loaded a couple of hours before launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202520-0)[\[140\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202520) The pre-count operations started around six days before launch; equipment was installed and additional testing was performed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202523-0)[\[141\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202523) At T−24 hours, the Saturn V was completely powered up using ground equipment and the [Kennedy Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center "Kennedy Space Center") began monitoring wind speeds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202525-0)[\[142\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202525)

At T−9 hours before launch, an 11-hour hold would begin to allow work on any problems discovered. After, the backup crew of the mission entered the [Command Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_command_and_service_module "Apollo command and service module") (CM) to set the switches and circuit breakers to a predetermined position. At T−7 hours 30 minutes, the liquid oxygen tanks on all three stages began to be loaded[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202527-0)[\[143\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202527) and would finish an hour later.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202528-0)[\[144\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202528) At T−5 hours, the liquid hydrogen tanks began to be loaded, starting with the second stage and then the third stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202528–29-0)[\[145\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202528–29) Both the liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen tanks had to be continuously refilled as the previously loaded fuel boiled and evaporated.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202528–29-1)[\[145\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202528–29) At T−3 hours 45 minutes, the crew was awakened and shortly after, another hold began. The hold lasted an hour and a half, allowing time to fix any remaining issues.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202529-0)[\[146\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202529) At T−2 hours 40 minutes, the crew entered the Command Module and prepared for launch,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202530-0)[\[147\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202530) with the hatch being closed 40 minutes later.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202531-0)[\[148\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202531)

In the event of an abort, the range safety officer would remotely shut down the engines and send a command for the explosive charges attached to the rocket to detonate. These explosions would open the fuel and oxidizer tanks to help disperse the fuel and minimize mixing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681-3)[\[149\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19681)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196840-0)[\[150\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196840) Around T−40 minutes, a test was conducted to see if the Saturn V could pick up the signal, with the explosives being armed at T−5 minutes 30 seconds.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202533-0)[\[151\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202533)

### Startup sequence

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Apollo_11_launch.jpg/500px-Apollo_11_launch.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Apollo_11_launch.jpg) [Condensation clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation_cloud "Condensation cloud") surrounding the [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11") Saturn V as it works its way through the dense lower atmosphere

At T−8 minutes, the onboard computer, the [Launch Vehicle Digital Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_Vehicle_Digital_Computer "Launch Vehicle Digital Computer") (LVDC), was armed.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202536–37-0)[\[152\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202536–37) At T−5 minutes 45 seconds, the final [Go/No-Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go/no-go "Go/no-go") was given from the launch controllers. At T−4 minutes 30 seconds, the Terminal Countdown Sequencer (TCS) was armed and prepared to execute the countdown sequence automatically.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202537-0)[\[153\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202537) At T−3 minutes 7 seconds, the TCS began the automatic countdown sequence.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202539-0)[\[154\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202539) At T−16.97 seconds, the TCS sent a signal to the LVDC giving it access to the internal [gyroscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscope "Gyroscope") and [accelerometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerometer "Accelerometer").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202543-0)[\[155\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202543)

At T−8.9 seconds, the first-stage ignition sequence was initiated, with[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202544-0)[\[156\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202544) the center engine being ignited first, followed by opposing outboard pairs at 300-millisecond intervals to reduce the structural loads on the rocket. At T+0.3 seconds, the rocket was "soft released" in two stages. First, the hold-down arms released the rocket. Second, as the rocket began to accelerate upwards, it was slowed by tapered metal pins being pulled through holes for half a second.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196842-0)[\[157\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196842)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202546-0)[\[158\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202546) At T+0.63 seconds, the IU [umbilical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbilical_cable "Umbilical cable") cable was disconnected, indicating to the LVDC that launch had occurred.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202547-0)[\[159\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202547)

At about T+1.7 seconds, the vehicle [yawed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaw_(rotation) "Yaw (rotation)") 1.25° away from the launch tower to ensure adequate clearance despite winds. It took about 10 seconds for the rocket to clear the tower; this varied between missions. At an altitude of 140m (450ft) the rocket rolled to the correct flight [azimuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azimuth "Azimuth"), which varied from 72 to 108 degrees depending on the time and date of launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202551-0)[\[160\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202551)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19682-0)[\[161\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19682) At T+20.6 seconds, the four outboard engines were tilted outward, in case of a premature outboard engine shutdown.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202551-1)[\[160\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202551) At around T+1 minute, the Saturn V reached the [speed of sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound "Speed of sound").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202553-0)[\[162\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202553)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19682-1)[\[161\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration19682)

### Max Q sequence

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Apollo_11_first_stage_separation.jpg/500px-Apollo_11_first_stage_separation.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Apollo_11_first_stage_separation.jpg) Apollo 11 S-IC separation

At about T+1 minute 6 seconds, the rocket experienced [maximum dynamic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_q "Max q") (max _q_). Dynamic pressure on a rocket varies with [air density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_density "Air density") and [relative velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_velocity "Relative velocity"). Although velocity continues to increase, air density decreases so quickly with altitude that dynamic pressure falls below max _q_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202553-1)[\[162\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202553) As the Saturn V consumed fuel, its weight decreased, increasing its acceleration. This caused the crew to experience 4 [_g_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force "G-force") at T+2 minutes 15 seconds. To reduce the g forces, the center engine was cut, lowering the g forces to 3 _g_. Each F-1 engine consumed 900,000kg/min (16.5short ton/s). At this time, the Saturn V had an [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude "Altitude") of 44.1km (23.8nmi), weighted 1,110,000kg (2,450,000lb), and had a speed of 7,124.4km/h; 4,426.9mph (6,492.8ft/s)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202554–55-0)[\[163\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202554–55)

When the [optical depletion sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_sensor "Optical sensor") detected low fuel or oxidizer, the remaining four outboard engines were shut down. This would happen around T+2 minutes 40 seconds. The Saturn V had an altitude of 66.1km (35.7nmi), weighed 830,000kg (1,820,000lb), and had a speed of 9,950.8km/h; 6,183.1mph (9,068.6ft/s)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202555-0)[\[164\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202555) Just before first stage separation, on earlier Apollo missions, small [ullage engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ullage_motor "Ullage motor") on the second stage were ignited for a few seconds to ensure the fuels were at the bottom of their tanks. First stage separation occurred less than one second after the engines were cut off. Eight small solid-fuel separation motors separated the S-IC from the rest of the vehicle. The first stage would continue on a [ballistic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_trajectory "Ballistic trajectory") for another minute and 45 seconds before falling into the [Atlantic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean "Atlantic Ocean") about 560km (350mi) downrange.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202556-0)[\[165\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202556)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196843-0)[\[166\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196843)

### S-II sequence

Apollo 4 interstage falling away. The interstage glows as it interacts with the engine exhaust from the S-II.

After S-IC separation, the five J-2 engines were ignited and began providing thrust. It took a couple of seconds for the J-2 engines to reach full thrust.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202557-0)[\[167\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202557) At about T+3 minutes 12 seconds, the interstage ring dropped from the second stage. The ring, sitting only a 1m (3.3ft) from the outboard J-2 engines, needed to fall cleanly without hitting them, as it could have potentially damaged the engines. Shortly after interstage separation, the [Launch Escape System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_Escape_System "Launch Escape System") was also jettisoned.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202558-0)[\[168\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202558)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863-2)[\[107\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196863)

At about T+3 minutes 24 seconds, the Saturn V switched from a pre-programmed trajectory to a "closed loop," or Iterative Guidance Mode. The pre-programmed trajectory was designed to keep the vehicle on its course but prioritized making sure that the aerodynamic forces on the Saturn V did not exceed its limits. The instrument unit computed in real time the most fuel-efficient trajectory toward its target orbit. If the instrument unit failed, the crew could switch control of the Saturn to the command module's computer, take manual control, or abort the flight.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202558–59-0)[\[169\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202558–59) At about T+7 minutes 40 seconds, the center engine shut down to reduce [longitudinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave "Longitudinal wave") pogo oscillations.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202560–61-0)[\[170\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202560–61)

Five level [sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor "Sensor") in the bottom of each S-II propellant tank were armed during S-II flight, allowing any two to trigger S-II cutoff and staging when uncovered.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202561–62-0)[\[171\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202561–62) At about T+9 minutes 8 seconds, the rest of the J-2 engines shut off and the process of separating the S-II from the S-IVB began. At this time, the Saturn V had an altitude of 187.2km (101.1nmi) and had a speed of 24,897.9km/h; 15,470.9mph (22,690.6ft/s). Two small ullage engines were ignited and [retro motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrorocket "Retrorocket") were fired, separating the S-II from the rest of the rocket.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202563-0)[\[172\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202563)

### S-IVB sequence

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/AS17-148-22714_crop.jpg/500px-AS17-148-22714_crop.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:AS17-148-22714_crop.jpg) Apollo 17 [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") rocket stage, shortly after transposition and docking with the Lunar Module

Unlike the two-plane separation of the S-IC and S-II stages, the S-II and S-IVB separated with a single step.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196874–75-0)[\[173\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_AdministrationMcDonnell_Douglas_Astronautics_CompanyNorth_American_Rockwell_CorporationInternational_Business_Machines_Corporation196874–75) Less than one second after the S-II's engines were cut off, the S-IVB's single J-2 engine was ignited, taking about 5 seconds to reach full thrust.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202564-0)[\[174\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202564)

The third stage burned for about 2.5 minutes until first cutoff at about T+11 minutes 40 seconds. The vehicle had an altitude of 191.1km (103.2nmi) and had a speed of 28,048km/h; 17,428mph (25,561ft/s). Unlike the previous two stages, S-IVB's J-2 engine was restarted for [trans-lunar injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-lunar_injection "Trans-lunar injection") (TLI).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202565-0)[\[175\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202565) After engine cutoff, the Saturn V entered an [Earth parking orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parking_orbit "Parking orbit") 190km (100nmi) above the Earth. The third stage remained attached to the spacecraft while it [orbited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit "Orbit") the Earth one and a half times.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202566–67-0)[\[176\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202566–67)

### Lunar Module sequence

TLI came at about T+2 hours 44 minutes after launch. The J-2 took around 10 seconds to reach full thrust.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202573-0)[\[177\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202573) The S-IVB burned for almost six minutes, giving the spacecraft a velocity of about 39,027.6km/h; 24,250.6mph (35,567.6ft/s).[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202574-0)[\[178\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202574) About 40 minutes after TLI, the Apollo Command and Service module (CSM) separated from the third stage, turned 180 degrees, and docked with the Lunar Module (LM), which rode below the CSM during launch. The CSM and LM separated from the spent third stage 50 minutes later in a maneuver known as [transposition, docking, and extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition,_docking,_and_extraction "Transposition, docking, and extraction").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202576–77-0)[\[179\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202576–77)

If it remained on the same trajectory as the spacecraft, the S-IVB could have presented a collision hazard, so its remaining propellants were vented, and the auxiliary propulsion system was fired to move it away. For lunar missions before Apollo 13, the S-IVB was directed toward the Moon's trailing edge in its orbit, [slingshotting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_slingshot "Gravitational slingshot") it beyond Earth escape velocity and into solar orbit. From Apollo 13 onwards, the S-IVB was sent to hit the Moon.[&#8202;](#ref-back-impact-0)[\[180\]](#refnote-impact)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202578-0)[\[181\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202578) [Seismometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismometer "Seismometer") left behind by previous missions detected the impacts, and the information helped map the [internal structure of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_the_Moon "Internal structure of the Moon").[&#8202;](#ref-back-188-0)[\[182\]](#refnote-188)[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTESchulman202579-0)[\[183\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTESchulman202579)

### Skylab sequence

Main article: [Skylab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab "Skylab")

In 1965, the [Apollo Applications Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Applications_Program "Apollo Applications Program") (AAP) was created to explore scientific missions that could be performed using Apollo hardware. Much of the planning centered on the idea of a space station. Von Braun's earlier 1964 plans employed a " [wet workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_workshop "Wet workshop")" concept, with a spent S-II Saturn V second stage being launched into orbit and outfitted in space. The following year, AAP studied a smaller station using the [Saturn IB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_IB "Saturn IB") second stage. By 1969, Apollo funding cuts eliminated the possibility of procuring more Apollo hardware and forced the cancellation of some later Moon landing flights. This freed up at least one Saturn V, allowing the wet workshop to be replaced with the "dry workshop" concept: the station—now known as Skylab— would be built on the ground from a surplus Saturn IB second stage and launched atop the first two live stages of a Saturn V.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEYoung2009245-0)[\[184\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEYoung2009245) A backup station, constructed from a Saturn V third stage, was built and is now on display at the [National Air and Space Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Air_and_Space_Museum "National Air and Space Museum").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEShayler2001301-0)[\[185\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEShayler2001301) The only significant changes to the Saturn V from the Apollo configurations involved some modifications to the S-II to act as the terminal stage for inserting the Skylab payload into Earth orbit.[&#8202;](#ref-back-192-0)[\[186\]](#refnote-192) The S-II remained in orbit for almost two years before it made an uncontrolled re-entry on January 11, 1975.[&#8202;](#ref-back-193-0)[\[187\]](#refnote-193)

## Post-Apollo proposal

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Saturn-Shuttle_model_at_Udvar-Hazy_Center.jpg/500px-Saturn-Shuttle_model_at_Udvar-Hazy_Center.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn-Shuttle_model_at_Udvar-Hazy_Center.jpg) The [Saturn-Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn-Shuttle "Saturn-Shuttle") concept

In the early 1970s, as the public's attention turned away from space exploration to other matters, such as the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"), [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress "United States Congress") began to cut NASA's budget. The U.S. Government was less willing to continue funding NASA, especially after the improvement of U.S.-Soviet relations.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978499–500-0)[\[188\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBensonFaherty1978499–500) The [Apollo Applications Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Applications_Program "Apollo Applications Program") explored other Saturn V mission profiles. [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") considered [three-man Venus flyby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manned_Venus_flyby#Apollo_Applications_Program "Manned Venus flyby"); the [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") stage would have been a " [wet workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_workshop "Wet workshop")" similar to the original design of [Skylab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab "Skylab").[&#8202;](#ref-back-195-0)[\[189\]](#refnote-195) After Apollo, the Saturn V was planned to be the prime launch vehicle for [Prospector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospector_(spacecraft) "Prospector (spacecraft)"). Prospector was a proposed 330kg (730lb) robotic rover, similar to the two Soviet [Lunokhod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunokhod_programme "Lunokhod programme") rovers,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEUliviHarland200440-0)[\[190\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEUliviHarland200440) the [Voyager Mars probes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program_(Mars) "Voyager program (Mars)"), and a scaled-up version of the [Voyager interplanetary probes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program "Voyager program").[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTECortright199812-0)[\[191\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTECortright199812) The Saturn V was also to have been the launch vehicle for the nuclear rocket stage RIFT test program and for some versions of the upcoming [NERVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NERVA "NERVA") project.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEDewar200752–54-0)[\[192\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEDewar200752–54)

All of these planned uses of the Saturn V were canceled, with cost being a major factor. [Edgar Cortright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Cortright "Edgar Cortright"), who had been the director of [NASA Langley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Langley "NASA Langley"), stated decades later that "JPL never liked the big approach. They always argued against it. I probably was the leading proponent in using the Saturn V, and I lost. Probably very wise that I lost."[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTECortright199812-1)[\[193\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTECortright199812)

The canceled second production run of Saturn Vs would very likely have featured an [uprated F-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_F-1#F-1A_after_Apollo "Rocketdyne F-1") engine in its first stage, providing a substantial performance boost.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b1–2-0)[\[194\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b1–2) Other likely changes would have been bigger [fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin "Fin"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b15-0)[\[195\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b15) a strengthened construction,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b20,_27,_29-0)[\[196\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b20,_27,_29) a stretched S-IC first stage,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b21-0)[\[197\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b21) and the [HG-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HG-3_(rocket_engine) "HG-3 (rocket engine)") engine for the upper stages.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b25,_30-0)[\[198\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTENational_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration1965b25,_30)

A number of alternate Saturn vehicles were proposed based on the Saturn V, ranging from the [Saturn INT-20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_INT-20 "Saturn INT-20") with an [S-IVB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-IVB "S-IVB") stage and interstage mounted directly onto a S-IC,[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBoeing_Company19663-0)[\[199\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBoeing_Company19663) through to the Saturn V-24(L), which would not only have five uprated F-1 engines in the first stage, but also four strap-on [boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booster_(rocketry) "Booster (rocketry)") with two uprated F-1 engines each, giving a total of thirteen F-1 engines firing at launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBoeing_Company19665-0)[\[200\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBoeing_Company19665)

Lack of a second Saturn V production run killed these plans, and left the United States without a [super heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle "Super heavy-lift launch vehicle"). Some in the U.S. space community came to lament this situation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-207-0)[\[201\]](#refnote-207) The [Saturn-Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn-Shuttle "Saturn-Shuttle") could also have eliminated the [Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster "Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Booster") that ultimately precipitated the [_Challenger_ accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster "Space Shuttle Challenger disaster") in 1986.[&#8202;](#ref-back-spaceodysseytwo-0)[\[202\]](#refnote-spaceodysseytwo)

## Saturn V displays

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/2024_Alabama_American_Innovation_Dollar.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2024_Alabama_American_Innovation_Dollar.jpg) The Saturn V depicted on the reverse of the 2024 Alabama [American Innovation dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Innovation_dollars "American Innovation dollars")

- There is one Saturn V on display at the [U.S. Space & Rocket Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Space_&_Rocket_Center "U.S. Space & Rocket Center") in Huntsville. [SA-500D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-500D "SA-500D") is on horizontal display made up of its S-IC-D, S-II-F/D and S-IVB-D. These were all test stages not meant for flight. This vehicle was displayed outdoors from 1969 to 2007, was restored, and is now displayed in the Davidson Center for Space Exploration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-displays-4)[\[69\]](#refnote-displays)
- There is one at the [Johnson Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson_Space_Center "Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center") made up of the first stage from SA-514, the second stage from SA-515, and the third stage from SA-513 (replaced for flight by the Skylab workshop). With stages arriving between 1977 and 1979, this was displayed in the open until its 2005 restoration when a structure was built around it for protection. This is the only display Saturn consisting entirely of stages intended to be launched.[&#8202;](#ref-back-209-0)[\[203\]](#refnote-209)[&#8202;](#ref-back-displays-5)[\[69\]](#refnote-displays)
- One at the [Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Visitor_Complex "Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex"), made up of S-IC-T (test stage) and the second and third stages from SA-514.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980439-0)[\[204\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEBilstein1980439) It was displayed outdoors for decades, then in 1996 was enclosed for protection from the elements in the Apollo/Saturn V Center.[&#8202;](#ref-back-211-0)[\[205\]](#refnote-211)[&#8202;](#ref-back-displays-6)[\[69\]](#refnote-displays)

## Discarded stages

On September 3, 2002, [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer "Astronomer") [Bill Yeung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Yeung "Bill Yeung") discovered a suspected [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid"), which was given the discovery designation [J002E3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J002E3 "J002E3"). It appeared to be in orbit around the Earth, and was soon discovered from spectral analysis to be covered in white [titanium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium_dioxide "Titanium dioxide"), which was a major constituent of the paint used on the Saturn V. Calculation of orbital parameters led to tentative identification as being the [Apollo 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_12 "Apollo 12") S-IVB stage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NASA-0)[\[208\]](#refnote-NASA)[&#8202;](#ref-back-215-0)[\[209\]](#refnote-215) Mission controllers had planned to send Apollo 12's S-IVB into solar orbit after separation from the Apollo spacecraft, but it is believed the engine burn lasted too long, and hence did not send it close enough to the Moon, so it remained in a barely stable orbit around the Earth and Moon. In 1971, through a series of [gravitational perturbations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy) "Perturbation (astronomy)"), it is believed to have entered in a solar orbit and then returned into weakly captured Earth orbit 31 years later. It left Earth orbit again in June 2003.[&#8202;](#ref-back-FOOTNOTEJorgensenRivkinBinzelWhitely2003981-0)[\[210\]](#refnote-FOOTNOTEJorgensenRivkinBinzelWhitely2003981)
