# BSNL’s Ascent, Decline and Revival (2000–2025): An Objective Data-Driven Appraisal.

### A ₹3.28 Lakh Crore Gamble—Was the Cumulative Bailout Worth a Defence Budget When BSNL’s Core Problems Remain Unfixed?

[KBS Sidhu](https://substack.com/@kbssidhu)

***Karan Bir Singh Sidhu**, IAS (retd.), former Special Chief Secretary to the Government of Punjab, and a Gold Medalist in Electronics & Telecommunications Engineering. He also holds an MA in Economics from the University of Manchester, UK.*

# BSNL’s Decline: A Critical Assessment

Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited (BSNL), formed on October 1, 2000 through the corporatisation of the Department of Telecom Services and Department of Telecom Operations, represents one of India’s most spectacular corporate declines—from a profitable public sector monopoly to a debt-ridden entity sustained largely by government support.

### The Golden Years (2000–2009)

BSNL began as India’s telecommunications leader with commanding market share. In 2005–06, it held approximately 45–47% of the overall telecom market and 72% of the fixed-line segment. During its early years, the company remained profitable, earning ₹10,183 crore in 2004–05, ₹8,940 crore in 2005–06, and ₹7,806 crore in 2006–07. In mobile services, BSNL held around 20% market share by 2006, second only to Airtel, with approximately 18.45 million mobile subscribers.

By 2010, BSNL’s mobile subscriber base had grown to 86.7 million, and its market share in wireless telephony stood at approximately 11.88%. The company generated revenues of around ₹29,686 crore in 2010–11.

### The Breaking Point (2009–10)

**FY 2009–10 marked BSNL’s first-ever loss since inception—₹1,822.65 crore**. This watershed moment resulted from multiple structural failures: the rapid migration from landlines to mobile phones (landline subscribers fell from 33.7 million in 2006–07 to 16 million by 2014–15), rising employee costs for 2.38 lakh employees, and fierce competition from private operators offering superior 4G services that BSNL could not match.

### The Descent into Crisis (2010–2020)

The losses accelerated dramatically:

* **2010–11**: Loss of ₹6,384 crore
* **2011–12**: Loss of ₹8,851 crore
* **2012–13**: Loss of ₹7,884 crore
* **2013–14**: Loss of ₹7,085 crore (with landline business alone losing ₹14,979 crore)
* **2014–15**: Loss of ₹3,785 crore
* **2019–20**: Revenue collapsed to ₹18,907 crore from ₹29,686 crore in 2010–11—a decline of nearly 36%

By 2015, BSNL’s accumulated losses had swelled to approximately ₹36,000 crore.

### Financial Performance: Last Decade

**Revenue Trajectory:**

* 2010–11: ₹29,686 crore
* 2014–15: ₹27,242 crore
* 2019–20: ₹18,907 crore
* 2021–22: ₹18,595 crore
* 2022–23: ₹20,699 crore
* 2023–24: ₹21,302 crore
* 2024–25 (Q3): Revenue from operations reached ₹14,197 crore for nine months

The company posted its **first profit in 18 years** during Q3 and Q4 of FY 2024–25, earning ₹262 crore and ₹280 crore respectively. However, for the April–September 2024 period, BSNL still recorded a net loss of ₹2,785 crore.

### Debt Crisis and Government Bailouts

**Debt Reduction (on paper):**

* March 31, 2022: ₹40,400 crore
* March 31, 2023: ₹28,092 crore
* March 31, 2024: ₹23,297 crore

This 42% debt reduction over three years appears impressive until you examine how it was achieved—not through operational excellence, but through substantial government intervention.

**Total Government Support (2019–2025): ₹3.28 lakh crore**

The government approved three successive revival packages:

1. **2019 Revival Package: ₹69,000 crore** — Focused on debt restructuring through sovereign-guarantee bonds, Voluntary Retirement Scheme (VRS), and reducing operating costs.
2. **2022 Revival Package: ₹1.64 lakh crore** — Included capital infusion, spectrum allocation, debt restructuring, viability-gap funding for rural telephony, and merger with Bharat Broadband Nigam Ltd (BBNL).
3. **2023 Revival Package: ₹89,047 crore** — Allocated 4G/5G spectrum across multiple bands (700 MHz, 3300 MHz, 26 GHz, 2500 MHz) through equity infusion. Authorised capital increased from ₹1,50,000 crore to ₹2,10,000 crore.
4. **2025 Additional Support: ₹6,982 crore** — Further capital-expenditure support for 4G expansion.

**Of the total ₹2.74 lakh crore package approved since 2019, ₹1.53 lakh crore has been released so far**. The 2024–25 budget alone allocated ₹82,916 crore.

To put this in perspective, BSNL’s total support exceeds India’s entire defence budget for 2019–20 (₹3.18 lakh crore).

### Subscriber Base Collapse

**Peak to Valley:**

* March 2014: 94.6 million mobile subscribers (60 million active)
* March 2022: 113 million total subscribers
* March 2023: 103.6 million (53.66 million active) — **loss of 10 million users in one year**
* November 2024: 92.05 million

BSNL’s market share plummeted from double-digit percentages in the 2000s to just **7.98–8.03%** by 2024. The company briefly gained subscribers during July–October 2024 (adding nearly 7 million users) after private operators hiked tariffs, but started losing users again by November 2024, dropping 344,473 subscribers.

#### **The Jio Effect (2016 onwards):**

Reliance Jio’s entry in 2016 with free voice calls and extremely low-priced data transformed the market into a brutal price war. While BSNL remained largely on 2G/3G networks, Jio and Airtel aggressively expanded 4G coverage. By 2022, when private telcos launched 5G, BSNL customers had little choice but to migrate to competitors who offered high-speed networks. Between 2020 and mid-2024, BSNL lost millions of subscribers annually.

### The Technology Disaster

**The 4G Catastrophe:**

BSNL’s technological obsolescence is the single biggest reason for its decline. The company’s inability to deploy 4G services for over a decade—while competitors rolled out 4G and then 5G—proved fatal.

**Timeline of Delays:**

* **2010**: BSNL allocated 4G spectrum in 2300 MHz band (BWA spectrum) without auction participation, paying ₹8,500 crore. However, BSNL used WiMAX technology for 4G, which failed globally. The company returned the equipment and recovered its investment.
* **2006–2010**: Multiple tender disruptions plagued network expansion. In 2006–07, Nokia Siemens refused to accept a 22.5 million-line GSM contract due to price differences. Motorola challenged its disqualification in court, delaying the tender by 8 months. These procurement battles caused BSNL to exhaust capacity and lose market share.
* **2010s**: BSNL was compelled to purchase 3G spectrum as part of government social obligations, even when the technology was becoming obsolete.
* **2017–18**: Limited 4G services launched in select areas under “spectrum reframing,” but these were not proper 4G networks.
* **2019–2023**: Under the “Make in India” initiative, **BSNL was not permitted to procure standard 4G equipment from global vendors like Nokia, Ericsson, and Samsung—unlike Jio and Airtel**. Instead, BSNL was required to use only indigenous equipment from C-DOT, Tejas Networks (later acquired by Tata), and ITI.

This policy, framed in self-reliance terms, significantly constrained BSNL’s options. The indigenous 4G stack took years to mature. Former Telecom Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw promised 4G launch “in a few weeks” in May 2023, with 5G by December 2023—neither materialised on schedule.

**Current 4G Status (October 2025):**

* Purchase order placed for 100,000 4G sites
* Equipment supply began September 2023
* As of October 2025: 95,000–98,000 4G towers installed, approximately 74,000–80,000 operational
* Commercial 4G launched September 27, 2025 in partnership with the TCS-C-DOT-Tejas consortium
* Full rollout initially targeted for June 2025, now delayed further

**5G Plans:**

* Government allocated ₹61,000 crore worth of 5G spectrum (700 MHz, 3300 MHz, 26 GHz bands) to BSNL in April 2025
* All 4G infrastructure is “5G-upgradable”
* 5G trials completed; commercial 5G launch planned for Delhi and Mumbai by December 2025
* Telecom Minister Jyotiraditya Scindia announced plans to upgrade all 4G towers to 5G within 6–8 months.

### The Human Cost: VRS 2019

As part of the 2019 revival package, BSNL launched the “BSNL Voluntary Retirement Scheme-2019” targeting employees aged 50 and above. The scheme aimed to “optimise and right-size” human resources.

**Results:**

* Approximately **78,500–93,000 BSNL employees** (and total 92,500 including MTNL) opted for VRS
* Internal target was 77,000; over 80,000 applied within the first two weeks
* Total BSNL workforce reduced from approximately 1.76 lakh to around 55,000–56,000 employees (29,750 executives and 26,435 non-executives as of March 2024)
* **Employee costs reduced by 51%**, saving approximately ₹7,000–8,500 crore annually
* Package was generous: employees received lump-sum payment for remaining months until age 60, plus gratuity, earned leave, and future pension. A 50-year-old employee could receive approximately ₹90 lakh.

**VRS 2.0 Controversy (2024–25):**  
In December 2024, reports emerged that BSNL’s board approved a second VRS proposal to cut the workforce by an additional 35% (18,000–19,000 employees), seeking ₹15,000 crore from the finance ministry. However, employee unions strongly opposed this, blaming management issues rather than workforce size for BSNL’s problems. As of March 2025, the government stated **“no proposal for VRS in BSNL is under consideration.”**

### Root Causes: A Policy Post-mortem— hindsight 20/20

**1. Policy Asymmetries:**

* BSNL required to use an indigenous 4G stack while private operators procured from Nokia, Ericsson, Samsung.
* Compulsory 3G spectrum purchases when the technology was already outdated.
* Social obligations to provide unprofitable rural connectivity that recovered barely 10% of costs.
* Delayed spectrum allocation timelines: 4G spectrum went to private operators over a decade before BSNL could deploy.

**2. Bureaucratic Procurement Disasters:**

* Repeated tender cancellations and legal challenges (Motorola, Nokia Siemens) delayed capacity expansion by years.
* The cancelled 93 million-line tender in 2010 left BSNL capacity-starved precisely when competition intensified.

**3. Legacy Cost Structure:**

* High pension obligations for retired government employees absorbed into BSNL.
* Employee costs consumed 38% of revenue (₹7,500 crore annually) before VRS 2.0 discussions.

**4. Competitive Onslaught:**

* Private operators (Jio, Airtel) invested aggressively in 4G/5G while BSNL remained on 2G/3G.
* Jio’s 2016 entry with free voice and cheap data disrupted the entire market.
* BSNL’s Average Revenue Per User (ARPU) estimated at ₹90 versus Airtel’s ₹211 and Jio’s ₹195.

**5. Government Contradictions and Trade-offs:**

* While the current government has often argued that “the government has no business to be in business,” it has also invested ₹3.28 lakh crore in BSNL—framed as necessary for universal service and strategic aims.
* The objective of serving “Universal Service Obligation” in remote areas is legitimate; even so, BSNL’s rural market share is merely 7.39%, raising questions on efficacy.

## Critical Verdict

#### **The Positive (Barely):**

* Recent back-to-back quarterly profits for the first time in 18 years.
* Debt reduced by 42% in three years.
* Subscriber gains during July–October 2024 (7 million additions).
* Indigenous 4G stack makes India one of only five nations with fully home-grown telecom technology.
* 95,000+ 4G towers now operational.

#### **The Reality Check:**

* **Profitability is largely policy-enabled**: driven by extraordinary public funding rather than a clear turnaround in operating fundamentals.
* **Debt reduction is primarily restructuring-led**: achieved via sovereign-guarantee bonds, not repayment from cash profits.
* **Revenue remains stagnant**: ₹21,302 crore in FY24 barely higher than ₹20,699 crore in FY23.
* **Market share collapsed**: From 45%+ to ~8%.
* **Technology lag persists**: 4G launched commercially only in September 2025—**more than a decade behind private operators**.
* **Subscriber exodus**: Lost 10 million users in 2022–23 alone; started losing again by November 2024.
* **Indigenous 4G rationale remains contested**: the strategic intent is understandable, but the near-term cost to competitiveness was steep.

#### **The Structural Problems Remain Unsolved:**

1. No persuasive evidence that BSNL can compete without continued public funding.
2. 4G rollout still incomplete; 5G launch timelines remain ambitious.
3. Customer experience and network quality trail private operators.
4. High operating costs persist despite VRS.
5. Policymakers continue to treat BSNL as a vehicle for social objectives as well as commerce—an inherently difficult balance.

## The ₹3.28 Lakh Crore Question

Was rescuing BSNL worth more than India’s annual defence budget? The government’s rationale—rural connectivity, strategic importance, “Atmanirbhar Bharat”—requires rigorous scrutiny:

* **Rural connectivity**: BSNL serves only 38 million rural subscribers with 7.39% market share. Private operators dominate rural areas too.
* **Affordability**: India already has some of the world’s lowest telecom tariffs; BSNL’s presence may not be the decisive factor on pricing.
* **Strategic autonomy**: Valid for defence/sensitive areas, but does not by itself justify nationwide operations requiring ₹3.28 lakh crore.
* **Indigenous technology**: Positioning BSNL as a test-bed for unproven technology while competitors used proven equipment proved counter-productive in the short term.

## **In Summary: Towards ‘Great Expectations’ or ‘A Bleak Story’?**

BSNL’s story is not simply one of corporate failure—it is a mirror held up to India’s policy choices. From being a dominant, profitable monopoly in 2000, the company was steadily eroded by asymmetrical spectrum policies, procurement paralysis, mounting social obligations without commensurate compensation, and delayed decision-making in the face of agile private competition. These structural handicaps—many of them policy-driven—left BSNL unable to compete on technology, speed, or customer experience.

Yes, the recent quarterly profits and long-delayed 4G rollout offer a glimmer of progress. Yes, debt has declined on paper. And yes, the indigenous 4G stack is a noteworthy achievement. But none of this changes the uncomfortable truth: BSNL’s survival still hinges on extraordinary government intervention rather than genuine operational strength. A ₹3.28 lakh crore lifeline has produced an operator with roughly 8% market share, persistent service gaps, and no assured path to self-sustaining profitability.

The Prime Minister recently showcased BSNL’s “revival” as a success story at the [NDTV World Summit](https://x.com/JM_Scindia/status/1979219769074290700). However, the underlying numbers tell a more cautious tale. They suggest that simply pouring public money—however large the amount—into the PSU behemoth may not, by itself, ensure long-term sustainability. A bailout of this scale must translate into genuine competitiveness, not just temporary relief.

So, is the future one of “*Great Expectations*” or “*A Bleak Stor*y”? Much depends on what comes next. If BSNL can transform policy support into true operational strength—by catching up technologically, prioritising customer experience, and enforcing managerial discipline—it could finally justify the massive national investment. But if the familiar cycle of dependence, delay, and complacency continues, India may end up with neither a world-class public-sector telecom nor an efficient use of public funds.

The question is no longer whether BSNL should exist—it is whether it can survive, revive, and ultimately thrive.
