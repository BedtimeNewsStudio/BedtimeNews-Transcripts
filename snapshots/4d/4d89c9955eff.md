# Loneliness and depression among community-dwelling older adults in China during the COVID-19 epidemic: The mediating role of social support

Pan Li, Jiaming Zhong, Yongmei He, Hairong Yan, Mi Xie, Lingyao Meng

Medicine · 2023-09-15 · 102(37):e35171 · DOI: 10.1097/MD.0000000000035171 · PMID: 37713830 · PMCID: PMC10508438

## Abstract

The psychological condition of community-dwelling older adults is a global concern under coronavirus disease 2019. Loneliness is the key risk factor for depression among community-dwelling older adults. This study aims to explore the role of social support as a mediating factor in the relationship between depression and loneliness among community-dwelling older adults. We conducted a cross-sectional study in Chenzhou, Hunan Province, China, from June to December 2021. The sample consisted of 570 community-dwelling adults aged over 60 years. Data were collected through a general information questionnaire, the Social Support Rating Scale, the University of California at Los Angeles Loneliness Scale, and the Center for Epidemiological Survey, Depression Scale. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences PROCESS macro was used to examine the mediating effect of social support between loneliness and depression. This study found that depression was negatively associated with social support (r = −0.381, P < .001), but it was positively correlated with loneliness (r = 0.403, P < .001); loneliness was a predictor of depression (B = 0.333, P < .001). In addition, social support significantly mediated the relationship between loneliness and depression, with an indirect effect of 0.239 (95% Bootstrap CI −0.264 to −0.510), the overall effect value for depression was 0.572, accounting for 56.3% of the total variance in depression within this model. In conclusion, the findings suggest that social support plays a mediating role between depression and loneliness in older adults. This implies that interventions aimed at reducing loneliness and enhancing social support have the potential to alleviate depressive symptoms among this population. By addressing these factors, healthcare professionals and caregivers can promote the mental well-being of older adults and contribute to improved overall quality of life.

## Keywords

community-dwelling, depression, loneliness, mediating effect, older adults

## 1. Introduction

According to statistics published by the World Health Organization in March 2022, there was a 27.6% increase in cases of major depressive disorder and a 25.6% increase in cases of anxiety disorders worldwide during the first year of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.[1] Older adults are particularly vulnerable to severe COVID-19 infection or death due to secondary complications, as they often have weaker physical fitness and function and generally suffer from chronic diseases. This dangerous internal environment exacerbates psychological stress.[2–4] As a result of COVID-19 isolation measures, there has been a pandemic of loneliness among the aging population, leading to a rise in psychological problems such as depression and anxiety disorders.[5] Unfortunately, psychological problems in older adults are often overlooked.[6] Depression in older adults is frequently underestimated, and categorized as a normal part of aging, functional impairment, or physical illness, according to the World Psychiatric Association’s major report on depression.[7] Previous studies have also demonstrated that depressive symptoms can severely impact the quality of life and increase mortality rates in older adults.[8–10] China has a rapidly aging population, with the number of 60-year-olds projected to exceed 400 million in 2035, accounting for 30% of the total population.[11] This evidence highlights the urgent need for concern regarding the mental health of older adults in the context of the COVID-19 epidemic.[12–14]

Depression is a major global health concern affecting millions of people worldwide. Recent research indicates that approximately 350 million people currently suffer from depression, making it the second most prominent risk factor for health.[15] The economic burden of depression on a global scale is also substantial, with depression ranking among the top 5 diseases in terms of impact.[16] While extensive research has been conducted on depression, its specific pathogenesis remains under investigation.[17]

There is a notable disparity in depression prevalence between developed and low-and middle-income countries. Rates of depression in the United States and Singapore are comparatively lower than those in Africa and Asia (6.6%, 3.7% vs 9%, 34.8%). A recent longitudinal study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Survey database revealed a concerning increasing tendency in depression rates among older adults in China. From 2011 to 2018, the prevalence of depression increased from 36.8% to 44.5%.[18] Loneliness, defined as a negative emotional experience resulting from insufficient social relationships,[19] is recognized as an important predictor of depression. According to a recent report by the National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine,[20] more than one-third of the population above the age of 45 and nearly a quarter of those over 65 experience loneliness, with prevalence rates ranging from 20% to 34% in Europe, the United States, and Asia.

A study reported that although the prevalence of loneliness and depression fluctuates, the relationship between the two is relatively stable and loneliness is recognized as an important predictor of depression.[19] Sipowicz recruited 92 outpatients in 4 primary care units in Poland and reported a significant positive association between loneliness and depression in a population aged 60 years or older.[21] Another study found that loneliness and depression is cross-sectionally related but loneliness also had a causal effect over time.[22] In addition, loneliness is one of the most common subjective psychological feelings among elderly empty nesters and a common psychological problem caused by interpersonal isolation during the COVID-19 period.[23,24] There is growing evidence that loneliness accelerates physiological functional aging and predicts poorer sleep quality and immunity in older adults, and so on,[25,26] which potentially contributes in turn to the onset and persistence of depression.[27] Thus, mental health in older adults requires more attention.

Previous research has highlighted social support as an important factor in addressing loneliness and depression among older adults.[28] Moreover, studies have confirmed a negative correlation between social support and depression among older adults.[29] Social support is generally defined as a network of support that includes family, friends, neighbors, or members of the community, offering financial, life, emotional, and informational assistance, among others.[30] Older adults with adequate social networks in terms of size, quality, and frequency of interaction feel valued and loved.[31] Support from family and friends can alleviate psychological stress, promote health, and effectively prevent loneliness and depression.[32] Peer and neighbor support is reciprocal, and, when combined with community services, creates a safe living environment for older adults and enhances their life satisfaction.[33] Several studies in Asian countries have reported that social support partially mediated the link between loneliness and depression in older adults.[30,34] However, Son et al[32] conducted a study utilizing data from the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project Wave 3 (2015–2016) involving 1532 Korean older adults aged 65 years and older. Their findings revealed that spousal support and family support were associated with decreased levels of depression in the Young-Old group (aged 65–74 years). However, in the Old-Old group (aged 75 years and above), only spousal support was found to be linked to reduced depression. The study also demonstrated that social support had a significant moderating effect specifically in the old-old group, while it showed no significant impact in the young-old group.

A substantial body of literature supports the importance of both loneliness and social support in recognizing depressive symptoms. However, the moderating role and mechanisms of social support on loneliness and depression require further investigation. While national surveys have been conducted in China, information on specific regions is limited, and therefore, it is still essential to conduct regional studies on mental health in older adults. This study presents the current situation of depression among older adults who live in the community during the COVID-19 epidemic. It explores the impact of loneliness on the depression of older adults and uses PROCESS macro to investigate the mediating role and influence mechanism of social support. This study offers theoretical support for the development of community elderly services and interventions aimed at enhancing social support to reduce depression and loneliness among community-dwelling older adults. This study explores effective ways to improve depression in older adults from the perspective of loneliness, using Chinese community-dwelling older adults as an example, to provide new ideas and methods for developing social support programs to reduce the prevalence of depression in the future. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the relationship and mediators between loneliness and depression in older adults and proposed the following a priori hypotheses (Fig. 1): loneliness and depression are positively related; and social support will mediate the relationship between loneliness and depression.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Participants

A descriptive cross-sectional design was used in a survey study conducted from June to December 15, 2021. Upon receiving ethical approval from the committee of Xiangnan University, we contacted 6 communities that cooperated with the University in Chenzhou, China. The administrators of these communities were willing to participate in the study and were assigned as representatives of their communities, serving mainly as contacts and liaisons between the community and our research team. Participants were recruited using flyers distributed by community staff and research team students based on the following inclusion criteria: age ≥ 60 years; conscious and without language impairment, residence in the community for at least 1 year, and agreement to participate in the study. Elderly individuals who refused or were unable to take part in the study or withdrew in the middle were excluded. A total of 627 participants initially met the eligibility criteria. Subsequently, the research assistant contacted these potential participants via phone for further screening to confirm the inclusion and exclusion criteria. G*Power was used to determine the appropriate sample size for this study. The results indicated a required minimum sample size of 314 (effect size = 0.2, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.85). Considering a 20% loss of the sample, the actual sample size requirement was 393. Ultimately, 570 valid respondents were obtained, and all participants provided written informed consent.

### 2.2. Measurements

#### 2.2.1. Demographic characteristic.

This section presents the demographic characteristics of community-dwelling older adults, including gender, age, marital relationship, education level, and living status.

#### 2.2.2. Social Support Rating Scale.

This scale, developed by Xiao,[35] comprises 10 items that assess 3 dimensions of social support: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and support utilization (3 items). The scoring rules for the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) are as follows: Questions 1–4 and 8–10 offer 4 response options (Option A–D), with corresponding scores ranging from 1 to 4. Question 5 involves ranking support levels from 1 to 4, corresponding to “none,” “very little,” “average,” and “completely supportive,” respectively. For Questions 6 and 7, if the respondent selects “no source,” a score of 0 is assigned, and the score is calculated based on the number of items chosen from the options listed under “following sources.” Higher scores indicate a higher level of social support for the participants. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.879, indicating good internal consistency, and the SSRS has previously demonstrated validity and reliability among Chinese older adults.[36] In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the SSRS was 0.895.

#### 2.2.3. Center for Epidemiological Survey, Depression Scale.

The scale used in this study was developed by the Center for Epidemiologic Studies,[37] and consists of a total of 20 items. A 4-point Likert scale (0–3 points) was employed for assessment, with response options including “rarely (0),” “some days (1),” “occasionally (2),” and “most of the time (3).” The total score on this scale ranges from 0 to 60 points, with higher scores indicating a higher level of depression. A cutoff score of ≥17 is indicative of a tendency toward depression. The scale demonstrates good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient exceeding 0.883 in this study.

#### 2.2.4. University of California at Los Angeles Loneliness Scale.

Loneliness was measured using a Chinese version of the University of California at Los Angeles Loneliness Scale.[38] This self-report scale consists of 20 items, with each item rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 4 based on frequency (“never,” “rarely,” “sometimes,” and “often,” respectively). The total score on the scale ranges from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of loneliness. In this study, the scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.927.

### 2.3. Data analysis

In this study, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was utilized. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, such as loneliness, depression, and social support, were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables, including age and marital status, were presented as N (%). Independent samples t tests and 1-way analysis of variance were employed to compare differences in depression scores among older adults with various demographic and sociological characteristics. To explore the correlation between loneliness, depression, and social support among older adults, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. The hypothesized mediation model (Fig. 1) was tested using the PROCESS macro of Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, which is widely employed to examine mediating effects in current studies.[39,40] In this model, loneliness served as the predictor variable, social support was the mediator, and depression was the outcome variable. Sociodemographic factors such as gender, marital status, and education level were included as covariates to account for potential confounding effects. The coefficient c’ represents the direct effect of loneliness on depression. Loneliness may also indirectly impact depression through social support, which is captured by the product of coefficients a and b (a * b). The coefficient c denotes the total effect of loneliness on depression, which is equal to c’ + a * b. Point estimates were based on a bootstrap sample of 5000, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were constructed. In this model, mediation is considered significant if the 95% CI for the indirect effect (i.e.) does not include zero, spanning from the lower limit to the upper limit. Statistical significance was determined by a P value < .05.

A total of 627 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 591 (94.3%) were returned. Twenty-one incompletely answered questionnaires were discarded and 570 (90.9%) remained for analysis. Over half (n = 373, 65.44%) of the participants had spouses, and almost two-thirds (n = 390, 68.42%) had a junior high school degree or below. ### 3.2. Comparison of depression scores among older adults with different characteristics

Table 1 shows the average depression score of older adults was 18.97 ± 1.95, and the prevalence of depression in this study was 19.30 % (110/570). Females achieved higher scores than males (20.05 ± 2.09 vs 17.16 ± 2.01, t = 13.251, P < .01), and the older adults aged ≥ 80 years (F = 9.268, P < .01), without a spouse (F = 31.043, P < .01), with a junior high school education or below (F = 24.673, P < .01), and living alone obtained higher depression scores (t = 9.104, P < .01) than the respective comparison groups.

### 3.4. Mediating effect of social support between loneliness and depression in older adults

Table 3 and Figure 2 present the results of the mediation analysis conducted using the PROCESS macro. Controlling for sociodemographic factors, the total effect of the model was determined to be 0.572 (c = ab + c’). The direct effect of loneliness on depression was significant, explaining 58.2% of the total effect (c’ = 0.333) (Fig. 2). To assess the size of the i.e., we generated a random sample of 5000 self-leading samples from the original dataset. The results indicated an i.e. (a * b) of 0.239, with a 95% CI ranging from −0.264 to −0.510. The mediation analyses using the bootstrap method revealed that the 95% CI for social support did not include 0, suggesting that social support mediated the relationship between loneliness and depression. Therefore, social support exhibited a mediating effect between loneliness and depression, accounting for 56.3% of the total variance in depression within this model. The mediating effect accounted for 41.8% of the total effect (Table 4).

## 4. Discussion

This study examined the mediating role of social support between loneliness and depression in community-dwelling older adults and explored the relationship between loneliness, depression, and social support. The findings indicated that loneliness and social support were all significantly correlated to depression, and social support mediated the association between loneliness and depression. These findings suggest the importance of supporting community-based programs to improve the mental, and social health of community-dwelling older adults.

The mean loneliness score in this study was 60.76 ± 9.03, which was much higher than that reported in previous studies on loneliness among elderly individuals in nursing homes in Jinan City,[29] China in 2018 (38.62 ± 10.39) and retired community-dwelling older adults in Shenzhen city,[41] China in 2017 (30.30 ± 8.52). This dramatic increase in the loneliness rate may be related to the strict quarantine policies during the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in limited access to public places and reduced opportunities for interpersonal interaction. Elderly individuals with low-level abilities to use digital media, such as displaying their health codes on smartphones, were particularly affected.[42] As a result, their range of activities was reduced, and problems such as decreased exercise and disrupted daily routines arose. The resulting strong sense of disparity may have contributed to their increased feelings of loneliness compared to their pre-pandemic lives.[43]

The prevalence of depression in this study was 19.30% (110/570), which is lower than the depression rate of elderly people in nursing institutions in Jinan,[29] Shandong Province (26.60%), and higher than those in the community in Beijing, Wuhan, Hubei Province and Xi’an, Shaanxi Province (13.5%, 14.04, and 15.80%, respectively).[44–46] The reasons for such variability may be related to geographic characteristics, sample size, population distribution, and measurement tools. Likewise, the social support score in this study was 37.50 ± 8.43, which was higher than the score of the rural elderly in western Hunan (20.90 ± 2.60) measured using the same scale,[47] but lower than the scores of the community elderly in Beijing city and Guangzhou city (43.52 ± 7.65, 39.20 ± 8.20).[48,49] Possible explanations are accelerated urbanization and industrialization, the majority of young people going to work in cities, the lack of child support for older adults in rural areas, as well as the fact that western Hunan is a poor mountainous region with a low population density and inadequate coverage of basic public facilities, leading to urban-rural differences in social support. In parallel comparison to community-based older adults, Beijing City and Guangzhou City have higher levels of economic development and more robust infrastructure and medical coverage, allowing older adults to facilitate the purchase of care and medical services to access higher levels of support from society.

Our study found that depression was positively correlated with loneliness, whereas it was negatively correlated with the total social support score and each dimensional score. This indicates that the higher the social support, the lower the level of loneliness, and, thus, the lower the level the depression in older adults, which is consistent with the study conducted by Routasalo et al.[50] Previous studies have shown that 87.80% of community-dwelling elderly people feel lonely and show more significantly severe depression symptoms in the population with loneliness.[51,52] As defined by Activity theory, it highlights the importance of social activities in the daily lives of older adults, they need to maintain psychological contact with society to prevent loneliness.[53] Most of the older community-dwelling adults belong to the retired group. After retirement, most social and interpersonal contacts related to their previous jobs are cut off; thus, they are more likely to experience loneliness and develop negative emotions than people who have jobs and engage in social activities. The focus of these elderly people’s activities then shifts from their workplaces to their homes, their social activities are significantly reduced, and their effective use of positive social support is diminished.[54,55] At the same time, contemporary Chinese older adults grew up in a collectivist culture where support and recognition obtained from interpersonal interactions and the outside social world were important sources of individuals’ sense of meaning in life.[56] After retirement, the social and family status of older adults has shifted, and the sense of accomplishment gained from family life is relatively less compared to that of their working years, thus resulting in a sense of loss.

At the same time, the findings showed that the older adults who were female, aged ≥ 80 years, were without a spouse, and had a junior high school education or below obtained higher depression scores (Table 1). This could be attributed to the fact that Chinese older women usually have greater household chores and spend less time in outside activities, resulting in fewer outlets to distract attention and release negative emotions. This finding is consistent with Rutland’s research,[57] which found that older women generally have a higher workload than men at home. The highest depression scores were observed among those ≥80 years old, which aligns with previous studies. This may be due to the increase in the number and severity of chronic illnesses with age, which raises the risk of developing depression, particularly when combined with social isolation and chronic pain, which are potent depressogenic factors in older adults. Those who are without a spouse have higher depression scores, this is similar to the findings of Schnittger et al.[58] Older adults inevitably encounter negative life events, such as the loss of partners or closeness in later life, which may increase feelings of loneliness. And as the high infection risk and vulnerable population under COVID-19, they may be more concerned about their physical situation, which can further induce the onset of depression. Moreover, most of their children cannot be with them all day due to their work needs. If older adults live alone, they not only need to bear loneliness alone but also face difficulties in meeting daily needs and lack communication with others, this finding supports the study of Guo et al.[59] Previous studies have reported a negative correlation between educational level and depression.[60,61] This relationship may be because individuals with higher levels of education tend to be more knowledgeable about the availability and accessibility of health services, which in turn leads to better utilization of healthcare and improved health behaviors, especially among older adults.[62] Given the inextricable association between depressive status and the occurrence of suicide in older adults population,[51] our study suggests that more attention should be paid to the mental health of older adults and that targeted measures are needed to reduce the occurrence of depression, especially in older adults population who are female, older over 80 years, without a spouse, or less educated.

The results of the mediation analysis conducted in this study revealed that loneliness, social support, and depression are interrelated in older adults. The two-by-two correlation between loneliness (independent variable) and social support (mediating variable), as well as the correlation between social support and depression (dependent variable), met the basic conditions for a mediating effect. The bias-corrected bootstrap mediating effect test further demonstrated that social support plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between loneliness and depression (B = −0.387, P < .01), accounting for 23.9% of the total effect.

Loneliness can, directly and indirectly, affect depression in older adults. The direct effect occurs when loneliness itself contributes to the development of depression. The i.e. occurs when loneliness inversely affects social support, which in turn contributes to the development of depression. The total effect of loneliness on depression was found to be 0.572. The reasons for these findings may include the reduced communication frequency and poor exchange of information among older adults, leading to increased loneliness and depression. Once depression occurs, the motivation and initiative in all aspects of activities are reduced, resulting in decreased social and emotional support, which further exacerbates loneliness and depression in older adults. On the other hand, social support can lead to changes in the dominant activities and social roles of older adults, satisfying their social and respect needs, according to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and Engeström’s activity theory.[53,63] Therefore, promoting social activities among older adults is crucial in preventing depression which should be promoted as an essential means of preventing depression in older adults. Numerous studies[63–66] have demonstrated the efficacy of psychological support groups, group reminiscence therapy, and video call and phone communication in helping older adults to adapt to new living environments, deal with interpersonal relationships, and expand their social field. Such interventions can effectively reduce loneliness, alleviate depression in older adults, and make their lives more colorful, promoting mutual psychological support and mutual love in daily life.

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design characteristics; thus, any extrapolation of the findings of the mediation analyses should be performed with caution. Moreover, only 3 variables were adopted in this study; more variables could be considered to explore their inner relationships with each other. Furthermore, qualitative designs could be used to explore the experiences of depression status of community-dwelling older adults, and prospective studies should be used to render the results more reliable for further study.

## 5. Conclusions

In summary, the prevalence of depression among community-dwelling older adults was 19.30% in the present study. loneliness was directly and indirectly associated with depression through its association with social support for older adults in China. The findings suggest the importance of promoting social support for depression in older adults, as a way to minimize the level of loneliness and prevent depression. This study’s results will help families, communities, and government to consider developing interventions to enhance social support in their programs that care for the mental health of older adults, to promote the development of an active aging society.
