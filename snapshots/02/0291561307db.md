The **Delta rocket family** was a versatile range of American [rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket "Rocket")-powered [expendable launch systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expendable_launch_system "Expendable launch system") that provided space launch capability in the United States from 1960 to 2024. Japan also launched license-built derivatives ( [N-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-I_(rocket) "N-I (rocket)"), [N-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-II_(rocket) "N-II (rocket)"), and [H-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-I "H-I")) from 1975 to 1992. More than 300 Delta rockets were launched with a 95% success rate. The series was phased out in favor of the [Vulcan Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur "Vulcan Centaur"), with the [Delta IV Heavy rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy "Delta IV Heavy")'s last launch occurring on April 9, 2024.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-1)

## Origins

See also: [Thor (rocket family)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor_(rocket_family) "Thor (rocket family)")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Goddard_delta.JPG/500px-Goddard_delta.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Goddard_delta.JPG) Delta rocket on display at the [Goddard Space Flight Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddard_Space_Flight_Center "Goddard Space Flight Center")

The original Delta rockets used a modified version of the [PGM-17 Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PGM-17_Thor "PGM-17 Thor"), the first [ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile "Ballistic missile") deployed by the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force "United States Air Force") (USAF), as their [first stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket "Multistage rocket"). The Thor had been designed in the mid-1950s to reach Moscow from bases in Britain or similar allied nations, and the first wholly successful Thor launch had occurred in September 1957. Subsequent [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite "Satellite") and [space probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_probe "Space probe") flights soon followed, using a Thor first stage with several different upper stages. The fourth upper-stage combination of the Thor was named the Thor "Delta", reflecting the fourth letter of the Greek alphabet. Eventually the entire Thor–Delta launch vehicle came to be called simply "Delta".[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-3) [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") intended Delta as "an interim general-purpose vehicle" to be "used for [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_satellite "Communications satellite"), [meteorological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_satellite "Weather satellite"), and [scientific satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_satellites "Scientific satellites") and [lunar probes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon "Exploration of the Moon") during 1960 and 1961". The plan was to replace Delta with other rocket designs when they came on-line. The Delta design emphasized reliability rather than performance by replacing components that had caused problems on earlier Thor flights; in particular, the trouble-prone [inertial guidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_guidance "Inertial guidance") package made by [AC Spark Plug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACDelco "ACDelco") was replaced by a radio ground guidance system, which was mounted to the second stage instead of the first. NASA made the original Delta contract to the [Douglas Aircraft Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Aircraft_Company "Douglas Aircraft Company") in April 1959 for 12 vehicles of this design:\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

- Stage 1: Modified [Thor IRBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PGM-17_Thor "PGM-17 Thor") with a BlockI MB-3 engine group consisting of one Rocketdyne LR-79 main engine and two [Rocketdyne LR-101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne_LR-101 "Rocketdyne LR-101") [vernier thrusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernier_thruster "Vernier thruster") for roll control, producing a total of 683kN (154,000lbf) thrust, including LOX/RP1 turbopump exhaust.
- Stage 2: Modified [Able](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Able_(rocket_stage) "Able (rocket stage)"). Pressure-fed [UDMH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsymmetrical_dimethylhydrazine "Unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine")/ [nitric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid")-powered [Aerojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerojet "Aerojet") [AJ-10-118](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AJ10 "AJ10") engine producing 34kN (7,600lbf). This reliable engine cost US$4million to build and is still flying in modified form today. Gas-jet [attitude control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft_attitude_control "Spacecraft attitude control").
- Stage 3: [Altair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair_(rocket_stage) "Altair (rocket stage)"). A spin-stabilized (via a turntable on top of the Able) at 100 [rpm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_per_minute "Revolutions per minute") by two solid rocket motors before separation. One [ABL X-248](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair_(rocket_stage) "Altair (rocket stage)") solid rocket motor provided 12kN (2,700lbf) of thrust for 28seconds. The stage weighed 230kg (510lb) and was largely constructed of wound fiberglass.

These vehicles would be able to place 290kg (640lb) into a 240 to 370km (150 to 230mi) [LEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") or 45kg (99lb) into [GTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit "Geostationary transfer orbit"). Eleven of the twelve initial Delta flights were successful, and until 1968, no failures occurred in the first two minutes of launch. The high degree of success achieved by Delta stood in contrast to the numerous West Coast Thor failures. The total project development and launch cost came to US$43million, US$3million over budget. An order for 14 more vehicles was made before 1962.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Delta 0100-series

This section is [transcluded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Transclusion "Help:Transclusion") from [List of Delta 1 launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Delta_1_launches "List of Delta 1 launches"). The [Delta 0100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_0100 "Delta 0100") series was the first stage of the initial numbered Delta was the Long Tank Thor, a version of the [Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PGM-17_Thor "PGM-17 Thor") missile with extended propellant tanks. Up to nine strap-on [solid rocket boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_rocket_booster "Solid rocket booster") (SRBs) could be fitted. With three SRBs, the Delta was designated a 300 series, while the nine SRB variant was designated the 900 series. A new and improved DeltaF second stage using the higher-thrust Aerojet [AJ 10-118F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AJ-10 "AJ-10") engine was also introduced. The first 900 series launch was the fourth Delta0100.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] On 23 July 1972, Thor-Delta 904 launched [Landsat 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landsat_1 "Landsat 1").[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-chrono-11) A license-built version of the Long Tank Thor stage with the MB-3 engine was also used for the Japanese [N-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-I_(rocket) "N-I (rocket)") launch vehicle.

### Delta 1000-series

### Delta 2000-series

This section is [transcluded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Transclusion "Help:Transclusion") from [List of Delta 1 launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Delta_1_launches "List of Delta 1 launches"). The [Delta 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_2000 "Delta 2000") introduced the new [Rocketdyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne "Rocketdyne") [RS-27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-27 "RS-27") main engine on an Extended Long Tank first stage with the same constant 8-foot diameter. ### Delta 4000-series

The [Delta 4000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_4000 "Delta 4000")-series and 5000-series were developed in the aftermath of the [Challenger disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster "Space Shuttle Challenger disaster") and consisted of a combination of 3000-era and DeltaII-era components. The first stage had the MB-3 main engine and Extended Long Tank of the 3000-series and mounted upgraded [Castor4A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castor_(rocket_stage) "Castor (rocket stage)") motors. The new DeltaK second stage was also included. A total of three were launched in 1989 and 1990, carrying two operational payloads.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Delta 5000-series

This section is [transcluded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Transclusion "Help:Transclusion") from [List of Delta 1 launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Delta_1_launches "List of Delta 1 launches"). The [Delta 5000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_5000 "Delta 5000") series featured upgraded [Castor4A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castor_(rocket_stage) "Castor (rocket stage)") motors on an Extended Long Tank first stage with the new [RS-27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-27 "RS-27") main engine and only launched one mission.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Delta II (6000-series and 7000-series)

The [Delta II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_II "Delta II") series was developed after the 1986 [_Challenger_ accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster "Space Shuttle Challenger disaster") and consisted of the Delta 6000-series and 7000-series, with two variants (Light and Heavy) of the latter.

The Delta 6000-series introduced the Extra Extended Long Tank first stage, which was 12ft (3.7m) longer, and the [Castor4A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castor_(rocket_stage) "Castor (rocket stage)") boosters. Six SRBs ignited at takeoff, and three ignited in the air.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

The Delta 7000-series introduced the [RS-27A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-27A "RS-27A") main engine, which was modified for efficiency at high altitude at some cost to low-altitude performance, and the lighter and more powerful [GEM-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-Epoxy_Motor "Graphite-Epoxy Motor") solid boosters from [Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hercules_Inc. "Hercules Inc."). The [DeltaII Med-Lite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_II "Delta II") was a 7000-series with no third stage and fewer strap-ons (often three, sometimes four) that was usually used for small NASA missions. The [DeltaII Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_II "Delta II") was a DeltaII 792X with the enlarged [GEM-46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-Epoxy_Motor "Graphite-Epoxy Motor") boosters from [Delta III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_III "Delta III").\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Delta III (8000-Series)

The [Delta III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_III "Delta III") 8000-series was a McDonnell Douglas / Boeing-developed program to keep pace with growing satellite masses:

- The two upper stages, with low-performance fuels, were replaced with a single cryogenic stage, improving performance and reducing recurring costs and pad labor. The engine was a single [Pratt & Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney "Pratt & Whitney") [RL10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RL10 "RL10"), from the [Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centaur_(rocket_stage) "Centaur (rocket stage)") upper stage. The hydrogen fuel tank, 4metres in diameter is exposed, showing its orange insulation; the narrower oxygen tank and engine are covered until stage ignition. Fuel tank contracted to [Mitsubishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi "Mitsubishi") and produced using technologies from Japanese [H-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-II "H-II") launcher.
- To keep the stack short and resistant to crosswinds, the first-stage kerosene tank was widened and shortened, matching the upper-stage and fairing diameters.
- Nine enlarged [GEM-46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-Epoxy_Motor "Graphite-Epoxy Motor") solid boosters were attached. Three of them have [thrust-vectoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_vectoring "Thrust vectoring") nozzles.

Of the three Delta III flights, the first two were failures, and the third carried only a [dummy (inert) payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilerplate_(spaceflight) "Boilerplate (spaceflight)").

### Delta IV (9000-series)

As part of the Air Force's [Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_Expendable_Launch_Vehicle "Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle") (EELV) program, [McDonnell Douglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas "McDonnell Douglas") / [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing "Boeing") proposed [Delta IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV "Delta IV"). As the program name implied, many components and technologies were borrowed from existing launchers. Both [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing "Boeing") and [Lockheed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin "Lockheed Martin") were contracted to produce their EELV designs. DeltaIVs were produced in a new facility in [Decatur, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decatur,_Alabama "Decatur, Alabama").

- The first stage changed to [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen "Liquid hydrogen") fuel. Tank technologies derived from [Delta III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_III "Delta III") upper stage, but widened to 5metres.
- The kerosene engine replaced with [Rocketdyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketdyne "Rocketdyne") [RS-68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-68 "RS-68"), the first new, large liquid-fueled rocket engine designed in the United States since the [Space Shuttle Main Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25 "RS-25") (SSME) in the 1970s. Designed for low cost, it had lower chamber pressure and efficiency than the SSME, and a much simpler nozzle. Thrust chamber and upper nozzle was a channel-wall design, pioneered by Soviet engines. Lower nozzle was ablatively cooled.
- The second stage and fairing were taken from the Delta III in smaller ( [DeltaIV Medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV "Delta IV")) models; widened to 5metres in [Medium+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV "Delta IV") and [Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy "Delta IV Heavy") models.
- Medium+ models had two or four [GEM 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-Epoxy_Motor "Graphite-Epoxy Motor"), 60-inch-diameter (1.5m) solid boosters.
- The plumbing was revised and electrical circuits eliminated need for a launch tower.

The first stage was referred to as a [Common Booster Core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Booster_Core "Common Booster Core") (CBC); a [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy "Delta IV Heavy") attached two extra CBCs as boosters.

#### Delta IV Heavy

This section is an excerpt from [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy "Delta IV Heavy").\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Delta_IV_Heavy&action=edit)\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Delta_IV_launch_2013-08-28.jpg/500px-Delta_IV_launch_2013-08-28.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Delta_IV_launch_2013-08-28.jpg) Delta IV Heavy launches from [Vandenberg Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Force_Base "Vandenberg Space Force Base")

The [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy "Delta IV Heavy") (Delta 9250H) was an [expendable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_Expendable_Launch_Vehicle "Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle") [heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-lift_launch_vehicle "Heavy-lift launch vehicle"), the largest member of the [Delta IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV "Delta IV") family. Following the retirement of the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle") in 2011, it was the most capable operational launch vehicle until the [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy")'s debut in 2018. At the time of its retirement in 2024, it ranked third among active rockets in payload capacity.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-Delta_IV_Heavy_DIV_Heavy_ars-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-14)[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-15) Developed by [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Defense,_Space_&_Security "Boeing Defense, Space & Security") and later manufactured by [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance") (ULA), it first flew in 2004. The Delta IV Heavy was retired after its 16th and final launch on 9 April 2024 and was succeeded by ULA's [Vulcan Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur "Vulcan Centaur") rocket, which can offer similar heavy-lift capabilities at a lower cost with a single-core and six solid rocket boosters.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-Delta_IV_Heavy_retire-16)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-17)

The vehicle consisted of three [Common Booster Cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Booster_Core "Common Booster Core") (CBCs), each powered by an [RS-68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-68 "RS-68") engine. Two served as strap-on boosters attached to a central core. During ascent, all three engines ignited at liftoff, with the central engine throttling down partway through flight to conserve propellant before throttling up again after booster separation.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-Delta_IV_Heavy_d4ppg-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-Delta_IV_Heavy_SFN20200826-19)

A distinctive feature of Delta IV Heavy launches was the hydrogen-fueled ignition sequence, which often produced a large fireball that scorched the booster’s exterior surface.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-20)

## Launch reliability

From 1969 through 1978 (inclusive), Thor-Delta was NASA's most used launcher, with 84 launch attempts. ( [Scout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scout_(rocket) "Scout (rocket)") was the second-most used vehicle with 32 launches.)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-21) Satellites for other government agencies and foreign governments were also launched on a cost-reimbursable basis, totaling 63 satellites. Out of the 84 launch attempts there were 7 failures or partial failures, a 91.6% success rate.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-22)

### Orbital debris

The Delta was a launch success, but it has also been a significant contributor to orbital debris, as a variant used in the 1970s was prone to in-orbit explosions. Eight Delta second stages launched between 1973 and 1981 were involved in fragmentation events between 1973 and 1991 usually within the first 3 years after launch, but others have broken apart 10 or more years later. Studies determined that explosions were caused by propellant left after shutdown. The nature of the propellant and the thermal environment occupied by the derelict rockets made explosions inevitable. Depletion burns were started in 1981, and no fragmentation events for rockets launched after that have been identified. Deltas launched before the 1970s variant have had fragmentation events as long as 50 years after launch.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_(rocket_family)#cite_note-23)
