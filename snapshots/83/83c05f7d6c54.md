**Falcon 9 booster B1058** was a reusable [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") [Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5 "Falcon 9 Block 5") [first-stage booster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booster_(rocketry) "Booster (rocketry)") manufactured by [SpaceX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX "SpaceX"). B1058 was the first Falcon 9 booster to fly fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen and nineteen times and broke a turnaround record on its later flights. Its first flight was for [Crew Dragon Demo-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2"), on 30 May 2020, the first crewed orbital spaceflight by a private company. The booster was the first and only Falcon 9 booster to feature [NASA's worm logo and meatball insignia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_logo "NASA logo"), the former of which was reintroduced after last being used in 1992.[&#8202;](#ref-back-NYT-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-NYT) The booster was destroyed several days after successfully landing on the [autonomous spaceport drone ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") _[Just Read the Instructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Read_the_Instructions "Just Read the Instructions")_ on 23 December 2023. B1058 toppled over as the drone ship sailed toward [Port Canaveral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Canaveral "Port Canaveral") in rough seas.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)

While in service, B1058 had an average turnaround time of 72 days with a record turnaround of 27 days and 8 hours.[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3) For its 19 flights, it held the [Guinness World Record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Record "Guinness World Record") of most missions flown by a rocket first stage, until B1062 surpassed it by completing its 20th flight on 13 April 2024.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4)

### First flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: First flight")\]

Further information: [Crew Dragon Demo-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2")

B1058 entered service on May 30, 2020 launching [Demo-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2") from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A "LC-39A") to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") with astronauts [Bob Behnken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Behnken "Bob Behnken") and [Doug Hurley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Hurley "Doug Hurley"). It was the first crewed orbital spaceflight launched from the United States since the final [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle "Space Shuttle") mission, and the first crewed flight test of [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon_2 "SpaceX Dragon 2"). This was the first time US astronauts launched using a privately owned rocket and the first US crewed mission to the [ISS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") in 9 years. B1058 landed aboard the [autonomous spaceport drone ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") [Of Course I Still Love You](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Course_I_Still_Love_You "Of Course I Still Love You") approximately eight and a half minutes after launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/DM-2_Falcon_Rollout_%28cropped%29.jpg/330px-DM-2_Falcon_Rollout_%28cropped%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DM-2_Falcon_Rollout_(cropped).jpg) [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") B1058 and [Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon "Crew Dragon") rolling out to the launch pad, bearing the [NASA "worm" logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_insignia "NASA insignia")

### Second flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Second flight")\]

On July 20, 2020, B1058 launched again with the [ANASIS-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ANASIS-II "ANASIS-II") payload, a military satellite for South Korea. This booster launched at [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Canaveral_Space_Launch_Complex_40 "Cape Canaveral Space Launch Complex 40") towards a [GTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit "Geostationary orbit") orbit and landed successfully at the [Just Read the Instructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Read_the_Instructions "Just Read the Instructions") droneship.[&#8202;](#ref-back-6-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-6) Both fairing halves were recovered for the first time, and B1058 set a new turnaround record which was just 51 days.

### Third flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Third flight")\]

B1058 made its third flight from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A "LC-39A") on October 6, 2020 carrying 60 [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") satellites to [Low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit"). This was B1058's first mission for Starlink. About eight minutes after launch, B1058 landed on [Of Course I Still Love You](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Course_I_Still_Love_You "Of Course I Still Love You") – completing its mission. Also, by completing this launch, B1058 had achieved the shortest time it had reached 3 flights, clocking in at 129 days, beating [B1046](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_B1046 "Falcon 9 B1046") by 77 days.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-:0)

### Fourth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Fourth flight")\]

B1058 carried [SpaceX CRS-21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-21 "SpaceX CRS-21") and the first cargo [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon_2 "SpaceX Dragon 2") from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A "LC-39A") to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station"). The old [SpaceX Dragon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon_1 "SpaceX Dragon 1") needed the [Canadarm 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadarm_2 "Canadarm 2") to help [berth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docking_and_berthing_of_spacecraft "Docking and berthing of spacecraft") it to a US-derived module, but B1058 was flying the independent version of Dragon, which means that it could dock without the use of a robotic arm. B1058 completed a partial boost-back burn towards the [Of Course I Still Love You](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Course_I_Still_Love_You "Of Course I Still Love You") drone ship, 8 minutes after launch.

### Fifth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Fifth flight")\]

SpaceX announced that it would begin launching small satellites using its smallsat rideshare program,\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] [Transporter.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transporter_(SpaceX) "Transporter (SpaceX)") B1058 was the first to service this type of mission targeting a 525km (326mi) [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_altitude "Orbital altitude") [Sun-synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit "Sun-synchronous orbit").[&#8202;](#ref-back-RsA-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-RsA) The launch deployed a record 143 satellites, consisting of 120 [CubeSats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CubeSat "CubeSat"), 11 [microsatellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsatellite_(spaceflight) "Microsatellite (spaceflight)"), 10 [Starlinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink"), and 2 transfer stages. In addition, 2 hosted payloads and 1 non-separating dummy satellite[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-9) were launched.[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10) By this launch, SpaceX broke the record held by India, which launched 104 satellites in 2017.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-:1) These include [SpaceBEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceBEE "SpaceBEE") (x 36), [Lemur-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire_Global "Spire Global") (x 8), [ICEYE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICEYE "ICEYE") (x 3), UVSQ-SAT,[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12) [ELaNa 35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_Launch_of_Nanosatellites "Educational Launch of Nanosatellites") ( [PTD-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathfinder_Technology_Demonstrator "Pathfinder Technology Demonstrator")),[&#8202;](#ref-back-Upcoming-ELaNa-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-Upcoming-ELaNa) and Kepler nanosats (x 8).[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14)[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-15) D-Orbit [ION Satellite Carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ION_Satellite_Carrier "ION Satellite Carrier") and 10 [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") satellites made for testing optical laser inter-satellite links placed in a polar orbit[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-16) and 2 of 15 payloads remained attached to [SHERPA-FX1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHERPA_(space_tug) "SHERPA (space tug)"). [Exolaunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exolaunch "Exolaunch") deployed several small satellites and cubesats via their own deployment mechanisms. First flight of a Falcon 9 with a [SHERPA-FX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHERPA_(space_tug) "SHERPA (space tug)") transfer stage called SHERPA-FX1.[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-17)[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [OCISLY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCISLY "OCISLY") droneship.

### Sixth flight

B1058 launched the twentieth operational launch of Starlink satellites, again 60 in number, bringing the total to 1,265 (including prototypes) launched Starlink satellites.[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [JRTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

### Seventh flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Seventh flight")\]

B1058 launched the 23rd operational launch of Starlink satellites, bringing the total to 1,385 launched [Starlink satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") (including prototype). This launch featured the fastest for B1058 and second fastest overall booster turnaround time at 27 days and 8 hours (after Starlink 18 with B1060.5, which was 4 hours faster).[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-20) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [OCISLY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCISLY "OCISLY") droneship.

### Eighth flight

Rideshare launch with a targeted orbit at 569x582, significantly higher than typical Starlink launches, to allow for needs of the rideshare payloads.[&#8202;](#ref-back-SL28-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-SL28) Fairing "wet recovery" done by contracted recovery vessel _Shelia Bordelon_ for the last time. ### Ninth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Ninth flight")\]

First East Coast Starlink launch after the v1.0 L28 launch which completed the first shell of the Starlink network located at 540km altitude. Fairing "wet recovery" was attempted by SpaceX multipurpose ship, Bob for the first time, and both fairing halves were retrieved from water.[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22)[&#8202;](#ref-back-SXF_fr-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-SXF_fr) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [JRTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

### Tenth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Tenth flight")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/46/Space_Launch_Delta_45_Supports_Successful_Transporter-3_Launch_%28220113-X-WN929-1092%29_%28cropped%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Space_Launch_Delta_45_Supports_Successful_Transporter-3_Launch_(220113-X-WN929-1092)_(cropped).jpg) B1058 landing back at LZ-1 during its 10th mission

B1058 launched the third dedicated SmallSat Rideshare mission to Sun-synchronous orbit. A total of 105 payloads including: [Planet Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Labs "Planet Labs") SuperDoves (×44),[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24) and some of the customer payloads on SpaceFlight's _SXRS-6_ mission.[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-25) In addition, four secret satellites, likely test satellites built by SpaceX based on the Starshield bus (based on Starlink Block v1.5 or v2.0 technology),[&#8202;](#ref-back-USA320-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-USA320) were also deployed for the US army. Their purpose has not been revealed, but is likely either technical demonstration, communications, earth observation or signals intelligence.[&#8202;](#ref-back-USA_320,_...,_323,_328,_...,_331-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-USA_320,_...,_323,_328,_...,_331) In 2020, SpaceX had won a US$149million contract for developing and launching missile tracking satellites based on the Starlink architecture.[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on [SpaceX Landing Zone 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Landing_Zone_1 "SpaceX Landing Zone 1").

### Eleventh flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Eleventh flight")\]

The eleventh mission of B1058 was the first Group 4 mission to feature 2 upper stage burns like v1.0 Starlink launches, with deployment of the 46 satellites approximately 1 hour after lift-off into a higher circular orbit. This is aimed at reducing the risk of high drag that caused [38 of the Group 4–7 satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Starlink_launches#Group_4-7_satellite_reentries "List of Starlink launches") to fail reaching their intended orbits, and instead, reenter shortly after launch.[&#8202;](#ref-back-29-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-29) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [A Shortfall of Gravitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Shortfall_of_Gravitas "A Shortfall of Gravitas") droneship.

### Fourteenth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Fourteenth flight")\]

B1058 launched a regular East Coast Starlink network launch to a 540km circular orbit at an inclination of 53.2°. Bluewalker-3 is a rideshare[&#8202;](#ref-back-32-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-32) mission launched to 513km altitude 53° inclination.[&#8202;](#ref-back-33-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-33) In addition to this, the 2nd stage first executed two burns to deploy the Bluewalker3, followed by executing two more burns to deploy the Starlinks to a 330km altitude 53.2° inclination orbit, concluding with deorbit burn, which made it one of the most complex F9 missions up to date.[&#8202;](#ref-back-34-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-34) On 11 September 2022, it flew for the 14th time and became the first booster to be recovered 14 times.[&#8202;](#ref-back-35-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-35) B1058 launched from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A "LC-39A") and landed successfully on the [ASOG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

### Fifteenth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Fifteenth flight")\]

On another record breaking 15th flight for the first time for SpaceX, B1058 launched a regular East Coast Starlink network launch to a 540km circular orbit at an inclination of 53.2°.[&#8202;](#ref-back-36-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-36)[&#8202;](#ref-back-37-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-37) B1058 launched from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A "LC-39A") and landed successfully on the [JRTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

### Certification for further flights

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Certification for further flights")\]

Until then, Block 5 boosters were only certified for 15 launches.[&#8202;](#ref-back-38-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-38) A "deep-dive" examination has been performed on B1058 and B1060 after their 15th flight,[&#8202;](#ref-back-Ax2FRR-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-Ax2FRR) and SpaceX certified Falcon 9 boosters for 20 missions.

### Sixteenth flight

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Sixteenth flight")\]

On 10 July 2023, B1058 launched a regular East Coast v2 mini Starlink launch to their Generation 2 network. B1058 was the first booster to launch and land 16 times, pushing the envelope and surpassing its previous record, which was 15 flights.[&#8202;](#ref-back-40-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-40) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [JRTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

It was the last flight of B1058.[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-43) B1058 launched from [SLC-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLC-40 "SLC-40") and landed successfully on the [JRTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") droneship.

### Demise

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Falcon_9_B1058&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Demise")\]

Despite successfully landing after its nineteenth flight, B1058 tipped over during transit due to rough seas and high winds. SpaceX has already equipped newer Falcon boosters with upgraded landing legs that have the capability to self-level and mitigate this type of issue.[&#8202;](#ref-back-44-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-44)

This was the second instance of a booster toppling over during transit due to weather. The first time that this occurred was in April 2019, when [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") core booster [B1055](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_first-stage_boosters#B1055 "List of Falcon 9 first-stage boosters") suffered the same fate.[&#8202;](#ref-back-45-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-45)

SpaceX intends on salvaging and inspecting the engines to learn about how they were affected by flying nineteen times, which may produce learnings for the rest of the Falcon 9 fleet. Doug Hurley suggested that the remnants of the booster (as well as the _Endeavour_ capsule, after its retirement) would be suitable for museum display as a historically significant artifact in the history of crewed spaceflight.[&#8202;](#ref-back-46-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-46)
