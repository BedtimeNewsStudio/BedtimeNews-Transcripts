# History of the Royal Society

TimelineJS Embed

Previous

Date

Gresham College

November 28, 1660 — 1710

## Foundation

### November 28, 1660

Following a lecture by Christopher Wren, twelve men of science establish a 'College for the Promoting of Physico-Mathematical, Experimental Learning' at Gresham College, London.

Loading

## Gresham College

### November 28, 1660 — 1710

The Fellows hold their weekly meetings in rooms at Gresham College on Bishopsgate until 1710; only interrupted by the Great Fire of London in 1666.

Loading

## Royal Charter

### January 12, 1662

The Fellowship receives a Royal Charter from Charles II, and an official name: The Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge

Loading

## Arundel House

### 1665 — 1673

Henry Howard, Duke of Norfold welcomes the Fellows in Arundel House for long intervals, including during the reconstruction that followed the Great Fire of London in 1666. He also donates his exceptional library collection to the Society.

Loading

## The Philosophical Transactions

### March 6, 1665

Henry Oldenburg, the Secretary of the Society, publishes the first edition of the world's first scientific periodical.

## Micrographia

### November 1, 1665

The Royal Society publishes Robert Hooke's Micrographia, containing landmark drawings made using a microscope and including the famous flea. The book coins the word cell as a biological term.

Loading

## History of the Royal Society

### 1667

Only two years after its creation, the first history of the Royal Society is published by Thomas Sprat to celebrate the institution and its scientific pursuits.

Loading

## Observation of ‘little animals’ in rainwater

### October 16, 1677 — November 16, 1677

Dutch microscopist Antoni van Leeuwenhoek observes micro-organisms for the first time. The Royal Society repeats his observation and the science of microbiology is born.

Loading

## Principia Mathematica

### December 1, 1687

Sir Isaac Newton's Principia Mathematica describing the action of gravity is published by the Royal Society with the help of Edmond Halley. It remains one of the most influential books of all time.

Loading

## Crane Court

### 1710 — 1780

The Society acquires its first home on Crane Court, off Fleet Street. It requires extensive re-designing under the direction of Sir Christopher Wren to house the Society's collections.

Loading

## Hans Sloane

### December 7, 1727

Sir Hans Sloane becomes President of the Royal Society. On his death in 1754, Sloane's collections, including much Royal Society-related material, became the core of the British Museum.

Loading

## Benjamin Franklin

### December 14, 1752

Benjamin Franklin demonstrates the electrical nature of lightning using a kite and key in a letter to the Royal Society. It is still the world's most famous scientific experiment.

Loading

## Transit of Venus

### 1769

The Royal Society backs an expedition to observe the transit of Venus from Tahiti. The vessel Endeavour, commanded by Lieutenant James Cook, reaches Australia and New Zealand.

Loading

## Somerset House

### 1780 — 1857

Newly elected President Sir Joseph Banks accepts the offer from the Government to move the Society to Somerset House joining the Royal Academy of Arts and the Society of Antiquaries.

Loading

## Uranus

### March 13, 1781

William Herschel discovers a new body in the solar system. Mistaken at first for a comet, it is sensationally identified as a planet by astronomers around the globe, reported to the Royal Society as Georgium Sidus (George's Star) by 1783 and eventually renamed Uranus.

Loading

## Charles Babbage

### 1823

The Royal Society approves Charles Babbage's Difference Engine, a mechanical means of computing mathematical tables. Babbage goes on to design Analytical Engines capable of storing programs.

Loading

## William Henry Fox Talbot

### 1839

William Henry Fox Talbot communicates his process of photogenic drawing to the Royal Society. His colleague Sir John Herschel promptly renames it photography, the first new art form in centuries.

Loading

## Government Grant

### 1851

The British Government awards the Royal Society its first annual Government Grant of £1,000 to be distributed for private individual scientific research. Today, funding scientific research is one of the main roles of the Society.

## Burlington House

### 1857 — 1967

The Royal Society joins seven other learned societies into Burlington House, recently purchased by the Government as a meeting place for the arts and science.

Loading

## Copley Medal

### 1864

Charles Darwin receives the Copley Medal. On the Origin of Species (1859) is controversially excluded from the citation, but a speech affirms it is with that work that the public will naturally recollect the honour.

Loading

## Krakatoa

### 1883

Krakatoa explodes killing an estimated 40,000 people. The Royal Society solicits observations from the public and letters pour in from many nations describing this global phenomenon.

Loading

## William Ramsay and Lord Rayleigh

### 1894

William Ramsay and Lord Rayleigh agree to research atmospheric gases in the aftermath of a Royal Society lecture. Ramsay discovers argon, helium, neon, krypton and xenon in a research tour-de-force.

Loading

## Albert Einstein

### 1919

Astronomers confirm general relativity theory to the Royal Society using observations made during the total eclipse of this year. Albert Einstein is elected to the Fellowship in 1921.

Loading

## James Chadwick

### 1932

James Chadwick detects the neutron, and publishes his findings with the Royal Society. Soon, neutron bombardment of uranium will release the power of the atom.

Loading

## Second World War

### 1939

The Royal Society hosts a 'secret party' at Burlington House for European Jewish scientists and intellectuals, all refugees from Nazism. Widely reported in the press, the guest list remained closed to protect relatives still in danger.

## Kathleen Lonsdale and Marjory Stephenson

### 1945

The first women Fellows are elected - Kathleen Lonsdale - a crystallographer and Marjory Stephenson, a biochemist.

Loading

## The Festival of Britain

### 1951

The Festival of Britain takes place on the Southbank, with the Royal Festival Hall built for this purpose. The Royal Society is involved in aspects of the festival including the Dome of Discovery.

## Crick and Watson

### June 25, 1953

Rosalind Franklin, Francis Crick, James Watson and others, present a model of DNA structure at the Royal Society Soirée in Burlington House, following their research breakthroughs.

Loading

## Halley Bay, Antarctica

### 1956

The Royal Society establishes a research base at Halley Bay, Antarctica. Here in 1985, dramatic losses in the ozone layer are observed and the base remains an important location for climate research.

Loading

## Dorothy Hodgkin

### 1964

Dorothy Hodgkin becomes Britain's only female Nobel Prize winning scientist. Her x-ray crystallography work on penicillin and vitamin B12 had long been nurtured by the Royal Society.

Loading

## 6-9, Carlton House Terrace

### 1967

The Society moves into its current premises, occupying four of the houses designed by architect John Nash in 1827, overlooking the Mall, leading to Buckingham Palace.

## University Fellowship Scheme

### 1983

The Royal Society launches its University Fellowship scheme, now its flagship grants scheme. The URF scheme seeks to identify and support scientists with the potential to become leaders in their field and in the wider UK scientific community. 1000 URFs have been appointed since its establishment, including mathematician Marcus du Sautoy and physicist Brian Cox.

Loading

## Sir Tim Berners-Lee

### 2001

Sir Tim Berners-Lee is elected to the Fellowship. His proposal of a global hypertext project in 1989 results in the creation of the World Wide Web.

Loading

## Stephen Hawking

### 2006

Stephen Hawking is presented with the Copley Medal for his contributions to theoretical physics. The medal is first flown into space courtesy of NASA's Space Shuttle.

Loading

## 350th Anniversary

### 2010

The Royal Society celebrates its 350th anniversary with a programme of activities including exhibition partnerships at local and national levels, publication of a book of essays about the Society's history, and a festival of science and arts at the Southbank Centre.

Loading

## COVID-19

### 2019

The Royal Society works with its community of scientists to deliver timely and rapid advice on data and policy matters during the coronavirus pandemic.

## Queen Elizabeth II

### 2022

The Society's longest serving Royal Patron, Queen Elizabeth II, celebrated her platinum Jubilee in June, before being laid to rest in September 2022.

## Where does the Royal Society come from?

In the mid-17th century, informal gatherings of London- and Oxford-based intellectuals coalesced to form a chartered organisation. Its name would be The Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge.

## What were the early days of the Royal Society like?

From its first meeting, on 28 November 1660, following a lecture by the Professor of Astronomy at Gresham College in London, [Christopher Wren](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8299), the new Fellowship would concern itself with natural philosophy – what we would now term science. Wren considered that it should act to transform knowledge, profit, and health and the conveniences of life. The Royal Society would gather information by [correspondence](https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/el/early-letters-letters-from-correspondents-in-natural-philosophy-sent-to-the-royal-society-and-its-fellows), but its Fellows would also observe the natural world, conduct experiments, discuss their outcomes, and eventually publish the results.

Natural philosophers of this period were products of their society. They were drawn from the professional and aristocratic classes and were exclusively male. They were not professional scientists; but lawyers, merchants, physicians, aristocrats, and landowners, who were brought together by a common interest at the Royal Society’s weekly meetings. By 1662, the Society had appointed a Secretary, [Henry Oldenburg](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8001), to manage its correspondence, and a Curator of Experiments, [Robert Hooke](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8242), to oversee demonstrations. They became influential figures in the early years of the Society: Oldenburg by establishing the journal [Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/loi/rstl) in 1665, and Hooke by becoming its leading experimentalist, most memorably in the minute observations collected in his book [Micrographia](https://ttp.royalsociety.org/ttp/ttp.html?id=a9c4863d-db77-42d1-b294-fe66c85958b3&type=book), published in the same year.

Among the early Fellows were [Robert Boyle](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8137), [John Evelyn](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8217), [John Locke](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8232), and by 1672, [Isaac Newton](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8414), whose [Principia Mathematica](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-ROYALSOCIETY-00069/7) (1687) was published under the Royal Society’s imprimatur. Fellows from across Europe and the New World were also elected, including [Johann Hevelius](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8253), [Antoni van Leeuwenhoek](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8438), [Gottfried Leibniz](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8450), and [John Winthrop](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA4933).

## What does its motto ‘Nullius in verba’ mean?

The Royal Society's motto 'Nullius in verba' was adopted in its First Charter in 1662. is taken to mean 'take nobody's word for it'. It is an expression of the determination of Fellows to withstand the domination of authority and to verify all statements by an appeal to facts determined by experiment.

## When were the first Royal Society medals and lectures created?

By the 18th century, the Society was not only publishing science, but rewarding its practitioners. The [Copley Medal](https://royalsociety.org/medals-and-prizes/copley-medal/), the earliest prize for scientific excellence, was first awarded in 1731, to the electrical researcher [Stephen Gray](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA7365). It was later won by [Benjamin Franklin](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA7974), [John Smeaton](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA6321), [Joseph Priestley](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8274), [William Herschel](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8060), and [Alessandro Volta](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA6396). Prize lectures were also established, including the [Croonian](https://royalsociety.org/medals-and-prizes/croonian-lecture/) and [Bakerian](https://royalsociety.org/medals-and-prizes/bakerian-lecture/).

## When did the Royal Society start administering grants?

In 1828, [William Hyde Wollaston](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8298) donated money to establish a grant-making fund: the Society was no longer limited to honouring discoverers after the event but could encourage their research in the most practical possible way. The approach was endorsed by a Government Grant of £1,000 in 1851, to be administered by the Royal Society in underwriting the costs of original investigations.

## When did science become a profession?

The 19th century saw other changes in how the Royal Society operated, and in the nature of its activities. [Refereeing of scientific papers](https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/rr/referees-reports-on-scientific-papers-submitted-to-the-royal-society-for-publication) commenced from 1832, replacing previous gentlemanly communication of research. The nature of the Fellowship itself was modified towards more professional practitioners, as a result of rule changes enacted in 1847. And by the 1870s, the Society had become responsible for the annual scientific exhibitions known as [conversazioni](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/8gUxFYZiFMOMJQ), formerly managed by its Presidents. The organisation had become a public face for science, even if that public remained a limited one.

## How did the Royal Society advise Parliament through its history?

Throughout the Victorian period, although independent of government, the Royal Society had been consulted in matters of public interest and had acted in the administration of national institutions: either directly, for example in the management of the Meteorological Office (1865-1905) and Kew Observatory (1871-1899) or by their establishment, including the National Physical Laboratory (1900). In specific instances, the Society reported on a variety of scientific matters to Parliament or to colonial administrations: from progress on Babbage’s calculating engine (1823-1831), to investigations into sleeping sickness (1896-1903) and malaria (1898-1903).

## How did the Royal Society change at the start of the 20th century?

The pace of scientific development in the 20th century encompassed the new physics of relativity and quantum mechanics, the technology of two world wars, and advances in the understanding of genetics and the science of life. Social mores changed too, and with them the Royal Society, which although it had given research grants to women scientists throughout the century, and had intermittently published their work, only relented to their admission to the Fellowship from 1945. [Kathleen Lonsdale](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA7993) and [Marjory Stephenson](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?src=CalmView.Persons&id=NA8092) led the way.

By then, the foundations of the modern Royal Society had been laid. The Fellowship would be drawn from high achieving professional scientists. The organisation would go on to refine its key functions, notably in grant-making, policy reporting, public engagement in science, publishing, and international affairs. The original weekly gatherings of Fellows were transformed into scientific discussion meetings on topics of international importance in the sciences. In these, and in all activities, the Royal Society is guided by its founding principles, its Fellows, and its motto: Nullius in verba.

To discover more on the history of the Royal Society, visit its collections of archives, printed books, portraits and scientific objects.
