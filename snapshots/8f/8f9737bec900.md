***Cinchona*** (pronounced [/sɪŋˈkoʊnə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") or [/sɪnˈtʃoʊnə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")[[1]](#cite_note-1)) is a [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus "Genus") of flowering plants in the family [Rubiaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubiaceae "Rubiaceae") containing at least 23 species of trees and shrubs. All are native to the [tropical Andean forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_Andes "Tropical Andes") of western [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America"). A few species are reportedly [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization_(biology) "Naturalization (biology)") in [Central America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America "Central America"), [Jamaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaica "Jamaica"), [French Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Polynesia "French Polynesia"), [Sulawesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi "Sulawesi"), [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena "Saint Helena") in the [South Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Atlantic "South Atlantic"), and [São Tomé and Príncipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/São_Tomé_and_Príncipe "São Tomé and Príncipe") off the coast of tropical Africa, and others have been cultivated in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") and [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java "Java"), where they have formed hybrids.

*Cinchona* has been historically sought after for its medicinal value, as the bark of several species yields [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine") and other [alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloid "Alkaloid"). Trees in the genus are also known as **fever trees** because of their antimalarial properties.[[2]](#cite_note-2) These were the only effective treatments against [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria") during the height of [European colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonialism "European colonialism"), which made them of great economic and political importance. Europeans appropriated indigenous peoples' contributions to medicinal knowledge about cinchona, omitting them from scientific accounts.[[3]](#cite_note-:4-3) Mass production of quinine from cinchona funneled wealth to Europe and enforced colonial power dynamics in South America.[[4]](#cite_note-:6-4)

The artificial [synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine_total_synthesis "Quinine total synthesis") of quinine in 1944, an increase in resistant forms of malaria, and the emergence of alternate therapies eventually ended large-scale economic interest in *Cinchona* cultivation. *Cinchona* alkaloids show promise in treating *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria, which has evolved resistance to synthetic drugs, reinforcing the plant's medicinal significance 400 years after Europeans' first documentation of its quinine.[[5]](#cite_note-:5-5) Cinchona plants continue to be revered for their historical legacy; the [national tree of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_officinalis "Cinchona officinalis") is in the genus *Cinchona*.[[6]](#cite_note-6)

## Etymology and common names

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology and common names")]

[Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus "Carl Linnaeus") named the genus in 1742, based on a story from 104 years earlier.[[7]](#cite_note-u071-7)

The claim is that the plant cured a case of malaria suffered by the Countess of [Chinchón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Jerónimo_de_Cabrera,_4th_Count_of_Chinchón "Luis Jerónimo de Cabrera, 4th Count of Chinchón"), the vicereine of [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru") in 1638.[[8]](#cite_note-t867-8) She then ordered the commercial harvesting of the bark and organized its successful distribution in Europe starting in the late 1600s.[[9]](#cite_note-s347-9)

Linnaeus used the Italian spelling *Cinchona*, but the name Chinchón (pronounced [[tʃinˈtʃon]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Spanish "Help:IPA/Spanish") in Spanish) led to [Clements Markham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clements_Markham "Clements Markham") and many others proposing a correction of the spelling to *Chinchona*, and some prefer the pronunciation [/tʃɪnˈtʃoʊnə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") for the common name of the plant.[[10]](#cite_note-z818-10)

[Traditional medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_medicine "Traditional medicine") uses from South America known as **[Jesuit's bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit's_bark "Jesuit's bark")** and Jesuit's powder have been traced to *Cinchona*.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] In Nigeria it is locally called **dogo yaro**, or Dogo'n yaro, which translates to 'tall man'.[[11]](#cite_note-k758-11)[[12]](#cite_note-g544-12)

## Description

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Description")]

*Cinchona* plants belong to the family Rubiaceae and are large [shrubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrub "Shrub") or small [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree "Tree") with [evergreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evergreen "Evergreen") foliage, growing 5 to 15 m (16 to 49 ft) in height. The [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf "Leaf") are opposite, rounded to lanceolate, and 10–40 cm long. The [flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower "Flower") are white, pink, or red, and produced in terminal [panicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panicle "Panicle"). The [fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit "Fruit") is a small [capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsule_(fruit) "Capsule (fruit)") containing numerous [seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed "Seed"). A key character of the genus is that the flowers have marginally hairy corolla lobes. Some species display [heterostyly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterostyly "Heterostyly"). The [tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_(biology) "Tribe (biology)") Cinchoneae includes the genera *Cinchonopsis*, *Jossia*, *Ladenbergia*, *Remijia*, *Stilpnophyllum*, and *Ciliosemina*.[[13]](#cite_note-13) In South America, natural populations of *Cinchona* species have geographically distinct distributions. During the 19th century, the introduction of several species into cultivation in the same areas of India and Java, by the English and Dutch East India Company, respectively, led to the formation of [hybrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)").[[14]](#cite_note-nybg-14)

Linnaeus described the genus based on the species *Cinchona officinalis*, which is found only in a small region of [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuador "Ecuador") and is of little medicinal significance.[[15]](#cite_note-15)[[16]](#cite_note-16) Nearly 300 species were later described and named in the genus, but a revision of the genus in 1998 identified only 23 distinct species.[[14]](#cite_note-nybg-14)[[17]](#cite_note-tropicos-17)

## History

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: History")]

### Early references

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Early references")]

The [febrifugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipyretic "Antipyretic") properties of bark from trees now known to be in the genus *Cinchona* were used by many South American cultures prior to European contact.[[18]](#cite_note-Crawford-2014-18) [Jesuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Jesus "Society of Jesus") played a key role in the transfer of remedies from the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World "New World").

The traditional story connecting *Cinchona* species with malaria treatment was first recorded by Italian physician [Sebastiano Bado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastiano_Bado "Sebastiano Bado") in 1663.[[19]](#cite_note-19)[[20]](#cite_note-20) It tells of the wife of [Luis Jerónimo de Cabrera, 4th Count of Chinchón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Jerónimo_de_Cabrera,_4th_Count_of_Chinchón "Luis Jerónimo de Cabrera, 4th Count of Chinchón") and [Viceroy of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroy_of_Peru "Viceroy of Peru"), who fell ill in [Lima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lima "Lima") with a tertian fever. A Spanish governor advised a traditional remedy, which resulted in a miraculous and rapid cure. The countess then supposedly ordered a large quantity of the bark and took it back to Europe. Bado claimed to have received this information from an Italian named Antonius Bollus, who was a merchant in Peru. Clements Markham identified the countess as Ana de Osorio, but this was shown to be incorrect by Haggis.[[21]](#cite_note-haggis-21) Ana de Osorio married the Count of Chinchón in August 1621 and died in 1625, several years before the count was appointed viceroy of Peru in 1628. His second wife, Francisca Henriques de Ribera, accompanied him to Peru. Haggis further examined the count's diaries and found no mention of the countess suffering from fever, although the count himself had many malarial attacks.[[21]](#cite_note-haggis-21) Because of these and numerous other discrepancies, Bado's story has been generally rejected as little more than a legend.

Quina bark was mentioned by [Fray Antonio de La Calancha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_de_la_Calancha "Antonio de la Calancha") in 1638 as coming from a tree in [Loja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loja,_Ecuador "Loja, Ecuador") (Loxa). He noted that bark powder weighing about two coins was cast into water and drunk to cure fevers and "tertians". Jesuit Father [Bernabé Cobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernabé_Cobo "Bernabé Cobo") (1582–1657) also wrote on the "fever tree" in 1653. The legend was popularized in English literature by Markham, and in 1874, he also published a "plea for the correct spelling of the genus *Chinchona*".[[22]](#cite_note-22)[[23]](#cite_note-markham1880-23) Spanish physician and botanist [Nicolás Monardes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolás_Monardes "Nicolás Monardes") wrote of a New World bark powder used in Spain in 1574, and another physician, Juan Fragoso, wrote of bark powder from an unknown tree in 1600 that was used for treating various ills. Both identify the sources as trees that do not bear fruit and have heart-shaped leaves; they were suggested to have been referring to *Cinchona* species.[[24]](#cite_note-fragoso-24)

The name *quina-quina* or *quinquina* was suggested as an old name for *Cinchona* used in Europe and based on the native name used by the [Quechua people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechua_people "Quechua people") which means 'bark of barks'. Italian sources spelt *quina* as "*cina*", which was a source of confusion with *[Smilax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smilax "Smilax")* from China.[[25]](#cite_note-cinch-25) Haggis argued that *qina* and Jesuit's bark actually referred to *Myroxylon peruiferum*, or Peruvian balsam, and that this was an item of importance in Spanish trade in the 1500s. Over time, the bark of *Myroxylon* may have been adulterated with the similar-looking bark of what is now known as *Cinchona*.[[21]](#cite_note-haggis-21) Gradually, the adulterant became the main product that was the key therapeutic ingredient used in malarial therapy. The bark was included as *Cortex Peruanus* in the [London Pharmacopoeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacopoeia "Pharmacopoeia") in 1677.

### Economic significance

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Economic significance")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/63/Cortex_peruvianus_Leeuwenhoek.jpg/250px-Cortex_peruvianus_Leeuwenhoek.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cortex_peruvianus_Leeuwenhoek.jpg)

*Cortex peruvianus* study by [Antonie van Leeuwenhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek "Antonie van Leeuwenhoek"), 1706

The "fever tree" was finally described carefully by astronomer [Charles Marie de la Condamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Marie_de_la_Condamine "Charles Marie de la Condamine"), who visited [Quito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quito "Quito") in 1735 on a quest to measure an [arc of the meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc "Meridian arc"). The species he described, *Cinchona officinalis*, however, was found to be of little therapeutic value. The first living plants seen in Europe were *C. calisaya* plants grown at the *Jardin des Plantes* from seeds collected by [Hugh Algernon Weddell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Algernon_Weddell "Hugh Algernon Weddell") from [Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivia "Bolivia") in 1846.[[26]](#cite_note-26) [José Celestino Mutis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Celestino_Mutis "José Celestino Mutis"), physician to the Viceroy of Nueva Granada, Pedro Messia de la Cerda, gathered information on cinchona in [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombia "Colombia") from 1760 and wrote a manuscript, *El Arcano de la Quina* (1793), with illustrations. He proposed a Spanish expedition to search for plants of commercial value, which was approved in 1783 and was continued after his death in 1808 by his nephew Sinforoso Mutis.[[27]](#cite_note-27) As demand for the bark increased, the trees in the forests began to be destroyed. To maintain their monopoly on cinchona bark, Peru and surrounding countries began outlawing the export of cinchona seeds and saplings beginning in the early 19th century.[[28]](#cite_note-28)

The colonial European powers eventually considered growing the plant in other parts of the tropics. The French mission of 1743, of which de la Condamine was a member, lost their cinchona plants when a wave took them off their ship. The [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Empire "Dutch Empire") sent [Justus Hasskarl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_Carl_Hasskarl "Justus Carl Hasskarl"), who brought plants that were then cultivated in Java from 1854. English explorer Clements Markham went to collect plants that were introduced in [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") and the Nilgiris of southern India in 1860.[[29]](#cite_note-mysore-29) The main species introduced were *[C. succirubra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_succirubra "Cinchona succirubra")*, or red bark, (now *C. pubescens*) as its sap turned red on contact with air, and *[Cinchona calisaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_calisaya "Cinchona calisaya")*. The alkaloids quinine and cinchonine were extracted by [Pierre Joseph Pelletier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Joseph_Pelletier "Pierre Joseph Pelletier") and [Joseph Bienaimé Caventou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bienaimé_Caventou "Joseph Bienaimé Caventou") in 1820. Two more key alkaloids, quinidine and cinchonidine, were later identified and it became a routine in quinology to examine the contents of these components in assays. The yields of quinine in the cultivated trees were low and time was needed to develop sustainable methods to extract bark.

In the meantime, [Charles Ledger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Ledger "Charles Ledger") and his native assistant [Manuel Incra Mamani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Incra_Mamani "Manuel Incra Mamani") collected another species from Bolivia. Mamani was caught and beaten by Bolivian officials, leading to his death, but Ledger obtained seeds containing high levels of quinine. These seeds were offered to the British, who were uninterested, leading to the rest being sold to the Dutch. The Dutch saw their value and multiplied the stock. The species later named *[Cinchona ledgeriana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_calisaya "Cinchona calisaya")*[[30]](#cite_note-30) yielded 8 to 13% quinine in bark grown in Dutch Indonesia, which effectively outcompeted the [British Indian production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_cultivation_in_British_India "Cinchona cultivation in British India") which focused on a range of alkaloids other than quinine.[[31]](#cite_note-31) Only later did the English see the value and sought to obtain the seeds of *C. ledgeriana* from the Dutch.[[32]](#cite_note-32)[[33]](#cite_note-Williams-1962-33) In the 19th century, the British had established cinchona plantations in India from seeds they smuggled out of Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia, which were mass manufactured into pills and tablets used by imperial soldiers.[[34]](#cite_note-:7-34)

[Francesco Torti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Torti "Francesco Torti") used the response of fevers to treatment with *Cinchona* as a system of classification of fevers or a means for diagnosis between malarial and non-malarial fevers. Its use in the effective treatment of malaria brought an end to treatment by [bloodletting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodletting "Bloodletting") and long-held ideas of [humorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humorism "Humorism") from [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen "Galen").[[35]](#cite_note-35) Clements Markham was knighted for his role in establishing *Cinchona* species in Indonesia. Hasskarl was knighted with the Dutch order of the Lion.[[36]](#cite_note-36)

### Bioprospecting and medicinal plant relationships

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Bioprospecting and medicinal plant relationships")]

Indigenous people in the Amazon view plants as beings with agency, practicing a reciprocal relationship with them.[[37]](#cite_note-:1-37) In this cosmology, a plant has a spiritual and bodily dimension, which are used in conjunction to heal a person.[[37]](#cite_note-:1-37) [Bioprospecting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioprospecting "Bioprospecting") inserts a different perspective on nature, placing value on what can become a commercial product, which can objectify the plant and downgrade it as a being.[[38]](#cite_note-:0-38)[[37]](#cite_note-:1-37) Bioassays are used to isolate active chemical compounds with desired properties, which is not representative of the complexity of the plant as a whole.[[39]](#cite_note-:2-39) Exploitation of the cinchona plant and indigenous knowledge, exported overseas for profit, can be viewed as a form of [extractivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extractivism "Extractivism").[[37]](#cite_note-:1-37)

#### Bioprospecting revenue for conservation initiatives

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Bioprospecting revenue for conservation initiatives")]

Ideally, income generated by drugs developed from bioprospecting ventures would assist biodiverse countries in conservation and sustainable development, but poor governance and regulation can prevent its enactment.[[40]](#cite_note-40) Exploitation and endangerment can occur when medicinal plants are over-harvested, which is reinforced once communities rely on that harvest for income, making the pharmaceutical industry's demand significant for conservation implications.[[41]](#cite_note-41) However, conservation and sustainability programs can also impose capitalist systems, assigning value to organisms and biodiversity in terms of money, as well as dismissing indigenous management practices.[[38]](#cite_note-:0-38)

### Use of indigenous medicinal plant knowledge

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Use of indigenous medicinal plant knowledge")]

When Western bioprospectors benefited from indigenous medicinal knowledge, determining intellectual property rights and financial compensation was complicated by the transmissible flow of knowledge between and within these communities, which do not conform to Western notions of ownership.[[42]](#cite_note-42) It It has been argued that due to these cultural differences, indigenous culture may be oversimplified, in the process dismissing obligation to observe intellectual property rights, and condoning appropriations of knowledge that can result in [biopiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopiracy "Biopiracy").[[43]](#cite_note-43) On the other hand, some elements of the pharmaceutical industry have noted that overly arduous patent processes can limit access to the full potential of drug discovery in the Amazon.[[39]](#cite_note-:2-39)

It has further been argued that indigenous contributions, particularly those of women who played a major role in traditional phyto-medicine, are often undervalued or even ignored by Western botany.[[38]](#cite_note-:0-38) The European discovery of cinchona excluded its local Quechua names, yarachucchu and ccarachucchu, which has been argued to have delegitimized the culture, knowledge, and relationships that preceded its scientific classification.[[37]](#cite_note-:1-37)

### Western medicine and indigenous society

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Western medicine and indigenous society")]

Pharmaceutical ventures seeking to develop new drugs favor scans for diseases that impact the [Global North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_North_and_Global_South "Global North and Global South"), as a more lucrative market, but neglect to the Global South as significant beneficiaries.[[38]](#cite_note-:0-38) Further, imposition of Western medicine on local communities habituated to traditional medicine can generate mistrust and dismiss indigenous medicinal practices.[[44]](#cite_note-44) In the historical case of cinchona, the introduction of Western medicine in Brazil by Germany supported a campaign of modernization that has been qualified as discriminatory or even racist.[[4]](#cite_note-:6-4) As such, public health developments (enabled by quinine from cinchona) were driven in priority by the interest of German settlements.[[4]](#cite_note-:6-4) Malaria cures were tested on enslaved indigenous people[[4]](#cite_note-:6-4), and their labor was used to harvest cinchona, exposing them to abuse and poor living and working conditions.[[3]](#cite_note-:4-3) They faced insurmountable debt, which was used to pay off overvalued merchandise and goods, and increased when harvest quotas were not met.[[3]](#cite_note-:4-3)

## Ecology

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Ecology")]

*Cinchona* species are used as food plants by the [larvae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larva "Larva") of some [lepidopteran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidoptera "Lepidoptera") species, including [the engrailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engrailed_(moth) "Engrailed (moth)"), [the commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduza_procris "Moduza procris"), and members of the genus *[Endoclita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoclita "Endoclita")*, including *[E. damor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoclita_damor "Endoclita damor")*, *[E. purpurescens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoclita_purpurescens "Endoclita purpurescens")*, and *[E. sericeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoclita_sericeus "Endoclita sericeus")*.

*[C. pubescens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_pubescens "Cinchona pubescens")* has grown uncontrolled on some islands, such as the [Galapagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galapagos "Galapagos"), where it has posed the risk of outcompeting native plant species.[[45]](#cite_note-45)

## Cultivation and use

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Cultivation and use")]

See also: [Quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine")

Main article: [Jesuit's bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit's_bark "Jesuit's bark")

Whether cinchona bark was used in any traditional medicines within Andean Indigenous groups when it first came to notice by Europeans is unclear.[[18]](#cite_note-Crawford-2014-18) Since its first confirmed medicinal record in the early 17th century, it has been used as a treatment for malaria. This use was popularised in Europe by the Spanish colonisers of South America.[[18]](#cite_note-Crawford-2014-18) The bark contains [alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloids "Alkaloids"), including [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine") and [quinidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinidine "Quinidine").[[46]](#cite_note-46) Cinchona is the only economically practical source of quinine, a drug that is still recommended for the treatment of *falciparum* [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria").[[47]](#cite_note-47)[[5]](#cite_note-:5-5)

### Europe

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Europe")]

Italian botanist [Pietro Castelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Castelli "Pietro Castelli") wrote a pamphlet noteworthy as being the first Italian publication to mention the *Cinchona* species. By the 1630s (or 1640s, depending on the reference), the bark was being exported to Europe. In the late 1640s, the method of use of the bark was noted in the *[Schedula Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schedula_Romana "Schedula Romana").* The Royal Society of London published in its first year (1666) "An account of Dr. Sydenham's book, entitled, Methodus curandi febres . . ."[[48]](#cite_note-48)

English King [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England "Charles II of England") called upon [Robert Talbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Talbor "Robert Talbor"), who had become famous for his miraculous malaria cure.[[49]](#cite_note-49) Because at that time the bark was in religious controversy, Talbor gave the king the bitter bark decoction in great secrecy. The treatment gave the king complete relief from the malaria fever. In return, Talbor was offered membership of the prestigious [Royal College of Physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Physicians "Royal College of Physicians").[[50]](#cite_note-Thompson-1928-50)

In 1679, Talbor was called by the King of France, [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV "Louis XIV"), whose son was suffering from malarial fever. After a successful treatment, Talbor was rewarded by the king with 3,000 gold crowns and a lifetime pension for this prescription. Talbor was asked to keep the entire episode secret. After Talbor's death, the French king published this formula: seven grams of rose leaves, two ounces of lemon juice and a strong decoction of the cinchona bark served with wine. Wine was used because some alkaloids of the cinchona bark are not soluble in water, but are soluble in the ethanol in wine.[[50]](#cite_note-Thompson-1928-50) In 1681 *[Água de Inglaterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Água_de_Inglaterra "Água de Inglaterra")* was introduced into Portugal from England by Dr. [Fernando Mendes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Mendes "Fernando Mendes") who, similarly, "received a handsome gift from ([King Pedro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_II_of_Portugal "Peter II of Portugal")) on condition that he should reveal to him the secret of its composition and withhold it from the public".[[51]](#cite_note-Augusto-51)

In 1738, *Sur l'arbre du quinquina*, a paper written by [Charles Marie de La Condamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Marie_de_La_Condamine "Charles Marie de La Condamine"), lead member of the [expedition, along with Pierre Godin and Louis Bouger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Geodesic_Mission "French Geodesic Mission") that was sent to Ecuador to determine the length of a degree of the 1/4 of [meridian arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc "Meridian arc") in the neighbourhood of the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator "Equator"), was published by the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"). In it he identified three separate species.[[52]](#cite_note-52)

### Homeopathy

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Homeopathy")]

The birth of [homeopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeopathy "Homeopathy") was based on cinchona bark testing. The founder of homeopathy, [Samuel Hahnemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Hahnemann "Samuel Hahnemann"), when translating [William Cullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cullen "William Cullen")'s *[Materia medica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materia_medica "Materia medica")*, noticed Cullen had written that Peruvian bark was known to cure intermittent fevers.[[53]](#cite_note-53) Hahnemann took daily a large, rather than homeopathic, dose of Peruvian bark. After two weeks, he said he felt malaria-like symptoms. This idea of "like cures like" was the starting point of his writings on homeopathy. Hahnemann's symptoms have been suggested by researchers, both homeopaths and skeptics, as being an indicator of his hypersensitivity to quinine.[[54]](#cite_note-54)

### Widespread cultivation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Widespread cultivation")]

The bark was very valuable to Europeans in expanding their access to and exploitation of resources in distant colonies and at home. Bark gathering was often environmentally destructive, destroying huge expanses of trees for their bark, with difficult conditions for low wages that did not allow the indigenous bark gatherers to settle debts even upon death.[[55]](#cite_note-55)

Further exploration of the [Amazon Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Basin "Amazon Basin") and the economy of trade in various species of the bark in the 18th century is captured by Lardner Gibbon:

> ... this bark was first gathered in quantities in 1849, though known for many years. The best quality is not quite equal to that of Yungas, but only second to it. There are four other classes of inferior bark, for some of which the bank pays fifteen dollars per quintal. The best, by law, is worth fifty-four dollars. The freight to Arica is seventeen dollars the mule load of three quintals. Six thousand quintals of bark have already been gathered from Yuracares. The bank was established in the year 1851. [Mr. [Thaddäus] Haenke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haenke "Haenke") mentioned the existence of cinchona bark on his visit to Yuracares in 1796
>
> :   — *Exploration of the Valley of the Amazon*, by Lieut. [Lardner Gibbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lardner_Gibbon "Lardner Gibbon"), USN. Vol. II, Ch. 6, pp. 146–47.

It was estimated that the British Empire incurred direct losses of 52 to 62 million pounds a year due to malaria sickness each year. It was therefore of great importance to secure the supply of the cure.[[56]](#cite_note-56) In 1860, a British expedition to South America led by [Clements Markham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clements_Markham "Clements Markham") smuggled back cinchona seeds and plants, which were [introduced in several areas of British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_cultivation_in_British_India "Cinchona cultivation in British India") and Sri Lanka. In India, it was planted in Ootacamund by [William Graham McIvor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Graham_McIvor "William Graham McIvor"). In Sri Lanka, it was planted in the [Hakgala Botanical Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakgala_Botanical_Garden "Hakgala Botanical Garden") in January 1861.[[57]](#cite_note-57) [James Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Taylor_(Ceylon) "James Taylor (Ceylon)"), the pioneer of tea planting in Sri Lanka, was one of the pioneers of cinchona cultivation.[[58]](#cite_note-timesonline-58) By 1883, about 64,000 acres (260 km2) were in cultivation in Sri Lanka, with exports reaching a peak of 15 million pounds in 1886. The cultivation (initially of *[C. succirubra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_succirubra "Cinchona succirubra")* (now *C. pubescens*) and later of *C. calisaya*[[59]](#cite_note-59)) was extended through the work of [George King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_King_(botanist) "George King (botanist)") and others into the hilly terrain of [Darjeeling District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darjeeling_District "Darjeeling District") of [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal "West Bengal"). Cinchona factories were established at Naduvattam in the Nilgiris and at [Mungpoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mungpoo "Mungpoo"), Darjeeling, West Bengal. Quinologists were appointed to oversee the extraction of alkaloids with John Broughton in the Nilgiris and C.H. Wood at Darjeeling. Others in the position included David Hooper and John Eliot Howard.[[33]](#cite_note-Williams-1962-33)[[60]](#cite_note-60)[[61]](#cite_note-61)

As Western nations gained control over cinchona from plantations beyond South America, countries the medicinal plant was native to, including Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia, lost their monopoly.[[34]](#cite_note-:7-34) The overseas cinchona market produced vast wealth for European countries, and enforced trade dynamics of imperial nations having power over the Global South, even once colonies were independent nations.[[4]](#cite_note-:6-4)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Cinchona.pubescens03.jpg/250px-Cinchona.pubescens03.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cinchona.pubescens03.jpg)

*[C. pubescens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_pubescens "Cinchona pubescens")* fruit

In 1865, "New Virginia" and "Carlota Colony" were established in [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico") by [Matthew Fontaine Maury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Fontaine_Maury "Matthew Fontaine Maury"), a former Confederate in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War "American Civil War"). Postwar Confederates were enticed there by Maury, now the "Imperial Commissioner of Immigration" for [Emperor Maximillian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_I_of_Mexico "Maximilian I of Mexico") of Mexico, and Archduke of Habsburg. All that survives of those two colonies are the flourishing groves of *cinchonas* established by Maury using seeds purchased from England. These seeds were the first to be introduced into Mexico.[[62]](#cite_note-62)

The cultivation of cinchona led from the 1890s to a decline in the price of quinine, but the quality and production of raw bark by the Dutch in Indonesia led them to dominate world markets. The producers of processed drugs in Europe (especially Germany[[63]](#cite_note-63)), however, bargained and caused fluctuations in prices, which led to a Dutch-led Cinchona Agreement in 1913 that ensured a fixed price for producers. A *[Kina Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kina_Bureau "Kina Bureau")* in Amsterdam regulated this trade.[[64]](#cite_note-64)

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), the Japanese conquered Java and the United States lost access to the cinchona plantations that supplied war-critical quinine medication. Botanical expeditions called [Cinchona Missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona_Missions "Cinchona Missions")[[65]](#cite_note-Cinchona-SI-65) were launched between 1942 and 1944 to explore promising areas of South America in an effort to locate cinchona species that contained quinine and could be harvested for quinine production.[[65]](#cite_note-Cinchona-SI-65) As well as being ultimately successful in their primary aim, these expeditions also identified new species of plants[[65]](#cite_note-Cinchona-SI-65) and created a new chapter in international relations between the United States and other nations in the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas").[[66]](#cite_note-Dynamis-66)

## Chemistry

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Chemistry")]

### Cinchona alkaloids

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Cinchona alkaloids")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Cinchona_alkaloids.svg/250px-Cinchona_alkaloids.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cinchona_alkaloids.svg)

General structure of *Cinchona* alkaloids

The bark of trees in this genus is the source of a variety of [alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloid "Alkaloid"), the most familiar of which is [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine"), an [antipyretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipyretic "Antipyretic") (antifever) agent especially useful in treating malaria.[[67]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-67)[[68]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEA1920-68) For a while the extraction of a mixture of alkaloids from the cinchona bark, known in India as the cinchona febrifuge, was used. The alkaloid mixture or its sulphated form mixed in alcohol and sold as quinetum was however very bitter and caused nausea, among other side effects.[[69]](#cite_note-69)

Cinchona alkaloids include:

* [cinchonine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchonine "Cinchonine") and [cinchonidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchonidine "Cinchonidine") ([stereoisomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoisomer "Stereoisomer") with R1 = [vinyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinyl_group "Vinyl group"), R2 = [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen"))
* [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine "Quinine") and [quinidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinidine "Quinidine") ([stereoisomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoisomer "Stereoisomer") with R1 = vinyl, R2 = [methoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methoxy "Methoxy"))
* [dihydroquinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydroquinine "Dihydroquinine") and [dihydroquinidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydroquinidine "Dihydroquinidine") (stereoisomers with R1 = [ethyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethyl_group "Ethyl group"), R2 = methoxy)

They find use in [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry") as [organocatalysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organocatalyst "Organocatalyst") in [asymmetric synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetric_synthesis "Asymmetric synthesis").

### Other chemicals

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Cinchona&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Other chemicals")]

Alongside the alkaloids, many cinchona barks contain [cinchotannic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchotannic_acid "Cinchotannic acid"), a particular [tannin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannin "Tannin"), which by oxidation rapidly yields a dark-coloured phlobaphene[[70]](#cite_note-70) called red cinchonic,[[71]](#cite_note-71) cinchono-fulvic acid, or cinchona red.[[72]](#cite_note-72)

In 1934, efforts to make malaria drugs cheap and effective for use across countries led to the development of a standard called "totaquina" proposed by the Malaria Commission of the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations "League of Nations"). Totaquina required a minimum of 70% crystallizable alkaloids, of which at least 15% was to be quinine with not more than 20% amorphous alkaloids.[[73]](#cite_note-73)[[74]](#cite_note-74)
