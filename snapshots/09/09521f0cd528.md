## March 1999, Volume 6, Number 3

### Turkey, Greece, Cyprus

Turkey. Abdullah Ocalan, the 50-year old leader of the Kurdish Workers' Party (PKK), was abducted by Turkish commandos in Kenya on February 15, 1999 after spending 12 days at the Greek ambassador's residence in Nairobi and flown to Turkey to stand trial. The US told Turkey that Ocalan was in Kenya. Turkish President Suleyman Demirel said that "Greece should be added to the list of countries that support terrorism and harbor terrorists. A country like that can only be described as an outlaw state." Greece countered that Turkey was trying to divert attention from its own "human rights abuses."

In October 1998, Syria, under Turkish military threats, ordered Ocalan out. He arrived in Italy via Moscow. Ocalan was permitted to leave Italy on January 16, 1999, after Italy refused to extradite him over to Turkey for trial because Turkey has the death penalty. Ocalan apparently moved between airports until Greece sheltered him in Kenya.

The arrest of Ocalan led to protests by Turks in many European cities against Greece. In Berlin, three Kurds were killed in an attempted takeover of the Israeli consulate; they believed that Israel had played a role in helping Turkey to capture Ocalan. In response to the demonstrations, the German CDU-CSU renewed the party's call for the deportation of foreigners convicted of crimes that carry sentences of one year or more in prison.

An estimated 500,000 of the 2.2 million Turks in Germany are Kurds. Chancellor Gerhard Schroeder said: "We cannot tolerate it when conflicts take place on German streets that are not our conflicts," and promised to deport foreigners who commit serious crimes in Germany.

The Turkish government sees the "Kurdish problem" as a rebellion that must be quashed rather than as the demands of a minority for minority rights. Turkey says that it has taped evidence that Ocalan ordered the murder of Turkish teachers in the southeast and the killing of civilians of residents who did not support Kurdish autonomy; 134 teachers have been killed to prevent "indoctrination" by the estimated 5,000 PKK fighters.

Ocalan first launched raids in southeastern Turkey from Iraq in 1984. In the 20 years of fighting in southeast Turkey, some 3,000 villages have been destroyed, in some cases by Turkish bombs, and two million villagers have been resettled; 30,000 people have died in the fighting. There has been massive emigration from the Kurdish region of southeastern Turkey; many of the Turks in Western Europe are Kurdish.

Neutral observers say that there is blame on both sides. One said: "The past seven governments, since 1990, have promised the southeast a whole list of economic and social improvements, an end to torture, the right of return to the villages. But they've done nothing. There's no trust left in the state, and that's what keeps the rebellion going."

In January 1999, Turkey's chief prosecutor, Vural Savas, demanded that the People's Democracy Party, which has elected representatives in Turkey's federal parliament, be outlawed because it a "political front" for the PKK. The next general elections are scheduled for April 18, 1999, and some interpreted the move to outlaw the PDP as an effort to reduce the party's vote in the next election; parties must receive 10 percent of the vote to enter Parliament. Turkey celebrated the 75th anniversary of the founding of the Turkish Republic in 1998.

The 20 million Kurds are the largest distinctive ethnic group living across more than one state and without a state of their own. Other groups without nations include Palestinians, Basques, Kashmiris and Tamils.

Greece. Greek police in February 1999 arrested a smuggler who was wanted in connection with the Christmas Day 1996 drowning of 280 migrants in the channel between Sicily and Malta. Greek authorities said the Pakistanis, Indians and Sri Lankans had paid between $5,000 and $8,000 each for flights to Egypt and a boat trip to Europe.

Cyprus. Cyprus will prosecute for negligence the police commander in charge when a riot in October, 1998 resulted in the hospitalization of several illegal migrants. The Attorney General announced that three Indians, four Nigerians and five Kurds would remain in Cyprus to testify about the incident; they will receive work permits until the trial ends. An additional 30 Nigerians were paid $300 to $1,000 for mistreatment in the October 23 incident, and were then deported.

The migrants, who were protesting poor conditions in the jail where they were being held, were beaten by police. The beatings were captured on video, and showed detainees clutching their heads as riot police beat them with batons. Policemen are not being charged because they could not be individually identified; their faces were hidden by gas masks. A detention center for illegal immigrants has been set up inside the Nicosia Central Prison.
