### What was the Scramble for Africa?

The [Scramble for Africa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Scramble-for-Africa) refers to the period from the late 19th to the early 20th century when European imperial powers claimed control of most African territory. It is also used to describe the actions undertaken by those countries with the goals of expanding strategic territorial claims and securing access to valuable natural resources and trade routes. In the 1870s only 10 percent of African territory was controlled by European countries, but by 1914 about 90 percent of African territory had been incorporated into one European empire or another, including the land of every present-day country on the continent except Ethiopia and Liberia.

### What seven countries were in the Scramble for Africa?

During the [Scramble for Africa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Scramble-for-Africa), when European imperial powers effectively partitioned the African continent, seven European countries claimed colonial holdings that, by the end of the Scramble, accounted for about 90 percent of Africa. The seven countries were Belgium, France, Germany, Great Britain, Portugal, Italy, and Spain. By the end of the Scramble, France and Great Britain had claimed the most territory in Africa.

### What role did the Berlin Conference play in the Scramble for Africa?

In the early stages of the [Scramble for Africa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Scramble-for-Africa), the [Berlin Conference](https://www.britannica.com/event/Berlin-West-Africa-Conference) (1884–85) provided a framework for European “effective occupation” of African territory and recognized spheres of influence on the continent. Representatives from 14 countries from Europe and beyond attended the conference, though only half of them—Belgium, France, Germany, Great Britain, Portugal, Italy, and Spain—already had, or would go on to have, recognized colonial holdings in Africa. Though the European “scramble” for African territory had already begun years earlier, the pace of colonization greatly accelerated after the conference.

The Berlin Conference provided a veneer of legality and orderliness to European actions in Africa over the next decades, though historians now view it as a cover for blatant profiteering and the avaricious exercise of imperial ambitions that were at the heart of the Scramble.

### What were the main motivations behind the Scramble for Africa?

A number of factors drove the [Scramble for Africa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Scramble-for-Africa). Primary motivations included competition among European powers, nationalist movements fueling imperial expansion, and the quest for economic profits, from both the extraction of natural resources in the colonies and the potential market expansion into the colonies. There was also a widely held belief in racial superiority among European societies, which was often used to justify colonization efforts as “civilizing missions.”

## Origins

[![King of the Zulu](https://cdn.britannica.com/92/141592-050-F7348B96/Cetshwayo-guard-British-Zulu-Southern-Africa-1879.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/92/141592-050-F7348B96/Cetshwayo-guard-British-Zulu-Southern-Africa-1879.jpg)

[King of the Zulu](https://cdn.britannica.com/92/141592-050-F7348B96/Cetshwayo-guard-British-Zulu-Southern-Africa-1879.jpg) Cetshwayo, king of the Zulu, under British guard after the end of the Anglo-Zulu War in Southern Africa, 1879.(more)

European interest in Africa was stimulated by 19th-century expeditions into the interior of the continent undertaken by European explorers, two of the most famous being [David Livingstone](https://www.britannica.com/biography/David-Livingstone) and [Henry Morton Stanley](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Henry-Morton-Stanley). These [expeditions](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/expeditions) led to the dissemination of information about the continent’s [geography](https://www.britannica.com/science/geography), natural resources, and commercial potential. This information, together with European industrial and military advantages and growing strategic and economic competition among European powers, encouraged further intervention on the continent. The completion of the [Suez Canal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Suez-Canal) in Egypt in 1869 and subsequent French-British ownership of the canal’s operating company as well as the victory of [Great Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom) in the [Anglo-Zulu War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Anglo-Zulu-War) in 1879, illustrated the growing strategic importance of Africa and foreshadowed the rapid territorial expansion that became known as the Scramble for Africa.

## The Scramble begins

In the 1880s various European empires began taking steps to secure and expand their territorial control in Africa. For example, [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) and [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy) expanded their holdings in the areas now known as [Senegal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Senegal), [Tunisia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tunisia), and [Eritrea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Eritrea), while in 1882 Great Britain began a military occupation of [Egypt](https://www.britannica.com/place/Egypt) to protect the imperial power’s financial interests in that country.

King [Leopold II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leopold-II-king-of-Belgium) of [Belgium](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgium) is often considered to be one of the principal figures who nudged the previously piecemeal process of colonization into a competitive multinational enterprise. Under the cover of securing trade along the [Congo River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-River) in the interior of Africa, by 1884 Leopold’s agents of the [International Association of the Congo](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Association-Internationale-du-Congo) had negotiated for territorial control—or what they interpreted as territorial control—with 450 local peoples and entities. The resulting treaties ostensibly gave Leopold’s association direct control over a vast swathe of [sub-Saharan Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/sub-Saharan-Africa), which the king claimed as his [sovereign](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereign) territory. But controversy ensued because Leopold’s control of this territory conflicted with an agreement between [Great Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom) and [Portugal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Portugal) regarding their right to operate along the Congo River.

The German chancellor and [prime minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/prime-minister) [Otto von Bismarck](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Otto-von-Bismarck) is also considered to be one of the leaders who accelerated the Scramble, stemming from his annexation of [Togo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Togo), [Cameroon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cameroon/German-Kamerun-1884-1916#ref281016), and [Angra Pequena](https://www.britannica.com/place/Luderitz) in 1884 as well as from his proposal for a conference to be held by the European powers regarding their African claims. That proposal resulted in the [Berlin Conference](https://www.britannica.com/event/Berlin-West-Africa-Conference) (also called the Berlin West Africa Conference), which was held from November 15, 1884, to February 26, 1885.

Representatives from 14 countries from Europe and beyond [attended](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/attended) the conference, though only half of them—Belgium, France, [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany), Great Britain, Portugal, Italy, and Spain—already had, or would go on to have, recognized colonial holdings in Africa. The conference resolved the immediate issue of Leopold’s claim by recognizing the [Congo Free State](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-Free-State) as being under his control, though he was required to agree to [free trade](https://www.britannica.com/money/free-trade) along rivers in his territory. The conference attendees also agreed on a common framework for the recognition of European “effective occupation” of African territory and on the principle that control of territory could not be claimed until said territory was actually occupied. Spheres of influence were recognized—though not fixed—and both the [Niger](https://www.britannica.com/place/Niger-River) and [Congo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-River) rivers were declared free waters. These agreements were formalized in the General Act of the Berlin Conference on [West Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/western-Africa), signed on February 26, 1885.

## Motivations

A number of factors drove the Scramble after the Berlin Conference legitimized the process of imperial expansion. One was straightforward competition: Each country feared that, if it did not participate in the Scramble, another would [accrue](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/accrue) the benefits of colonization that it could have had. Imperial expansion also played into powerful [nationalist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nationalism) movements within European countries. Germany and Italy had only recently unified, and both sought to demonstrate their status—and implicitly, legitimacy—as great powers through colonial expansion.

Perhaps the factor most commonly identified as truly motivating the Scramble for Africa is the [monetary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/monetary) profits from colonization. This did play an important role, and in cases such as [Leopold](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leopold-II-king-of-Belgium)’s, it was the main reason for colonization. Industrialization had created growing demand for many natural resources found in Africa, such as rubber, palm oil, and [copper](https://www.britannica.com/science/copper), and European industrial countries wanted to gain access to natural resources that could be refined into more-profitable commodities—a form of exploitation that provoked a key [criticism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/criticism) of [capitalism](https://www.britannica.com/money/capitalism) in the ensuing decades. Colonial expansion was also presented as a form of market expansion, in which conquered countries acted as captive markets for exports from the imperial center. However, much of this profit was either concentrated in the hands of those who gained monopolies from colonial governments or largely [rhetorical](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/rhetorical), as the costs of running a [colony](https://www.britannica.com/topic/colony-ancient-Roman-settlement) usually outweighed the profits that [accrued](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/accrued) to the colonizing power.

Another important [impetus](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/impetus) for European countries to participate in the Scramble was the complex interplay of [nationalism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nationalism), the growth of [social Darwinism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-Darwinism) and [eugenics](https://www.britannica.com/science/eugenics-genetics), and a sense of [racial](https://www.britannica.com/topic/racism) superiority within European societies, all of which led to widespread popular support for imperial expansion. One of the justifications for the Berlin Conference had in fact been to end the international [slave trade](https://www.britannica.com/topic/transatlantic-slave-trade) in Congo territory, and expansion of colonial territory in Africa was championed by social reformers and many European governments as a “civilizing mission,” through which African populations would be Christianized and therefore “civilized.” The movement of peoples and information between [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa) and [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) triggered by the Scramble formed a rhetorical echo chamber that reinforced Europeans’ ideas of their racial and cultural superiority. Perhaps the [pinnacle](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/pinnacle) of this process was the practice of holding exhibitions in which members of [Indigenous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Indigenous) African groups were displayed like zoo animals. Some early practitioners of [anthropology](https://www.britannica.com/science/anthropology) also played a key role in fueling public belief in the [moral](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/moral) legitimacy of the Scramble for Africa. Literature too perpetuated the idea of the civilizing mission of European powers in Africa, popularized in such works as the [Rudyard Kipling](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Rudyard-Kipling) poem “The White Man’s Burden.”

## Effects and legacy

[![Illustration from Le Petit Journal showing King Menelik II in a red cloak and headdress riding a white horse as he surveys Ethiopian troops at the Battle of Adwa.](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/273503-050-451DC256/King-Melenik-II-of-Ethiopia-during-Battle-of-Adwa-1896.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/273503-050-451DC256/King-Melenik-II-of-Ethiopia-during-Battle-of-Adwa-1896.jpg)

[Battle of Adwa](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/273503-050-451DC256/King-Melenik-II-of-Ethiopia-during-Battle-of-Adwa-1896.jpg) Ethiopian Emperor Menilek II surveying his troops at the Battle of Adwa on March 1, 1896, in which Ethiopia defeated Italy, from _Le Petit Journal_, November 10, 1898.(more)

African societies resisted European expansion in many different ways, although military advantages often enabled colonial powers to prevail. One notable example to the contrary is the [Battle of Adwa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Adwa) (1896), in which the Ethiopian army of Emperor [Menilek II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Menilek-II) triumphed over Italian forces after Italy tried to claim [Ethiopia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ethiopia) as a [protectorate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/protectorate-international-relations).

[![Battle of Modder River](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/141591-050-9B70753E/troops-British-river-Battle-of-Modder-River-November-28-1899.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/141591-050-9B70753E/troops-British-river-Battle-of-Modder-River-November-28-1899.jpg)

[Battle of Modder River](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/141591-050-9B70753E/troops-British-river-Battle-of-Modder-River-November-28-1899.jpg) British troops wading through the river at the Battle of Modder River, November 28, 1899, during the South African War (1899–1902).(more)

The Scramble for Africa resulted in the creation of dozens of polities and innumerable treaties, and from this several events emerged as pivotal in international politics, including the [Fashoda incident](https://www.britannica.com/event/Fashoda-Incident) (1898), the [Moroccan crises](https://www.britannica.com/event/Moroccan-crises) (1905–06, 1911), and the wars between the British and the [Boers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Boer-people) in [Southern Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Southern-Africa). In the Fashoda crisis, conflicting claims over lines of influence in Africa between British and French troops came to a head near a fort at Fashoda (located in what is now [South Sudan](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Sudan)). The standoff raised the [specter](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/specter) of a European war as a result of imperial jockeying, and it formed the basis of the [Entente Cordiale](https://www.britannica.com/event/Entente-Cordiale) (1904). In 1905 Germany challenged this treaty and agreements from the Berlin Conference when it tried to exert influence over [Morocco](https://www.britannica.com/place/Morocco), which was recognized by other European powers as falling under French control. This First Moroccan Crisis (1905–06)—and the [Second Moroccan Crisis](https://www.britannica.com/event/Agadir-Incident) (1911)—cemented ties between [Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom) and France as well as engendering hostility between them and Germany. These international relationships would play a key role in [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I) (1914–18). Finally, the 1902 British victory over the two [Boer](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Boer-people) republics in the [South African War](https://www.britannica.com/event/South-African-War) (1899–1902) allowed that empire to [consolidate](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/consolidate) its control over the strategically key southern region of the continent.

The effects of the Scramble for Africa on African peoples themselves were devastating, and they continue to be felt to the present day. For instance, it is estimated that as much as half the population of Leopold’s [Congo Free State](https://www.britannica.com/place/Congo-Free-State) died under his brutal and deadly rule. Indeed, his mismanagement was such that the [Belgian government assumed responsibility for the state in 1908](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgian-Congo). In another example, from 1904 to 1907 the German colonial government undertook the [genocide](https://www.britannica.com/topic/genocide) of the [Herero](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Herero) and [Nama](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nama-people) peoples in [German South West Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/German-South-West-Africa) ( _see_ [German-Herero conflict of 1904–07](https://www.britannica.com/topic/German-Herero-conflict-of-1904-1907)), doing incalculable harm to those societies. Some studies have shown that the borders put in place by colonial powers that became the basis for national borders in the 20th century have had a measurable negative impact on the stability of various countries.

Also, Indigenous languages, traditions, economic models, and modes of administration were typically overshadowed, if not completely replaced, by those of the colonizing countries, the effects of which can still be seen today. Most [sub-Saharan](https://www.britannica.com/place/sub-Saharan-Africa) countries have either English, French, or Portuguese as an official language as a result of colonial administration, and countries have had to adapt to or change the economic [infrastructure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/infrastructure) established during the colonial period. Thus, even more than a century after the Scramble for Africa ended, it continues to have an impact on the world.

### What is imperialism in history?

Imperialism is the state policy, practice, or advocacy of extending power and dominion, especially by direct territorial acquisition or by gaining political and economic control of other territories and peoples. Because it always involves the use of power, whether military or economic or some subtler form, imperialism has often been considered morally reprehensible. Examples from history include [Greek](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Greece) imperialism under [Alexander the Great](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-the-Great) and [Italian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy) imperialism under [Benito Mussolini](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benito-Mussolini).

### Does imperialism still exist today?

Today the term _imperialism_ is commonly used in international propaganda to denounce and discredit an opponent’s [foreign policy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/foreign-policy). International organizations, including the United Nations, attempt to maintain peace using measures such as collective security arrangements and aid to developing countries. However, critics say imperialism exists today; for example, many in the Middle East saw the [U.S.](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States)-led [Iraq War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Iraq-War) as a new brand of anti-Arab and anti-Islamic imperialism.

### Did imperialism cause World War I?

Following the opening of the [Suez Canal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Suez-Canal) in 1869, European nations sought to extend their economic and political power overseas, especially in Africa, in a period dubbed “the [New Imperialism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/20th-century-international-relations-2085155).” This competition led European elites and the broad literate classes to believe that the old European [balance of power](https://www.britannica.com/topic/balance-of-power) was over and a new world order was dawning. Some scholars argue that this process intensified imperial rivalries and helped provoke [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I).

**imperialism**, state policy, practice, or [advocacy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/advocacy) of extending power and dominion, especially by direct territorial acquisition or by gaining political and economic control of other areas. Because it always involves the use of power, whether military or economic or some subtler form, imperialism has often been considered morally reprehensible, and the term is frequently employed in international [propaganda](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/propaganda) to denounce and discredit an opponent’s [foreign policy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/foreign-policy).

[![Detail from Charles Le Brun's painting showing Alexander the Great in profile, wearing a gold helmet with white plumes and a blue cloak, with soldiers in the background.](https://cdn.britannica.com/95/121095-050-21005309/Alexander-the-Great-detail-painting-Porus-Charles.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/95/121095-050-21005309/Alexander-the-Great-detail-painting-Porus-Charles.jpg)

[Alexander the Great](https://cdn.britannica.com/95/121095-050-21005309/Alexander-the-Great-detail-painting-Porus-Charles.jpg) A painting showing Alexander the Great dressed for battle.(more)

Imperialism in ancient times is clear in the [history of China](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-China) and in the history of western [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia) and the Mediterranean—an unending succession of empires. The tyrannical [empire](https://www.britannica.com/topic/empire-political-science) of the [Assyrians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Assyria) was replaced (6th–4th century bce) by that of the [Persians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Persia), in strong contrast to the Assyrian in its liberal treatment of subjected peoples, assuring it long duration. It eventually gave way to the imperialism of [Greece](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Greece). When Greek imperialism reached an apex under [Alexander the Great](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-the-Great) (356–323 bce), a union of the eastern Mediterranean with western Asia was achieved. But the cosmopolis, in which all citizens of the world would live harmoniously together in equality, remained a dream of Alexander. It was partially realized when the Romans built their empire from [Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom) to [Egypt](https://www.britannica.com/place/Egypt).

This idea of empire as a unifying force was never again realized after the fall of Rome. The nations arising from the ashes of the [Roman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Roman-Empire) in Europe, and in Asia on the common basis of Islamic civilization ( _see_ [Islamic world](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islamic-world)), pursued their individual imperialist policies. Imperialism became a [divisive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/divisive) force among the peoples of the world.

Three periods in the modern era witnessed the creation of vast empires, primarily colonial. Between the 15th century and the middle of the 18th, England, France, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain built empires in the Americas, [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India), and the [East Indies](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Indies). For almost a century thereafter, relative calm in empire building reigned as the result of a strong reaction against imperialism. Then the decades between the middle of the 19th century and [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I) (1914–18) were again characterized by intense imperialistic policies.

[Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia), Italy, Germany, the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), and [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) were added as newcomers among the imperialistic states, and indirect, especially financial, control became a preferred form of imperialism. For a decade after World War I the great expectations for a better world inspired by the [League of Nations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/League-of-Nations) put the problem of imperialism once more in [abeyance](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/abeyance). Then Japan renewed its empire building with an attack in 1931 upon China. Under the leadership of Japan and the [totalitarian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/totalitarianism) states—Italy under the [Fascist Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Fascist-Party), [Nazi](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nazi-Party) Germany, and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union)—a new period of imperialism was inaugurated in the 1930s and ’40s.

In their modern form, arguments about the causes and value of imperialism can be classified into four main groups. The first group contains economic arguments and often turn around the question of whether or not imperialism pays. Those who argue that it does point to the human and material resources and the outlets for goods, investment capital, and [surplus](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/surplus) population provided by an empire. Their opponents—among them [Adam Smith](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Adam-Smith), [David Ricardo](https://www.britannica.com/money/David-Ricardo), and J.A. Hobson—often assert that imperialism may benefit a small favoured group but never the nation as a whole. [Marxist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Marxism) theoreticians interpret imperialism as a late stage of [capitalism](https://www.britannica.com/money/capitalism) wherein the national capitalist economy has become [monopolistic](https://www.britannica.com/money/monopoly-economics) and is forced to conquer outlets for its overproduction and surplus capital in competition with other capitalist states. This was the view held, for instance, by [Vladimir Lenin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Lenin) and [N.I. Bukharin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nikolay-Ivanovich-Bukharin), for whom capitalism and imperialism were identical. The weakness in their view is that historical evidence does not support it and that it fails to explain precapitalist imperialism and [communist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/communism) imperialism.

A second group of arguments relates imperialism to the [nature of human beings](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-nature) and human groups, such as the [state](https://www.britannica.com/topic/state-sovereign-political-entity). Such different personalities as [Machiavelli](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Niccolo-Machiavelli), [Sir Francis Bacon](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Francis-Bacon-Viscount-Saint-Alban), and [Ludwig Gumplowicz](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ludwig-Gumplowicz), reasoning on different grounds, nevertheless arrived at similar conclusions—which [Adolf Hitler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Adolf-Hitler) and [Benito Mussolini](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benito-Mussolini) also [endorsed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/endorsed), though not for [intellectual](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intellectual) reasons. Imperialism for them is part of the natural struggle for survival. Those endowed with superior qualities are destined to rule all others.

The third group of arguments has to do with strategy and security. Nations are urged, proponents of this viewpoint say, to obtain bases, strategic materials, buffer states, “natural” frontiers, and control of communication lines for reasons of security or to prevent other states from obtaining them. Those who deny the value of imperialism for these purposes point out that security is not thereby achieved. Expansion of a state’s control over territories and peoples beyond its borders is likely to lead to friction, hence insecurity, because the safety zones and spheres of influence of competing nations are bound to overlap sooner or later. Related to the security argument is the argument that nations are inevitably imperialistic in their natural search for power and [prestige](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/prestige).

The fourth group of arguments is based on [moral](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/moral) grounds, sometimes with strong missionary [implications](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implications). Imperialism is excused as the means of liberating peoples from tyrannical rule or of bringing them the blessings of a superior way of life. Imperialism results from a complex of causes in which in varying degrees economic pressures, human aggressiveness and greed, the search for security, the drive for power and prestige, nationalist emotions, humanitarianism, and many other factors are effective. This mixture of motivations makes it difficult to eliminate imperialism but also easy for states considering themselves potential victims to suspect it in policies not intended to be imperialistic. Some states of the developing world have accused the former colonial powers and other nations of [neocolonialism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/neocolonialism). Their fear is that the granting of aid or the supply of skilled personnel for economic and technical development might be an imperialist guise.

Under [international organizations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/international-organization), attempts have been made to satisfy by peaceful means the [legitimate](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legitimate) [aspirations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/aspirations) of nations and to contain their [illegitimate](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/illegitimate) ones. Measures for these purposes have included [collective security](https://www.britannica.com/topic/collective-security) arrangements, the [mandate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mandate-League-of-Nations) and the trusteeship system for dependent areas, the stimulation of cultural relations between nations, aid to developing countries, and the improvement of health and welfare everywhere. _See also_ [colonialism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism).
