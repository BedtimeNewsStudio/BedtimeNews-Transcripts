## 1\. Introduction

We welcome feedback on our statistics to ensure they meet user needs. To share your views, complete our short [User Engagement Survey](https://ditresearch.eu.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_5bCSO89yi4X6CLI).

For any other comments or questions contact us at: [statistics@businessandtrade.gov.uk](mailto:statistics@businessandtrade.gov.uk)

This publication covers and summarises the wide range of trade and investment statistics produced by the Office for National Statistics (ONS), HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC), the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), and statistics produced earlier in the year by the former Department for Business and Trade (DBT). Bringing together into a single user-friendly publication with the aim of helping users to quickly find the most relevant and up-to-date trade and investment statistics.

Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST) publishes a range of trade and investment statistics compendiums. For a summary of the latest headline statistics, in a simple visual format, see the [BIST UK trade in numbers](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/uk-trade-in-numbers). For a summary of the latest trade and investment statistics between the UK and each individual overseas partner, see the [BIST trade and investment factsheets](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/trade-and-investment-factsheets).

More information on the data sources used in this publication are available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book).

The latest publication of the Core Statistics Book has been updated with data from:

- [ONS imports and exports of services by modes of supply UK: 2024](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/internationaltrade/datasets/importsofservicesbycountrybymodesofsupply)

- [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)

- [ONSGDP first quarterly estimate, UK: July to September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/gdpfirstquarterlyestimateuk/previousreleases)

- [OBR Economic and fiscal outlook: October 2026](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-october-2026/)

- [ONS UK trade, quarterly trade in goods and services tables: April to June 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/datasets/uktotaltradeallcountriesseasonallyadjusted)

- [UNCTAD Foreign direct investment: Inward and outward flows and stock, annual](https://unctadstat.unctad.org/datacentre/dataviewer/US.FdiFlowsStock)

The next published update to the Core Statistics Book will be 18 December 2026.

Our statistical practice is regulated by the Office for Statistics Regulation (OSR). OSR sets the standards of trustworthiness, quality and value in the [Code of Practice for Statistics](https://code.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/the-code/) that all producers of official statistics should adhere to.

You are welcome to contact us directly with any comments about how we meet these standards. Alternatively, you can contact OSR by emailing [regulation@statistics.gov.uk](mailto:regulation@statistics.gov.uk) or via the [OSR website](https://osr.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/).

### 2.1 Total UK (goods and services) exports

All export statistics in this section are published by the ONS. Full details on how the data is sourced and the statistics are compiled is available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book).

The value of UK exports was £913.2 billion in 2025, up 3.5% on 2024. This reflects a 1.2% decrease of goods exports and a 7.3% increase of services exports. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

More timely data shows the value of UK exports was £956.8 billion in the 12 months to September 2026, up 6.2% on the 12 months to September 2025. This reflects a 5.7% increase of goods exports and a 6.5% increase of services exports. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

### 2.2 UK goods exports

All goods export statistics in this section are published by the ONS. Full details on how the data is sourced and the statistics are compiled is available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book#trade-in-goods).

In 2025, goods exports provided 42.7% of total UK exports, compared with 52.4% in 2015. More timely data shows the share was 43.0% in the 12 months ending September 2026. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

Exports of goods were £390.4 billion in 2025, down 1.2% since 2024. More timely data shows the value of UK goods exports was £411.4 billion in the 12 months ending September 2026, up 5.7% on the previous 12 months. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

#### UK goods exports to the EU and non-EU countries

( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

UK exports of goods to the EU were £181.9 billion in 2025, down 0.4% since 2024, while UK exports of goods to non-EU countries were £208.5 billion, down 1.9%. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

More timely data shows that goods exports to the EU were £193.5 billion in the 12 months ending September 2026, up 6.4% on the previous 12 months, while goods exports to non-EU countries were £217.9 billion, up 5.0%. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases)).

### 4.3 Trade asymmetries

Trade asymmetries are the differences between the published trade data of a given country and that of its partner countries. Each country collects their own data on international trade. In theory, each country’s exports to a partner country should match the partner country’s imports from them, and vice versa. In reality, it is often the case that figures do not match exactly. We refer to these differences as trade asymmetries.

Full details on trade asymmetries are available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book#trade-asymmetries).

### 4.4 Trade to GDP ratio

The trade-to-GDP ratio is used to measure the importance of international transactions relative to domestic transactions. This indicator is calculated as exports and/or imports relative to GDP. This ratio is often called the trade openness ratio, and can be used as a measure of the extent to which a country is engaged in the global trading system.

UK total trade was equivalent to 61.3% of UK GDP in 2025, down from 61.5% in 2024. In the 12 months ending September 2026, total trade was equivalent to 62.7% of UK GDP, up from 61.0% in the 12 months prior. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases) and [ONSGDP first quarterly estimate, UK: July to September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/gdpfirstquarterlyestimateuk/previousreleases)).

UK exports were equivalent to 30.0% of UK GDP in 2025, down from 30.4% in 2024. In the 12 months ending September 2026, exports were equivalent to 30.5% of UK GDP, up from 29.9% in the 12 months prior. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases) and [ONSGDP first quarterly estimate, UK: July to September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/gdpfirstquarterlyestimateuk/previousreleases)).

UK imports were equivalent to 31.3% of UK GDP in 2025, up from 31.1% in 2024. In the 12 months ending September 2026, imports were equivalent to 32.2% of UK GDP, up from 31.1% in the 12 months prior. ( [ONS UK trade: September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/balanceofpayments/bulletins/uktrade/previousreleases) and [ONSGDP first quarterly estimate, UK: July to September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/gdpfirstquarterlyestimateuk/previousreleases)).

### 4.5 Trade in real terms

UK trade statistics are typically reported in current (or nominal) prices, which measures the market prices during the reported period and are not adjusted for inflation. Statistics are also available in chained volume measures (CVM), which are a ‘real’ measure in that they have had the effect of inflation removed to measure the change in volume between consecutive periods, fixing the prices of goods and services in one period (known as the base year, which is currently 2024 for UK trade).

Full details on the methodology used to compile the trade figures in CVM are available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book#reporting-methodology).

### 7.1 Introduction

The OECD has revised its Trade in Value Added (TiVA) statistics for 2021 and 2022.

TiVA is an innovative dataset that makes it possible to examine aspects of international trade, such as Global Value Chains (GVCs), that are not possible with conventional (‘gross’) measures of trade. The novelty of this dataset is that it regards international trade as flows of value added rather than gross flows of final goods and services, enabling users to track the origin of value added in a country’s exports and final demand by source country and sector.

While TiVA offers many advantages over traditional ways of measuring trade, it relies on several strong assumptions, imputations and modelling, therefore the figures in this section should be interpreted as estimates rather than exact measures.

For more information on the TiVA data see the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book#organisation-for-economic-co-operation-and-development-oecd-trade-in-value-added-tiva--ad-hoc).

### 7.2 Backward linkages for exports

Backward linkages for exports measure how dependent UK exporters are on imported inputs for the production of their exports. A headline measure of backward linkages, and a gauge of the extent of a country’s participation in GVCs, is the share of foreign value added embodied in a country’s gross exports (or import content of exports).

The TiVA data indicates that the UK has relatively weak backward linkages meaning that it is not heavily dependent on imported intermediate inputs for the production of its exports. The import content of UK exports was 19.6% in 2022, well below the OECD average of 30.4%, and ranking 53rd out of 80 economies in the TiVA dataset on this measure. However, this should not be interpreted as a sign of lack of integration in GVCs or lack of competitiveness.

Several factors affect a country’s participation in GVCs, including the size of the economy, patterns of specialisation, trade openness and location. The UK’s relatively low backward participation partly reflects its specialisation in services exports which tend to have lower import content relative to manufacturing exports. Large and successful economies, such as the United States, rank even lower (76th) than the UK in terms of GVC participation.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the import content of the UK’s exports fell to 15.2%. Since then, it has risen by 4.4 percentage points, reflecting a rebound in GVC participation, in line with international trends. Other major developed economies - including the United States, France, Germany and Japan - have also seen a similar recovery in their GVC involvement since 2020.

In 2022, the UK sectors that were most reliant on foreign value added for their exports were manufacture of coke and refined petroleum products (with foreign value added accounting for 66.5% of this sector’s exports), electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply (59.4%) and manufacture of basic precious and other non-ferrous metals (42.8%).

The UK is more reliant on non-EU than EU suppliers of intermediate goods and services for the production of its exports. In 2022, around 65.0% of all foreign inputs used in UK exports originated from non-EU countries (35.0% originated from the EU). The largest suppliers of inputs for UK exporters in 2022 were the United States (supplying 16.5% of all foreign inputs used in UK exports), Norway (12.1%), China (7.7%), Germany (7.4%) and France (5.6%).

In 2022, all UK exporting sectors were more dependent on imported inputs from non-EU countries than the EU. The most dependent sectors on inputs from the non-EU were manufacture of coke and refined petroleum products, electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply and manufacture of basic precious and other non-ferrous metals.

### 7.3 Forward linkages for exports

Forward linkages measure the contribution of UK exporters as suppliers of inputs to exports of other countries. They provide a measure of how dependent UK exporters (suppliers of goods and services) can be on export prospects abroad.

The UK has stronger forward linkages than backward linkages, indicating its more prominent role as a supplier of inputs rather than a user of inputs. In 2022, the UK ranked as the 18th most forward integrated country (out of the 80 countries in the TiVA database), with the headline measure (UK value added embodied in foreign exports, as % of UK exports) at 25.6%. Despite the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, this share has increased over the past decade indicating that UK exporters have become more deeply integrated in downstream supply chains over time.

The UK has stronger forward linkages with EU countries (especially Ireland, Germany, France, the Netherlands and Luxembourg), with the EU as a bloc accounting for 64.0% of all UK valued added embodied in foreign exports in 2022. Outside of the EU, the UK has strong forward linkages with Singapore, the United States and China.

The main UK suppliers of inputs to foreign exporters are services sectors such as professional, scientific and technical services (accounting for 16.9% of total UK value added embodied in foreign exports in 2022), financial and insurance services (16.7%), administrative and support (9.7%) and wholesale & retail trade services (9.7%).

The contribution from the UK manufacturing sector is around 16.0%, with the chemicals sector being the largest supplier of inputs for foreign exporters. The UK extraction of crude petroleum and natural gas sector is also a significant supplier, contributing around 5.0% to the total UK value added embodied in foreign exports.

In 2022, the UK source sectors that were most dependent on supplying inputs to EU exporters (and hence demand conditions in this bloc) were extraction of crude petroleum and natural gas and publishing, audiovisual and broadcasting services. The UK source sectors most dependent on supplying inputs to non-EU exporters were building of ships and boats, air transport services and water transport services.

### 7.4 Overall (backward and forward) GVC participation

In terms of overall (backward and forward) GVC participation, UK performance is characterised by lower-than-average backward linkages and higher-than-average forward linkages - implying that the UK is more integrated in GVCs as a supplier (or seller) of inputs than a user (or buyer) of inputs. In 2022 the UK was ranked 55th out of the 80 economies in the TiVA database in terms of overall GVC participation.

As highlighted above, lower-than-average GVC participation is not necessarily an indication of weak international trade performance or lack of competitiveness. A large domestic market or relative specialisation in services (which require fewer imported inputs) could lower GVC participation, independent of a country’s trade performance.

### 7.5 Importance of services

Services are traded not only directly across borders, but also indirectly when they are embodied in the exports of goods. For example, UK engineering services used in the production of an exported car contribute indirectly to that international transaction. This type of indirect trade in services is captured in TiVA statistics but not in traditional trade data. Consequently, TiVA offers a more complete picture of the services sector’s contribution to exports. As services are increasingly embedded in manufacturing exports, policies to encourage services trade liberalisation can improve the export competitiveness of not just services industries but also manufacturing industries.

Accounting for the value added of services in the production of goods, the TiVA data reveal that services are more important to UK exports than they appear in traditional trade data. In value added terms, the share of services in total UK exports was 73.1% in 2022, around 9.0 percentage points higher than in traditional (gross) terms (64.4%). The difference is driven partly by the contribution of services inputs to UK manufacturing exports; the share of services value added in UK manufacturing gross exports was 37.7% in 2022.

### 7.6 Bilateral trade balances and export rankings

Measuring trade in value added terms does not affect a country’s overall trade balance with the rest of the world but it can change bilateral trade balances with individual countries. This is because, unlike gross trade statistics - which record the full value of goods and services crossing borders, including foreign-sourced inputs - TiVA measures as an export the domestic value added that is ultimately consumed in the final market (domestic value added embodied in foreign final demand).

In 2022, the UK’s trade deficit with non-EU countries was smaller in value added terms than in gross terms, whereas its trade deficit with EU countries was larger when measured in value added terms. Notably, the UK appears to run a trade surplus with transhipment hubs like Ireland, the Netherlands and Singapore in gross terms, but this turns into a deficit in value added terms. This discrepancy largely arises because gross trade statistics overstate UK exports to these hubs; many UK exports simply pass through them en-route to their final destination. In gross trade statistics, these flows are recorded as UK exports to the transhipment countries. However, in TiVA terms, they are reassigned to the final consumer countries, reducing the UK’s exports to the transhipment countries and turning the apparent surplus into a deficit.

In gross terms, the United States (16.1% export market share), Germany (8.7%), Ireland (8.1%), France (6.3%) and China (5.2%) ranked as the UK’s top 5 export destinations in 2022. In value added terms (as final consumers of UK exports), these countries remain in the top 5 UK export destinations, with the United States ranked top with a 18.8% market share, followed by Germany (8.2%), China (6.6%), France (6.1%) and Ireland (3.6%). In value added terms, the importance of non-EU export markets, such as the United States, China and India, is amplified while the importance of EU export markets such as Germany, France, Ireland, Spain and the Netherlands is reduced.

The reduced share of EU countries as destinations for UK exports in value added terms should not be interpreted as a sign of weakening UK ties with the EU; EU economies serve as important conduits for substantial amounts of UK value added that ultimately reach non-EU markets. Similarly, non-EU countries often access the UK market indirectly through the EU.

### 8.4 Inward investment project statistics

All FDI project statistics in this section were published by DBT. Full details on how the data is sourced and the statistics are compiled is available in the [technical annex](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book/technical-annex-to-trade-and-investment-core-statistics-book#dbt-inward-investment-results--annual).

Source: [DBT inward investment results 2025 to 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/dbt-inward-investment-results-2025-to-2026).

DBT recorded 1,020 investment projects coming into the UK in 2025 to 2026, down 25.8% from 1,375 in 2024 to 2025.

Out of all investment projects in 2025 to 2026, 453 (44.4%) were made by new investors to the UK, and 567 (55.6%) by existing investors in the UK.

Inward investments projects coming into the UK are estimated to have created 69,166 new jobs and safeguarded 16,407 jobs in 2025 to 2026, down 0.3% and up 60.9% respectively.

London was the UK region with the highest number of new jobs created by inward investment (14,261 new jobs) in 2025 to 2026. The highest growth of new created jobs was in the East of England, an increase of 211.2% in 2025 to 2026.

The software and computer services sector saw the most FDI projects and the environment, infrastructure and transportation sector saw the most new jobs created in 2025 to 2026.
