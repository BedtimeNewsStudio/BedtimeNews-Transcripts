### What is New Delhi?

New Delhi is the national capital of [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India).

### Where is New Delhi?

New Delhi is situated in the north-central part of [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) on the west bank of the [Yamuna River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yamuna-River), adjacent to and just south of Old Delhi, the historic centre of [Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi), and within the Delhi national capital territory.

### When was New Delhi founded?

In December 1911 King [George V](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-V-king-of-United-Kingdom) of Britain decreed that the capital of British [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) would be moved from Calcutta (which is today called [Kolkata](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kolkata)) to Delhi. Construction began in 1912 at a site about 3 miles (5 km), south of the [Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi) city centre, and the new capital was formally dedicated in 1931.

### What is New Delhi known for?

New Delhi is best known as the location of [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India)’s national government. New Delhi has great historical significance as it was home to powerful people, such as the [Pāṇḍavas](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pandavas) and the [Mughals](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mughal-dynasty). The city has many historical monuments and tourist attractions as well as lively marketplaces and great food, such as [chaat](https://www.britannica.com/topic/chaat).

### Why is New Delhi so polluted?

New Delhi is among the world’s most [polluted](https://www.britannica.com/science/pollution-environment) cities. Contributing to the [smog](https://www.britannica.com/science/smog) are traffic congestion, heavy [industry](https://www.britannica.com/money/industry), and the lack of cleansing winds in the area. Although antipollution measures undertaken since the 1980s have improved the city’s air quality considerably, pollution continues to be a deadly problem.

**New Delhi**, national capital of [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India). It is situated in the north-central part of the country on the west bank of the [Yamuna River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yamuna-River), adjacent to and just south of [Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi) city (Old Delhi) and within the Delhi national capital territory.

In December 1911 King [George V](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-V-king-of-United-Kingdom) of Britain decreed that the capital of British India would be moved from Calcutta (now [Kolkata](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kolkata)) to Delhi. Construction began in 1912 at a site about 3 miles (5 km) south of the Delhi city centre, and the new capital was formally dedicated in 1931. The straight and diagonal pattern of the broad tree-lined avenues in New Delhi, with extensive green spaces and wide vistas, contrasts sharply with the crowded, narrow, and winding streets characteristic of Old Delhi. The main east-west axis of New Delhi is Central Vista Park, which is flanked by government buildings, museums, and research centres in a parklike setting. The city is governed by the appointed New Delhi Municipal Council (NDMC). Area city, 16.5 square miles (42.7 square km). Pop (2001) 302,363.

### What is Delhi?

Delhi is a city and national capital territory in north-central [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi/Introduction#ref1). It consists of two parts: Old Delhi, the historic city, and [New Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Delhi), which became the capital of British India in the 20th century and has been the capital of India since 1947.

As one of India’s largest urban areas, Delhi is a commercial, transport, and cultural hub, as well as the country’s political center. The city’s cultural life displays a mix of traditional and cosmopolitan styles. Numerous museums, historical monuments, libraries, and gardens can be found throughout the city.

### Where is Delhi located in India?

Delhi, a city and union territory, is the national capital of India, located in the north-central part of the country. It consists of two main parts: Old Delhi, the historic city in the north, and [New Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Delhi), the capital of India since 1947, in the south.

Delhi is one of India’s largest urban areas, situated on the west bank of the [Yamuna River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yamuna-River), a tributary of the [Ganges](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ganges-River), approximately 100 miles (160 km) south of the [Himalayas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Himalayas). The territory is bordered by [Uttar Pradesh](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uttar-Pradesh) to the east and [Haryana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Haryana) to the north, west, and south.

Delhi holds historical significance as a commercial, transport, and cultural hub, as well as India’s political center. Its location on the great plain of India makes it a pivotal center in the subcontinent’s transportation network.

### Why is Delhi important to India?

Delhi is important to India as a commercial, transport, and cultural hub, as well as the political center of the country. Old Delhi has been a dominant trading and commercial center in northern India for many centuries, and New Delhi has emerged as an important node in the international corporate and financial network since the 1990s. The city’s cultural life exhibits a unique blend of traditional and cosmopolitan styles.

### What are some famous landmarks in Delhi?

Delhi is known for its rich architectural heritage. Some famous landmarks include the [Red Fort](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Red-Fort/Introduction#ref1), a [Mughal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mughal-dynasty) fort built by [Shah Jahān](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Shah-Jahan) in the mid-17th century, now a [UNESCO World Heritage site](https://www.britannica.com/topic/list-of-World-Heritage-sites-2038911). Its red sandstone walls enclose palaces, halls, gardens, and a mosque.

Another significant landmark is the [Qutb Minar](https://www.britannica.com/list/5-significant-buildings-in-delhi/Introduction#ref421396), a towering minaret and a UNESCO World Heritage site, showcasing Islamic architectural legacy. The Qutb Minar is a part of the [Qutb complex](https://www.britannica.com/list/5-significant-buildings-in-delhi/Introduction#ref421396).

### What makes Delhi’s culture unique?

Delhi’s culture is a unique blend of traditional and cosmopolitan styles. The city is filled with museums, monuments, libraries, gardens, and places of worship, alongside modern art galleries, multiplexes, sports venues, and diverse restaurants.

Numerous fairs and festivals, including film, trade, and book events, add to Delhi’s cultural diversity. Various religious groups contribute to ongoing celebrations.

Delhi also has a rich architectural heritage, with influences from early Muslim, Mughal, British colonial, and post-independence styles, as seen in landmarks such as [Humayun’s Tomb](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi/Cultural-life#ref280855) and the [Red Fort](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi/Cultural-life#ref280855).

### How has Delhi’s history influenced modern India?

Delhi’s extensive history has significantly shaped modern India. As a dominant trading center for centuries, Old Delhi’s commercial importance continues today. New Delhi has become a vital hub in the international corporate and financial network since the 1990s.

Delhi’s cultural institutions, museums, and architecture reflect a blend of traditional and cosmopolitan styles, showcasing India’s diverse artistic heritage. The city’s historical sites, such as the [Red Fort](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi/Cultural-life#ref280855) and [Humayun’s tomb](https://www.britannica.com/place/Delhi/Cultural-life#ref280855), are UNESCO World Heritage sites.

Since India’s independence in 1947, Delhi has grown into a major metropolitan area, absorbing people from all over the country. This influx has placed a strain on the city’s infrastructure but has also contributed to its vibrant, diverse culture.

The city of Delhi actually consists of two components: Old Delhi, in the north, the historic city; and [New Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Delhi), in the south, since 1947 the capital of India, built in the first part of the 20th century as the capital of [British India](https://www.britannica.com/event/British-raj).

One of the country’s largest urban agglomerations, Delhi sits astride (but primarily on the west bank of) the [Yamuna River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yamuna-River), a tributary of the [Ganges](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ganges-River) (Ganga) River, about 100 miles (160 km) south of the [Himalayas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Himalayas). The national capital territory embraces Old and New Delhi and the surrounding metropolitan region, as well as [adjacent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/adjacent) rural areas. To the east the territory is bounded by the state of [Uttar Pradesh](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uttar-Pradesh), and to the north, west, and south it is bounded by the state of [Haryana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Haryana).

Delhi is of great historical significance as an important commercial, transport, and cultural hub, as well as the political center of India. According to [legend](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legend), the city was named for Raja Dhilu, a king who reigned in the region in the 1st century bce. The names by which the city has been known—including Delhi, Dehli, Dilli, and Dhilli, among others—likely are corruptions of his name. Delhi has been the center of a succession of mighty empires and powerful kingdoms. Numerous ruins scattered throughout the territory offer a constant reminder of the area’s history. Popular lore holds that the city changed its locality a total of seven times between 3000 bce and the 17th century ce, although some authorities, who take smaller towns and strongholds into account, claim it changed its site as many as 15 times. All the earlier locations of Delhi fall within a triangular area of about 70 square miles (180 square km), commonly called the Delhi Triangle. Two sides of the triangle are [articulated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/articulated) by the rocky hills of the [Aravalli Range](https://www.britannica.com/place/Aravalli-Range)—one to the south of the city, the other on its western edge, where it is known as the Delhi Ridge. The third side of the triangle is formed by the shifting channel of the [Yamuna River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yamuna-River). Between the river and the hills lie broad [alluvial](https://www.britannica.com/science/alluvium) plains; the elevation of the territory ranges from about 700 to 1,000 feet (200 to 300 meters).

The ridges and hillsides of the national capital territory abound in thorny trees, such as [acacias](https://www.britannica.com/plant/acacia), as well as seasonal herbaceous species. The sissoo (shisham; _[Dalbergia sissoo](https://www.britannica.com/plant/sissoo)_) tree, which yields a dark brown and durable timber, is commonly found in the plains. Riverine vegetation, consisting of weeds and grass, occurs on the banks of the Yamuna. New Delhi is known for its flowering shade trees, such as the [neem](https://www.britannica.com/plant/neem-tree) ( _Azadirachta indica_; a drought-resistant tree with a pale yellow fruit), jaman ( _Syzygium cumini_; a tree with an edible grapelike fruit), [mango](https://www.britannica.com/plant/mango-plant-and-fruit), pipal ( _Ficus religiosa_; a [fig](https://www.britannica.com/plant/fig) tree), and sissoo. It also is known for its flowering plants, which include a large number of multicoloured seasonals: [chrysanthemums](https://www.britannica.com/plant/Chrysanthemum), [phlox](https://www.britannica.com/plant/phlox), [violas](https://www.britannica.com/plant/Viola-plant-genus), and [verbenas](https://www.britannica.com/plant/verbena-plant).

The animal life of the national capital territory, like its plant life, is quite [diverse](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diverse). Among carnivorous animals are [leopards](https://www.britannica.com/animal/leopard), [hyenas](https://www.britannica.com/animal/hyena), [foxes](https://www.britannica.com/animal/fox-mammal), [wolves](https://www.britannica.com/animal/wolf), and [jackals](https://www.britannica.com/animal/jackal), which inhabit the ravine lands and hilly ridges. Wild boars are sometimes spotted along the banks of the Yamuna. [Monkeys](https://www.britannica.com/animal/monkey) are found in the city, especially around some of the [temples](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hindu-temple) and historical ruins. The lakes around the city attract seasonal species. [Fish](https://www.britannica.com/animal/fish) are plentiful in the Yamuna, and an occasional [crocodile](https://www.britannica.com/animal/crocodile-order) also may be found there.

## Climate

The [climate](https://www.britannica.com/science/climate-meteorology) of Delhi is characterized by extreme dryness, with intensely hot [summers](https://www.britannica.com/science/summer-season) that can reach temperatures of about 113 °F (45 °C). _Loo_, or hot and dry winds, blow across the [northern plains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indo-Gangetic-Plain) in the summer. Dust storms, locally known as _andhi_ s, can bring temporary relief in the summer months by causing a drop in temperature. Delhi is associated with a general prevalence of [continental air](https://www.britannica.com/science/continental-air-mass) (dry air that originates over continents), which moves in from the west or northwest, except during the season of the [monsoon](https://www.britannica.com/science/monsoon), when an easterly to southeasterly [influx](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/influx) of oceanic air brings rain and increased humidity. The summer season lasts from mid-March to the end of June, with maximum temperatures typically reaching about 100 °F (about 37 °C) and minimum temperatures falling into the high 70s F (about 25 °C); it is characterized by frequent thunderstorms and squalls, especially in April and May.

PM2.5 and PM10

[Particulate matter](https://www.britannica.com/science/particulate-matter) 10 micrometers in diameter (PM10) can cause irritation as it settles in the upper respiratory tract, whereas matter 2.5 micrometers in diameter or smaller (PM2.5) can settle deeper, potentially lodging in the lungs and leading to [asthma](https://www.britannica.com/science/asthma) and cardiovascular health concerns. That is why PM10 and PM2.5 levels are key indicators of an area’s level of pollution.

The monsoon season normally begins in July and continues until the end of September. It is during these months that Delhi receives the bulk of its rainfall—roughly 23 inches (600 mm), or nearly three-fourths of the annual average. October and November [constitute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitute) a transition period from monsoon to [winter](https://www.britannica.com/science/winter) conditions. The dry winter season extends from late November to mid-March. The coldest month is January, with high temperatures in the low 70s F (about 21 °C) and low temperatures in the high-30s F (about 4 °C). Delhi experiences severe air [pollution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/pollution-in-India) in the winter months, with [smog](https://www.britannica.com/science/smog) and severe [PM2.5](https://www.britannica.com/science/particulate-matter) and PM10 levels raising serious health concerns. [AQI](https://www.britannica.com/science/Air-Quality-Index) levels frequently reach the “very poor” (301 to 400) and “severe” (401 to 500) categories in the months of November and December, and Delhi consistently features as the most polluted capital city in the world. In recent years, [climate change](https://www.britannica.com/science/climate-change) has resulted in unpredictable weather patterns, severe winters, and higher than usual summer temperatures.
