Top Questions

### What is Christopher Columbus known for?

Christopher Columbus was a navigator who explored the Americas under the flag of Spain. Some people think of him as the “discoverer” of America, but this is not strictly true. His voyages across the Atlantic paved the way for [European colonization and exploitation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism) of the Americas.

### What was Christopher Columbus looking for?

Columbus sailed in search of a route to [Cathay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cathay-medieval-region-China) (China) and [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) to bring back gold and spices that were highly sought in Europe. His patrons, [Ferdinand II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ferdinand-II-king-of-Spain) and [Isabella I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Isabella-I-queen-of-Spain) of Spain, hoped that his success would bring them greater status.

### Where did Christopher Columbus go?

Columbus made four transatlantic voyages: 1492–93, 1493–96, 1498–1500, and 1502–04. He traveled primarily to the Caribbean, including the [Bahamas](https://www.britannica.com/place/The-Bahamas), [Cuba](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cuba), [Santo Domingo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hispaniola), and [Jamaica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jamaica), and in his latter two voyages traveled to the coasts of eastern Central America and northern South America.

### Did Christopher Columbus discover America?

Some people say Columbus discovered America or the “New World,” but [Vikings](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Viking-people) such as [Leif Eriksson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leif-Erikson) had visited North America centuries earlier, and [Native American](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Native-American) tribes had lived in the Americas for centuries before either Columbus or the Vikings arrived.

### What was the impact of Columbus’s travels?

Columbus’s journeys to the Americas opened the way for European countries to [colonize and exploit](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism) those lands and their peoples. Trade was soon established between Europe and the Americas. Plants native to the Americas (such as potatoes, tomatoes, and tobacco) were imported to Europe. This trade route also paved the way for the [slave trade](https://www.britannica.com/topic/transatlantic-slave-trade) between Europe, Africa, and the Americas. Explorers and settlers brought with them diseases that had a devastating effect on Native American populations. Many native peoples perished or were driven from their homes by colonizers.

**Christopher Columbus** (born between August 26 and October 31?, 1451, Genoa \[Italy\]—died May 20, 1506, [Valladolid](https://www.britannica.com/place/Valladolid-Spain), Spain) was a master navigator and [admiral](https://www.britannica.com/topic/admiral) whose four transatlantic [voyages](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/voyages) (1492–93, 1493–96, 1498–1500, and 1502–04) opened the way for [European exploration](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-exploration), exploitation, and [colonization](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism) of the Americas. He has long been called the “discoverer” of the New World, although [Vikings](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Viking-people) such as [Leif Eriksson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leif-Erikson) had visited [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America) five centuries earlier. Columbus made his transatlantic voyages under the sponsorship of [Ferdinand II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ferdinand-II-king-of-Spain) and [Isabella I](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Isabella-I-queen-of-Spain), the [Catholic Monarchs](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Catholic-Monarchs) of [Aragon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Aragon-region-Spain), [Castile](https://www.britannica.com/place/Castile-region-Spain), and [Leon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Leon-medieval-kingdom-Spain) in [Spain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain). He was at first full of hope and ambition, an ambition partly gratified by his title “Admiral of the Ocean Sea,” awarded to him in April 1492, and by the grants enrolled in the Book of Privileges (a record of his titles and claims). However, he died a disappointed man.

The period between the quatercentenary celebrations of Columbus’s achievements in 1892–93 and the quincentenary ones of 1992 saw great advances in Columbus scholarship. Numerous books about Columbus appeared in the 1990s, and the insights of archaeologists and anthropologists began to [complement](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/complement) those of sailors and historians. This effort gave rise to considerable debate. There was also a major shift in approach and interpretation; the older pro-European understanding gave way to one shaped from the perspective of the inhabitants of the [Americas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Americas) themselves. According to the older understanding, the “discovery” of the Americas was a great triumph, one in which Columbus played the part of hero in accomplishing the four voyages, in being the means of bringing great material profit to Spain and to other European countries, and in opening up the Americas to European settlement. The more recent perspective, however, has concentrated on the destructive side of the European conquest, emphasizing, for example, the disastrous impact of the [slave trade](https://www.britannica.com/topic/slave-trade) and the ravages of imported disease on the [Indigenous peoples](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indigenous-peoples-of-the-Americas) of the [Caribbean](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-West-Indies) region and the American continents. The sense of triumph has diminished accordingly, and the view of Columbus as hero has now been replaced, for many, by one of a man deeply flawed. While this second perception rarely doubts Columbus’s sincerity or abilities as a navigator, it emphatically removes him from his position of honor. Political activists of all kinds have intervened in the debate, further hindering the reconciliation of these [disparate](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/disparate) views.

## Life

## Early career and preparation for the first voyage

[![Christopher, Diego, and Ferdinand Columbus](https://cdn.britannica.com/99/182599-050-D8CA127C/Christopher-Columbus-sons-woman-Ferdinand-Diego-I-1794.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/99/182599-050-D8CA127C/Christopher-Columbus-sons-woman-Ferdinand-Diego-I-1794.jpg)

[Christopher, Diego, and Ferdinand Columbus](https://cdn.britannica.com/99/182599-050-D8CA127C/Christopher-Columbus-sons-woman-Ferdinand-Diego-I-1794.jpg) Christopher Columbus with his sons, Diego and Ferdinand, and a woman; engraving by I. Stockdale, 1794.(more)

Little is known of Columbus’s early life. The vast majority of scholars, citing Columbus’s testament of 1498 and archival documents from Genoa and [Savona](https://www.britannica.com/place/Savona-Italy), believe that he was born in [Genoa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Genoa-Italy) to a [Christian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Christianity) household. However, it has also been claimed that he was a [converso](https://www.britannica.com/topic/converso) (a [Sephardic Jew](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sephardi) who had converted to Christianity under threat of persecution in Spain) or that he was born in [Portugal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Portugal) or elsewhere.

Columbus was the eldest son of Domenico Colombo, a Genoese [wool](https://www.britannica.com/topic/wool) worker and merchant, and Susanna Fontanarossa, his wife. His career as a seaman began effectively in the Portuguese [merchant marine](https://www.britannica.com/topic/merchant-marine). After surviving a shipwreck off [Cape Saint Vincent](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cape-Saint-Vincent) at the southwestern point of Portugal in 1476, he based himself in [Lisbon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lisbon), together with his brother [Bartholomew](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bartholomew-Columbus). Both were employed as chart makers, but Columbus was principally a seagoing [entrepreneur](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/entrepreneur). In 1477 he sailed to [Iceland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iceland) and [Ireland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland) with the merchant marine, and in 1478 he was buying sugar in [Madeira](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Madeira-wine) as an agent for the Genoese firm of Centurioni. In 1479 he met and married Felipa Perestrello e Moniz, a member of an impoverished noble Portuguese family. Their son, [Diego](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Diego-Columbus), was born in 1480. Between 1482 and 1485 Columbus traded along the [Guinea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Guinea-region-Africa) and [Gold](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gold-Coast-historical-region-Africa) coasts of tropical [West Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/western-Africa) and made at least one voyage to the Portuguese fortress of São Jorge da Mina (now Elmina, [Ghana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ghana)) there, gaining knowledge of Portuguese navigation and the Atlantic wind systems along the way. Felipa died in 1485, and Columbus took as his mistress Beatriz Enríquez de Harana of Córdoba, by whom he had his second son, Ferdinand (born c. 1488).

In 1484 Columbus began seeking support for an Atlantic crossing from King [John II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-II-king-of-Portugal) of Portugal but was denied aid. (Some [conspiracy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conspiracy) theorists have [alleged](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alleged) that Columbus made a secret pact with the monarch, but there is no evidence of this.) By 1486 Columbus was firmly in Spain, asking for patronage from King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella. After at least two rejections, he at last obtained royal support in January 1492. This was achieved chiefly through the interventions of the Spanish treasurer, Luis de Santángel, and of the [Franciscan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Franciscans) friars of La Rábida, near [Huelva](https://www.britannica.com/place/Huelva-Spain), with whom Columbus had stayed in the summer of 1491. Juan Pérez of La Rábida had been one of the queen’s confessors and perhaps [procured](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/procured) him the crucial audience.

Christian missionary and anti-Islamic fervor, the power of Castile and Aragon, the fear of Portugal, the lust for gold, the desire for adventure, the hope of conquests, and Europe’s genuine need for a reliable supply of herbs and spices for cooking, preserving, and medicine all combined to produce an explosion of energy that launched the first voyage. Columbus had been present at the siege of [Granada](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain/The-Almoravids#ref70377), which was the last [Moorish](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Moor-people) stronghold to fall to Spain (January 2, 1492), and he was in fact riding back from Granada to La Rábida when he was recalled to the Spanish court and the vital royal audience. Granada’s fall had produced euphoria among Spanish Christians and encouraged designs of ultimate triumph over the [Islamic world](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islamic-world), [albeit](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/albeit) chiefly, perhaps, by the back way round the globe. A direct assault eastward could prove difficult, because the [Ottoman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottoman-Empire) and other Islamic states in the region had been gaining strength at a pace that was threatening the Christian monarchies themselves. The Islamic powers had effectively closed the land routes to the East and made the sea route south from the [Red Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Red-Sea) extremely hard to access.

> …and I saw the Moorish king come out of the gates of the city and kiss the royal hands of Your Highnesses…and Your Highnesses, as Catholic Christians…took thought to send me, Christopher Columbus, to the said parts of India, to see those princes and peoples and lands…and the manner which should be used to bring about their [conversion](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/conversion) to our holy faith, and ordained that I should not go by land to the eastward, by which way it was the custom to go, but by way of the west, by which down to this day we do not know certainly that anyone has passed; therefore, having driven out all the Jews from your realms and lordships in the same month of January, Your Highnesses commanded me that, with a sufficient fleet, I should go to the said parts of India, and for this accorded me great rewards and ennobled me so that from that time henceforth I might style myself “Don” and be high admiral of the Ocean Sea and [viceroy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/viceroy-government-official) and [perpetual](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/perpetual) Governor of the islands and continent which I should discover…and that my eldest son should succeed to the same position, and so on from generation to generation forever.

Thus a great number of interests were involved in this adventure, which was, in essence, the attempt to find a route to the rich land of [Cathay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cathay-medieval-region-China) ( [China](https://www.britannica.com/place/China)), to [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India), and to the fabled gold and spice islands of the East by sailing westward over what was presumed to be open sea. European interest in a westward voyage reflected a combination of commercial, political, personal, and religious ambitions. Columbus sought a westward [maritime](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/maritime) route to the wealthy trading regions of Asia and hoped that success would bring riches and social advancement to himself and his family. Ferdinand and Isabella, meanwhile, saw the enterprise as an opportunity to strengthen Spain’s position relative to Portugal, expand its commercial reach, and advance Christian interests beyond Europe. With the support of the papacy under Pope [Alexander VI](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-VI), the Spanish monarchs also hoped to solidify their country’s role as a leading Catholic power.

Religious ideas also influenced Columbus’s ambitions. Like some [Franciscan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Franciscans) s of his time, he believed that the expansion of Christianity was connected to biblical prophecy and hoped that wealth gained through his voyages might eventually help finance a campaign to reclaim Jerusalem for Christian rule. He also expected to [encounter](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/encounter) powerful rulers in Asia who might become allies of Christian Europe. Among them was the legendary [Prester John](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Prester-John-legendary-ruler), long believed by Europeans to rule a Christian kingdom somewhere in the East. Columbus also carried a letter from Ferdinand and Isabella for the ruler Europeans knew as the Great Khan, in the hope of establishing diplomatic relations.

There were more immediate reasons for Spain to support a westward voyage. Portugal had spent decades exploring the western coast of Africa in search of its own sea route to Asia, and in 1488 [Bartolomeu Dias](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bartolomeu-Dias) rounded the southern tip of Africa. Columbus proposed an alternative: Rather than following the Portuguese route around Africa, Spain could attempt to reach Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic. The rivalry between the two kingdoms therefore added urgency to Columbus’s proposal.

# The first voyage of Christopher Columbus

[![Christopher Columbus's fleet](https://cdn.britannica.com/12/94912-050-01938555/Illustration-Christopher-Columbus-fleet-Spain-1492.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/12/94912-050-01938555/Illustration-Christopher-Columbus-fleet-Spain-1492.jpg)

[Christopher Columbus's fleet](https://cdn.britannica.com/12/94912-050-01938555/Illustration-Christopher-Columbus-fleet-Spain-1492.jpg) Illustration depicting Christopher Columbus's fleet departing from Spain in 1492.(more)

The ships for the first voyage—the _[Niña](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nina-ship)_, _[Pinta](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pinta-ship)_, and [_Santa María_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Santa-Maria-ship)—were fitted out at Palos, on the Tinto River in [Spain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Spain). [Consortia](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Consortia) put together by a royal treasury official and composed mainly of Genoese and Florentine bankers in [Sevilla](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sevilla-Spain) (Seville) provided at least 1,140,000 maravedis to outfit the expedition, and Columbus supplied more than a third of the sum contributed by the king and queen. Queen Isabella did not, then, have to pawn her jewels (a [myth](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/myth) first put about by [Bartolomé de Las Casas](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bartolome-de-Las-Casas) in the 16th century).

The fleet departed Palos on August 3, 1492, and sailed south toward the [Canary Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canary-Islands) rather than heading directly west across the Atlantic. From there Columbus could take advantage of the northeast trade winds for the westward crossing. After repairs and nearly a month in the Canaries, the three ships departed [San Sebastián](https://www.britannica.com/place/Donostia-San-Sebastian) de la Gomera on September 6.

[![Landing of Columbus](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/143622-050-FD012047/Landing-Columbus-oil-canvas-John-Vanderlyn-US-1846.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/143622-050-FD012047/Landing-Columbus-oil-canvas-John-Vanderlyn-US-1846.jpg)

[_Landing of Columbus_](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/143622-050-FD012047/Landing-Columbus-oil-canvas-John-Vanderlyn-US-1846.jpg) _Landing of Columbus_, oil on canvas by John Vanderlyn, 1846; in the U.S. Capitol, Washington, D.C.(more)

On several occasions in September and early October, sailors spotted floating vegetation and various types of birds—all taken as signs that land was nearby. But by October 10 the crew had begun to lose patience, complaining that with their failure to make landfall, contrary winds and a shortage of [provisions](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/provisions) would keep them from returning home. Columbus allayed their fears, at least temporarily, and on October 12 land was sighted from the _Pinta_ (though Columbus, on the _Niña_, later claimed the privilege for himself). The place of the first Caribbean landfall, called Guanahani, is hotly disputed, but [San Salvador](https://www.britannica.com/place/San-Salvador-Island-The-Bahamas) (Watlings) Island in the [Bahamas](https://www.britannica.com/place/The-Bahamas) is generally preferred to other Bahamian islands ( [Samana Cay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Samana-Cay), Rum Cay, or the Plana Cays) or to the [Turks and Caicos Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turks-and-Caicos-Islands). Beyond planting the royal banner, however, Columbus spent little time there, being anxious to press on to Cipango, or Cipangu ( [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan)). He thought that he had found it in [Cuba](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cuba), where he landed on October 28, but he convinced himself by November 1 that Cuba was the [Cathay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cathay-medieval-region-China) mainland itself, though he had yet to see evidence of great cities. Thus, on December 5, he turned back southeastward to search for the fabled city of Zaiton ( [Quanzhou](https://www.britannica.com/place/Quanzhou), China), missing through this decision his sole chance of setting foot on [Florida](https://www.britannica.com/place/Florida) soil.

Adverse winds carried the fleet to an island called Ayti ( [Haiti](https://www.britannica.com/place/Haiti)) by its [Indigenous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Indigenous) [Taino](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taino) inhabitants; on December 6 Columbus renamed it La Isla Española, or [Hispaniola](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hispaniola). He seems to have thought that Hispaniola might be Cipango or, if not Cipango, then perhaps one of the legendarily rich isles from which King [Solomon](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Solomon)’s triennial fleet brought back gold, gems, and spices to Jerusalem (1 Kings 10:11, 22); alternatively, he reasoned that the island could be related to the biblical kingdom of Sheba ( [Sabaʾ](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saba-ancient-kingdom-Arabia)). There Columbus found at least enough gold and [prosperity](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/prosperity) to save him from ridicule on his return to Spain. With the help of a [Taino](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Taino) cacique, or Indigenous chief, named Guacanagarí, he set up a stockade on the northern coast of the island, named it La Navidad, and posted 39 men to guard it until his return. The accidental running aground of the _Santa María_ on December 25, 1492, provided additional planks and provisions for the garrison.

Columbus began the return journey on January 16, 1493, aboard the _Niña_, accompanied by the _Pinta_. The journey was a nightmare. The Atlantic westerlies carried the ships toward Europe, but severe storms separated them during the crossing. The _Niña_ reached Santa Maria in the [Azores](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azores), where Portuguese authorities briefly detained several members of Columbus’s party after they went ashore. Another storm subsequently forced Columbus into [Lisbon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lisbon), where he met with King John II of [Portugal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Portugal) before continuing to Spain. Columbus returned to Palos on March 15, 1493, about seven months after departing from Spain.

The [voyage](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/voyage) demonstrated that ships could [cross](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cross-religious-symbol) the Atlantic and return to Europe using the prevailing wind systems, but Columbus remained mistaken about the geography of the lands he had encountered. He continued to believe that the Caribbean islands lay near Asia rather than recognizing that Europeans had encountered continents previously unknown to them. The voyage also established patterns that would have far-reaching consequences during Columbus’s subsequent expeditions and the Spanish colonization of the Caribbean. Columbus claimed inhabited territories for the Spanish crown from that first voyage and searched for sources of gold and other wealth, took Indigenous people captive, and established a Spanish outpost on Hispaniola. Although some early encounters involved trade and cooperation, relations between Spanish colonists and the [Indigenous peoples](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indigenous-people) of the Caribbean would soon be marked by increasing demands for labor and tribute, armed conflict, enslavement, and profound disruption of Indigenous [communities](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/communities).
