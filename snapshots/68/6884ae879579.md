**French 75**, field [gun](https://www.britannica.com/technology/gun-weapon) of 75-mm (2.95-inch) [bore](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/bore) devised in 1894 by Colonel Albert Deport of the French army. It was distinguished from other [cannon](https://www.britannica.com/technology/cannon-weapon) of its time by its [recoil](https://www.britannica.com/technology/recoil-weapon) system: the barrel and breech recoiled on rollers while the gun carriage itself remained in place instead of jumping or rolling backward.

The recoil was damped by a cylinder containing oil, and the barrel and breech were returned to firing position by air in a second cylinder, compressed by the recoil. Introduced in 1897, the [weapon](https://www.britannica.com/technology/weapon) was used by French and Allied armies until the fall of [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) in [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II).

[World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I) is known for the savagery of its [trench warfare](https://www.britannica.com/topic/trench-warfare), but the weapons available to the war’s combatants were wide-ranging.

## Depth charge

[![Image 23: Large plume of water and smoke rises from a depth mine explosion in the sea, viewed from the deck of a ship with silhouetted figures, during World War I.](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/200603-050-E923072E/ship-British-depth-charges-vicinity-German-submarine.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/200603-050-E923072E/ship-British-depth-charges-vicinity-German-submarine.jpg)

[depth charge](https://cdn.britannica.com/03/200603-050-E923072E/ship-British-depth-charges-vicinity-German-submarine.jpg)British ship laying depth charges in the vicinity of a submerged German submarine during World War I.(more)

[Depth charges](https://www.britannica.com/technology/depth-charge) were first developed by the [Royal Navy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Royal-Navy) during [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I) to combat German submarines.

## Military aircraft

[![Image 24: A row of early biplanes lines a grassy field at Hendon, England, with soldiers and horse-drawn wagons gathered nearby during a World War I military inspection.](https://cdn.britannica.com/44/200644-050-9C6BC631/airfield-British-Hendon-London-England-World-War-i.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/44/200644-050-9C6BC631/airfield-British-Hendon-London-England-World-War-i.jpg)

[military aircraft](https://cdn.britannica.com/44/200644-050-9C6BC631/airfield-British-Hendon-London-England-World-War-i.jpg)British airfield at Hendon, outside London, England, during World War I.(more)

World War I was a [crucible](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/crucible) for [military aircraft](https://www.britannica.com/technology/military-aircraft) development. Between 1914 and 1918, planes advanced from barely airworthy craft to effective weapons platforms.

## Rifle

[![Image 25: Two Serbian soldiers in uniform stand on a muddy path, carrying rifles and wearing backpacks, near Salonika, Greece, during World War I in April 1916.](https://cdn.britannica.com/43/200643-050-93044A76/infantryman-Serbian-Greece-Salonika-April-1916.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/43/200643-050-93044A76/infantryman-Serbian-Greece-Salonika-April-1916.jpg)

[World War I](https://cdn.britannica.com/43/200643-050-93044A76/infantryman-Serbian-Greece-Salonika-April-1916.jpg)Serbian infantryman near Salonika (now Thessaloníki), Greece, April 1916.(more)

Infantry weapons underwent a massive change in the late 19th century, as [repeating rifles](https://www.britannica.com/technology/repeating-rifle) entered widespread use. The World War I infantryman could produce a volume of fire that dwarfed that of his mid-19th-century predecessors.

## Zeppelin

[![Image 26: A German Zeppelin airship flies above several warships in Kiel Bay, Germany, during a World War I maneuver.](https://cdn.britannica.com/38/104338-050-DF135929/zeppelin-harbour-Kiel-Ger-maneuvers-World-War.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/38/104338-050-DF135929/zeppelin-harbour-Kiel-Ger-maneuvers-World-War.jpg)

[zeppelin](https://cdn.britannica.com/38/104338-050-DF135929/zeppelin-harbour-Kiel-Ger-maneuvers-World-War.jpg)A zeppelin flying over the harbour at Kiel, Germany, on maneuvers during World War I.(more)

German airships achieved moderate success in long-range bombing operations, as [Zeppelins](https://www.britannica.com/technology/zeppelin) could attain higher altitudes than the airplanes of the era.

## Chemical weapons

[![Image 27: French soldiers in uniform show a group of women how to put on gas masks outside a wooden shelter in a village near the front in Alsace during World War I.](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/201029-050-DAD0EE7A/front-lines-soldiers-region-gas-mask-Alsace-world-war-i.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/201029-050-DAD0EE7A/front-lines-soldiers-region-gas-mask-Alsace-world-war-i.jpg)

[Chemical weapons in World War I](https://cdn.britannica.com/29/201029-050-DAD0EE7A/front-lines-soldiers-region-gas-mask-Alsace-world-war-i.jpg)Soldiers demonstrating the proper wearing of a gas mask to civilians near the front lines in the French region of Alsace.(more)

Chemical weapons, such as [diphosgene](https://www.britannica.com/technology/diphosgene) and [mustard gas](https://www.britannica.com/science/mustard-gas), were employed extensively on the [Western Front](https://www.britannica.com/event/Western-Front-World-War-I).

## Artillery

[![Image 28: World War I; artillery](https://cdn.britannica.com/24/201024-050-3D369E37/American-railway-gun-firing-projectiles-German-troop.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/24/201024-050-3D369E37/American-railway-gun-firing-projectiles-German-troop.jpg)

[World War I; artillery](https://cdn.britannica.com/24/201024-050-3D369E37/American-railway-gun-firing-projectiles-German-troop.jpg)American 14-inch railway gun firing projectiles at a German railway and troop movement center some 20 miles (32 km) away.(more)

[Artillery](https://www.britannica.com/technology/artillery) literally shaped the battlefield in World War I. It ranged in size from the French 75-mm field gun to the massive 420-mm [Big Bertha](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Big-Bertha-weapon) and the 210-mm [Paris Gun](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Paris-Gun).

## Cavalry

[![Image 32: World War I](https://cdn.britannica.com/74/196074-050-A43E4F7F/Members-Australian-4th-Light-Horse-Brigade-Giza.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/74/196074-050-A43E4F7F/Members-Australian-4th-Light-Horse-Brigade-Giza.jpg)

[World War I](https://cdn.britannica.com/74/196074-050-A43E4F7F/Members-Australian-4th-Light-Horse-Brigade-Giza.jpg)Members of the Australian 4th Light Horse Brigade at Giza, Egypt, during World War I.(more)

Despite the advances in [technology](https://www.britannica.com/technology/technology), [cavalry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cavalry) retained a significant role in World War I, and horses died by the millions in the conflict.

## Battleship

[![Image 33: HMS Orion; dreadnought](https://cdn.britannica.com/14/60714-004-3363E418/HMS-Orion-Royal-Navy-power-guns-ship-1922.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/14/60714-004-3363E418/HMS-Orion-Royal-Navy-power-guns-ship-1922.jpg)

[HMS _Orion_; dreadnought](https://cdn.britannica.com/14/60714-004-3363E418/HMS-Orion-Royal-Navy-power-guns-ship-1922.jpg)HMS _Orion_, super dreadnought battleship of the Royal Navy. Heavier than the HMS _Dreadnought_ but just as fast, this ship mounted 10 13.5-inch guns of greater armour-piercing power in five turrets along the centreline of the vessel. The _Orion_ was present at the Battle of Jutland in 1916 and was scrapped under the Five-Power Naval Limitation Treaty of 1922.(more)

The age of the [battleship](https://www.britannica.com/technology/battleship-naval-ship) reached its [apotheosis](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/apotheosis) in World War I, as even the _[Dreadnought](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Dreadnought-British-battleship)_, the archetypal “big-gun” ship, found itself outgunned. Super dreadnoughts, such as the HMS _Orion_, ruled the waves; their reign was short, however, as developments in naval aviation would soon render such ships obsolete.

## Machine gun

[![Image 34: Trench warfare](https://cdn.britannica.com/90/177090-050-133E7B13/German-machine-gun-emplacement-World-War-I.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/90/177090-050-133E7B13/German-machine-gun-emplacement-World-War-I.jpg)

[Trench warfare](https://cdn.britannica.com/90/177090-050-133E7B13/German-machine-gun-emplacement-World-War-I.jpg)A German machine gun emplacement during World War I.(more)

[Machine guns](https://www.britannica.com/technology/machine-gun) were an exceptionally lethal addition to the battlefield in World War I. Heavy guns, such as the [Maxim](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Maxim-machine-gun) and [Hotchkiss](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Hotchkiss-machine-gun), made “no man’s land” a killing zone, and [Isaac Newton Lewis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Isaac-Newton-Lewis)’s light machine gun saw widespread use at the squad level and as an aircraft armament.

## Tank

[![Image 35: tank in the Battle of Cambrai](https://cdn.britannica.com/01/200901-050-BE42F690/Mark-IV-British-tank-trench-German-Battle-November-20-1917.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/01/200901-050-BE42F690/Mark-IV-British-tank-trench-German-Battle-November-20-1917.jpg)

[tank in the Battle of Cambrai](https://cdn.britannica.com/01/200901-050-BE42F690/Mark-IV-British-tank-trench-German-Battle-November-20-1917.jpg)British Mark IV (Male) tank ditched in a German trench during the Battle of Cambrai, November 20, 1917.(more)

[Tanks](https://www.britannica.com/technology/tank-military-vehicle) were used primarily in a supporting role. The [armoured vehicle](https://www.britannica.com/technology/armoured-vehicle) would not truly come into its own until the [doctrines](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/doctrines) of [J.F.C. Fuller](https://www.britannica.com/biography/J-F-C-Fuller) and Basil Liddell Hart were more widely adopted in [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II).

## Unexploded ordnance

[![Image 36: A tree trunk with a French World War I projectile lodged in its side, set in an outdoor landscape near Avricourt, France.](https://cdn.britannica.com/07/200607-050-A80B4173/shell-tree-France-Avricourt.jpg?w=1000)](https://cdn.britannica.com/07/200607-050-A80B4173/shell-tree-France-Avricourt.jpg)

[World War I](https://cdn.britannica.com/07/200607-050-A80B4173/shell-tree-France-Avricourt.jpg)Unexploded shell lodged in a tree near Avricourt, France.(more)

The French government estimates that millions of unexploded shells from World War I remain buried or undiscovered in the French countryside. Every year, bomb-clearing units remove more than 40 tons of unexploded munitions from the Verdun area alone.
