# 2.4 GHz radio use

2.4 GHz radio use

Uses of the 2.4 GHz ISM radio spectrum

There are several uses of the 2.4GHz [ISM radio band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISM_radio_band "ISM radio band"). Interference may occur between devices operating at [2.4 GHz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2.4_GHz "2.4 GHz"). This article details the different users of the 2.4GHz band, how they cause interference to other users and how they are prone to interference from other users.

## Phone

Main articles: [Cordless telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordless_telephone "Cordless telephone") and [Baby monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_monitor "Baby monitor")

Many\[ _[quantify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_\] of the [cordless telephones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordless_telephone "Cordless telephone") and [baby monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_monitor "Baby monitor") in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") and [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada "Canada") use the 2.4GHz frequency,[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1) the same frequency at which [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") standards [802.11b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/802.11b "802.11b"), [802.11g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/802.11g "802.11g"), [802.11n](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/802.11n "802.11n") and [802.11ax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/802.11ax "802.11ax") operate. This can cause a significant decrease in speed, or sometimes\[ _[when?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_\] the total blocking of the Wi-Fi signal when a conversation on the phone takes place.[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)

## Bluetooth

Main article: [Bluetooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth "Bluetooth")

[Bluetooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth "Bluetooth") devices intended for use in short-range [personal area networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_area_network "Personal area network") operate from 2.4 to 2.4835GHz. To reduce interference with other protocols that use the 2.45GHz band, the Bluetooth protocol divides the band into 80 channels (numbered from 0 to 79, each 1MHz wide. Bluetooth Low Energy has half the number of channels, with each channel twice as wide as Bluetooth Classic), and changes channels up to 1600 times per second. Bluetooth also features _Adaptive Frequency Hopping_ which attempts to detect existing signals in the [ISM band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISM_band "ISM band"), such as [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") channels, and avoid them by negotiating a channel map between the communicating Bluetooth devices.

The USB 3.0 computer cable standard has been proven to generate significant amounts of electromagnetic interference that can interfere with any Bluetooth devices a user has connected to the same computer.[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3)

## Wi-Fi

Main article: [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi")

Wi-Fi is technology for radio [wireless local area networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_local_area_network "Wireless local area network") of devices based on the [IEEE 802.11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_802.11 "IEEE 802.11") standards. _Wi‑Fi_ is a trademark of the [Wi-Fi Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi_Alliance "Wi-Fi Alliance"), which restricts the use of the term _Wi-Fi Certified_ to products that successfully complete [interoperability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interoperability "Interoperability") certification testing.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4)

Devices that can use Wi-Fi technologies include [desktops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_computer "Desktop computer") and [laptops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptop "Laptop"), [video game consoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_console "Video game console"), [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone "Smartphone") and [tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer "Tablet computer"), [smart TVs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_TV "Smart TV"), digital audio players, cars and modern printers. Wi-Fi compatible devices can connect to the Internet via a WLAN and a [wireless access point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_access_point "Wireless access point"). Such an access point (or [hotspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotspot_(Wi-Fi) "Hotspot (Wi-Fi)")) has a range of about 20 meters (66 feet) indoors and a greater range outdoors. Hotspot coverage can be as small as a single room with walls that block radio waves, or as large as many square kilometres achieved by using multiple overlapping access points.

Different versions of Wi-Fi exist, with different ranges, radio bands and speeds. Wi-Fi most commonly uses the 2.4 gigahertz (12cm) [UHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UHF "UHF") and 5.8 gigahertz (5cm) [SHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_high_frequency "Super high frequency") [ISM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISM_band "ISM band") radio bands; these bands are subdivided into multiple channels. Each channel can be [time-shared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-division_multiple_access "Time-division multiple access") by multiple networks. These [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength "Wavelength") work best for [line-of-sight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-of-sight_propagation "Line-of-sight propagation"). Many common materials absorb or reflect them, which further restricts range, but can tend to help minimise interference between different networks in crowded environments. At close range, some versions of Wi-Fi, running on suitable hardware, can achieve speeds of over 1 Gbit/s.

Anyone within range with a [wireless network interface controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_network_interface_controller "Wireless network interface controller") can attempt to access a network; because of this, Wi-Fi is more vulnerable to attack (called [eavesdropping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eavesdropping "Eavesdropping")) than wired networks. [Wi-Fi Protected Access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi_Protected_Access "Wi-Fi Protected Access") (WPA) is a family of technologies created to protect information moving across Wi-Fi networks and includes solutions for personal and enterprise networks. Security features of WPA have included stronger protections and new security practices as the security landscape has changed over time.

To guarantee no interference in any circumstances, the Wi-Fi protocol requires 16.25 (11g/n) or 22MHz (11b) of channel separation (as shown below). Any remaining gap is used as a [guard band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guard_band "Guard band") to allow sufficient attenuation along the edge channels. This guardband is mainly used to accommodate older routers with modem chipsets prone to full channel occupancy, as most modern Wi-Fi modems are not prone to excessive channel occupancy.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/2.4_GHz_Wi-Fi_channels_%28802.11b%2Cg_WLAN%29.svg/1280px-2.4_GHz_Wi-Fi_channels_%28802.11b%2Cg_WLAN%29.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2.4_GHz_Wi-Fi_channels_(802.11b,g_WLAN).svg) Graphical representation of overlapping 22MHz channels within the 2.4GHz band

While overlapping frequencies can be configured and will usually work, it can cause interference, resulting in slowdowns, sometimes severe, particularly in heavy use. Certain subsets of frequencies can be used simultaneously at any one location without interference (see diagrams for typical allocations):

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/NonOverlappingChannels2.4GHz802.11-en.svg/960px-NonOverlappingChannels2.4GHz802.11-en.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NonOverlappingChannels2.4GHz802.11-en.svg) Graphical representation of Wireless LAN channels in 2.4GHz band. Channels 12 and 13 are customarily unused in North America. As a result, the usual 20MHz allocation becomes 1/6/11, the same as 11b. Note "channel 3" in the 40MHz diagram above is often labelled with the 20MHz channel numbers "1+5" or "1" with "+ Upper" or "5" with "+ Lower" in router interfaces, and "11" as "9+13" or "9" with "+ Upper" or "13" with "+ Lower".

However, the exact spacing required when the transmitters are not colocated depends on the protocol, the data rate selected, the distances and the electromagnetic environment where the equipment is used.[&#8202;](#ref-back-adjacentchannels-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-adjacentchannels)

The attenuation by relative channel adds to that due to distance and the effects of obstacles. Per the standards, for transmitters on the same channel, transmitters must take turns to transmit if they can detect each other 3dB above the [noise floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_floor "Noise floor") (the thermal noise floor is around -101 [dBm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DBm "DBm") for 20MHz channels).[&#8202;](#ref-back-7-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-7) On the other hand, transmitters will ignore transmitters on other channels if the attenuated signal strength from them is below a threshold Pth which, for non [Wi-Fi 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi_6 "Wi-Fi 6") systems, is between -76 and -80dBm.[&#8202;](#ref-back-adjacentchannels-2)[\[5\]](#refnote-adjacentchannels) While there can be interference (bit errors) at a receiver, this is usually small if the received signal is more than 20dB above the attenuated signal strength from transmitters on the other channels.[&#8202;](#ref-back-adjacentchannels-3)[\[5\]](#refnote-adjacentchannels)

The overall effect is that if there is considerable overlap between adjacent channel transmitters, they will often interfere with each other. In general, using every fourth or fifth channel by leaving three or four channels clear between used channels causes much less interference than sharing channels, and narrower spacing still can be used at further distances.[&#8202;](#ref-back-compnetworking.about.com-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-compnetworking.about.com)[&#8202;](#ref-back-adjacentchannels-4)[\[5\]](#refnote-adjacentchannels)

## Zigbee/IEEE 802.15.4 wireless data networks

Many [Zigbee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zigbee "Zigbee")/ [IEEE 802.15.4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_802.15.4 "IEEE 802.15.4")-based wireless data networks operate in the 2.4–2.4835GHz band, and so are subject to interference from other devices operating in that same band. The definition is for 16 channels numbered 11–26 to occupy the space, each 2MHz wide and spaced by 5MHz. The F0 of channel 11 is set at 2.405GHz. The DSSS scheme is used to spread out the spectrum (from a data-rate of 250 kbit/s) and reduce interference.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nxpbee-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-nxpbee)

To avoid interference from [IEEE 802.11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_802.11 "IEEE 802.11") networks, an IEEE 802.15.4 network can be configured to only use channels 15, 20, 25, and 26, avoiding frequencies used by the commonly used [IEEE 802.11 channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_WLAN_channels "List of WLAN channels") 1, 6, and 11. The exact channel selection depends on the local popular 802.11 channel. For example, in a place that uses 1, 7, and 13 channels, the preference would be for channels 15, 16, 21, and 22. Channel coexistence is possible provided 8 meters of spacing between the 802.11 access point and the 802.15.4 device.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nxpbee-2)[\[6\]](#refnote-nxpbee)

## RF peripherals

Some wireless peripherals like keyboards and mice use the 2.4GHz band with a proprietary protocol.

## Amateur radio

[Amateur radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio "Amateur radio") operators in the US are able to operate from 2300 to 2450MHz, except for 2310 to 2390MHz. The frequencies between 2400 and 2410MHz are reserved in the United States for amateur satellite communications, and 2410 to 2450MHz for broadband activities. Amateur radio operators are permitted up to 1500 [watts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt "Watt") of power and 22MHz of bandwidth in the 2400MHz band. Various portions of the 2400MHz band are available to amateur radio operators in [many other countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio_frequency_allocations "Amateur radio frequency allocations").[&#8202;](#ref-back-9-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-9)[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10)[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)

## Microwave oven

Main article: [Microwave oven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_oven "Microwave oven")

Most domestic [microwave ovens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_oven "Microwave oven") operate by emitting a very high power signal in the 2.4GHz band. Older devices have poor shielding,[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12) and often emit a very "dirty" signal over the entire 2.4GHz band.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[a\]](#refnote-14)

This can cause considerable difficulties to [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") and video[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-15) transmission, resulting in reduced range or complete blocking of the signal.

The [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE "IEEE") [802.11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/802.11 "802.11") committee that developed the Wi-Fi specification conducted an extensive investigation into the interference potential of microwave ovens. A typical microwave oven uses a self-oscillating [vacuum power tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube "Vacuum tube") called a [magnetron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetron "Magnetron") and a high voltage [power supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_supply "Power supply") with a [half wave rectifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifier#Half-wave_rectification "Rectifier") (often with [voltage doubling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_doubler "Voltage doubler")) and no DC [filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_filter "Electronic filter"). This produces an RF [pulse train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_train "Pulse train") with a [duty cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty_cycle "Duty cycle") below 50% as the tube is completely off for half of every [AC mains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mains_electricity "Mains electricity") cycle: 8.33ms in 60Hz countries and 10ms in 50Hz countries.

This property gave rise to a Wi-Fi microwave oven interference robustness mode that segments larger data frames into fragments, each small enough to fit into the oven's off periods.

The 802.11 committee also found that although the instantaneous frequency of a microwave oven magnetron varies widely over each half AC cycle with the instantaneous supply voltage, at any instant it is relatively [coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_(physics) "Coherence (physics)"), i.e., it occupies only a narrow bandwidth.[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-16) The [802.11a/g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_802.11g-2003 "IEEE 802.11g-2003") signal is inherently robust against such interference because it uses [OFDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OFDM "OFDM") with [error correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction "Error correction") information interleaved across the carriers; as long as only a few carriers are wiped out by strong narrow band interference, the information in them can be regenerated by the error correcting code from the carriers that do get through.

## Audio-visual (AV) devices

### Baby monitors

Main article: [Baby monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_monitor "Baby monitor")

Some [baby monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_monitor "Baby monitor") use the 2.4GHz band. Some transmit only audio, but others also provide video.

### Audio devices

#### Wireless microphones

Main article: [Wireless microphone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_microphone "Wireless microphone")

[Wireless microphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_microphone "Wireless microphone") operate as transmitters. Some digital wireless microphones use the 2.4GHz band (e.g., AKG model DPT 70).

#### Wireless speakers

Main article: [Wireless speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_speaker "Wireless speaker")

[Wireless speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_speaker "Wireless speaker") operate as receivers. The transmitter is a [preamplifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preamplifier "Preamplifier") that may be integrated in another device. Some wireless speakers use the 2.4GHz band, with a proprietary protocol. They may be subject to dropouts caused by interference from other devices.

### Video devices

Main article: [Video sender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_sender "Video sender")

[Video senders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_sender "Video sender") typically operate using an [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_modulation "Frequency modulation") [carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrier_wave "Carrier wave") to carry a video signal from one room to another (for example, [satellite TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_TV "Satellite TV") or [closed-circuit television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-circuit_television "Closed-circuit television")). These devices typically operate continuously but have low (10mW) transmit power. However, some devices, especially wireless cameras, operate with (often unauthorized) high power levels, and have [high-gain antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-gain_antenna "High-gain antenna").\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

[Amateur radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio "Amateur radio") operators can transmit two-way [amateur television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_television "Amateur television") (and voice) in the 2.4GHz band—and all ISM frequencies above 902MHz—with maximum power of 1500watts in the US if the transmission mode does not include [spread spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_spectrum "Spread spectrum") techniques.[&#8202;](#ref-back-17-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-17)[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-18) Other power levels apply per regions. In the UK, the maximum power level for a full licence is 400watts.[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-19) In other countries, maximum power level for non-spread-spectrum emissions are set by local legislation.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Although the transmitter of some video cameras appears to be fixed on one frequency, it has been found in several models that the cameras are actually frequency agile, and can have their frequency changed by disassembling the product and moving solder links or [DIP switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIP_switch "DIP switch") inside the camera.

These devices are prone to interference from other 2.4GHz devices, due to the nature of an [analog video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_video "Analog video") signal showing up interference very easily. A carrier-to-noise ratio of some 20dB is required to give a "clean" picture.

Continuous transmissions interfere with these, causing "patterning" on the picture, sometimes a dark or light shift, or complete blocking of the signal.

Non-continuous transmissions, such as Wi-Fi, cause horizontal noise bars to appear on the screen, and can cause "popping" or "clicking" to be heard in the audio.

#### Wi-Fi networks

Main article: [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi")

Video senders are a big problem for Wi-Fi networks: Unlike intermittent Wi-Fi, they operate continuously and are typically only 10MHz in bandwidth. This causes a very intense signal as viewed on a spectrum analyser, and completely obliterates over half a channel. The result of this, typically in a [Wireless Internet service provider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_Internet_service_provider "Wireless Internet service provider")-type environment, is that clients (who cannot hear the video sender due to the " [hidden node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_node "Hidden node")" effect) can hear the Wi-Fi without any issues, but the receiver on the WISP's access point is completely obliterated by the video sender, so is extremely deaf. Furthermore, due to the nature of video senders, they are not interfered with by Wi-Fi easily, since the receiver and transmitter are typically located very close together, so the capture effect is very high. Wi-Fi also has a very wide spectrum, so only typically 30% of the peak power of the Wi-Fi actually affects the video sender. Wi-Fi is not a continuous transmission, so the Wi-Fi signal interferes only intermittently with the video sender. A combination of these factors - low power output of the Wi-Fi compared to the video sender, the fact that typically the video sender is far closer to the receiver than the Wi-Fi transmitter and the FM capture effect means that a video sender may cause problems to Wi-Fi over a wide area, but the Wi-Fi unit causes few problems to the video sender.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

#### Effective isotropic radiated power (EIRP)

Many video senders on the market in the UK advertise a 100mW [equivalent isotropically radiated power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_isotropically_radiated_power "Equivalent isotropically radiated power") (EIRP). However, the UK market only permits a 10mW EIRP limit. These devices cause far more interference across a far wider area, due to their excessive power. Furthermore, UK video senders are required to operate across a 20MHz bandwidth (not to be confused with 20MHz [deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_deviation "Frequency deviation")).

This more widely spread our and stronger power means that some foreign imported video senders are not legal, since they operate on a 15MHz bandwidth or lower, which causes a higher spectral power density, increasing the interference. Furthermore, most other countries permit 100 mW EIRP for video senders, meaning a lot of video senders in the UK have excessive power outputs.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

## Radio control

Main article: [Radio control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_control "Radio control")

### Radio-controlled models

Many radio-controlled drones, model aircraft, model boats and toys use the 2.4GHz band. These radio systems can go up to 500meters in radio-controlled cars and over 2.5 kilometres (1.6mi) in drones and airplanes.

### Garage doors

Some garage door openers use the 2.4GHz band.

## Car alarm

Main article: [Car alarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_alarm "Car alarm")

Certain car manufacturers use the 2.4GHz frequency for their [car alarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_alarm "Car alarm") internal movement sensors. These devices transmit on 2.45GHz (between channels 8 and 9) at a strength of 500mW. Because of channel overlap, this will cause problems for channels 6 and 11, which are commonly used default channels for Wi-Fi connections. Because the signal is transmitted as a continuous tone, it causes particular problems for Wi-Fi traffic. This can be clearly seen with spectrum analysers. These devices, due to their short range and high power, are typically not susceptible to interference from other devices on the 2.4GHz band.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

## Radars

Some [radars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radars "Radars") use the 2.4GHz band.
