**Daniel Reed Crenshaw** (born March 14, 1984) is an American politician and former [United States Navy SEAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_SEAL "United States Navy SEAL") officer serving as the [U.S. representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._representative "U.S. representative") for [Texas's 2nd congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas's_2nd_congressional_district "Texas's 2nd congressional district") since 2019. He is a member of the [Republican Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States) "Republican Party (United States)").

Crenshaw was a commissioned officer in the [U.S. Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy"), and served on [SEAL Team 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_SEALs#Navy_SEAL_teams_and_structures "United States Navy SEALs") in the [War in Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_(2001%E2%80%932021) "War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)"), reaching the rank of [lieutenant commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_commander_(United_States) "Lieutenant commander (United States)"). He was [wounded in action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wounded_in_action "Wounded in action") during his third deployment, losing his right eye to an [improvised explosive device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvised_explosive_device "Improvised explosive device"). He was later a legislative assistant to Representative [Pete Sessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Sessions "Pete Sessions").

Crenshaw was first elected to Congress in [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2018 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas"). In [2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2026 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas"), he lost renomination in the Republican primary to [state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_House_of_Representatives "Texas House of Representatives") [Steve Toth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Toth "Steve Toth").

## Early life and education

Daniel Reed Crenshaw[&#8202;](#ref-back-1-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-1) was born to American parents in [Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen "Aberdeen"), Scotland,[&#8202;](#ref-back-2-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-2)[&#8202;](#ref-back-3-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-3) on March 14, 1984.[&#8202;](#ref-back-4-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-4) He grew up in [Katy, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katy,_Texas "Katy, Texas").[&#8202;](#ref-back-5-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-5) His mother Susan died of cancer when he was ten years old.[&#8202;](#ref-back-wapo-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-wapo)[&#8202;](#ref-back-JInsider05072020-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-JInsider05072020) His father, Jim Crenshaw, is a [petroleum engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_engineering "Petroleum engineering") who worked abroad, and Crenshaw spent time growing up in [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuador "Ecuador") and [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombia "Colombia"), developing proficiency in Spanish.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Jervis2018-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-Jervis2018) In 2002, he graduated from [Colegio Nueva Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colegio_Nueva_Granada "Colegio Nueva Granada") in [Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1 "Bogotá"), Colombia.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Jervis2018-1)[\[8\]](#refnote-Jervis2018)

After high school, Crenshaw returned to the United States and attended [Tufts University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tufts_University "Tufts University"), graduating in 2006 with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in [international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_relations "International relations") and a minor in physics.[&#8202;](#ref-back-running-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-running)[&#8202;](#ref-back-10-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-10) After a decade of military service, he studied public administration at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University")'s [John F. Kennedy School of Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy_School_of_Government "John F. Kennedy School of Government"), receiving a [Master of Public Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Public_Administration "Master of Public Administration") in 2017.[&#8202;](#ref-back-ops-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-ops) He worked as a military [legislative assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_assistant "Legislative assistant") for U.S. representative [Pete Sessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Sessions "Pete Sessions").[&#8202;](#ref-back-running-1)[\[9\]](#refnote-running)[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12)

## Military service

While at Tufts, Crenshaw joined the [Naval Reserve Officers Training Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Reserve_Officers_Training_Corps "Naval Reserve Officers Training Corps") and received an officer's commission in the [U.S. Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy") after graduation.[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13) He received orders to [Basic Underwater Demolition/SEAL training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_SEAL_selection_and_training "United States Navy SEAL selection and training") (BUD/S) at [Naval Amphibious Base Coronado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Amphibious_Base_Coronado "Naval Amphibious Base Coronado"). After six months of training, Crenshaw graduated with BUD/S class 264.[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14) He completed SEAL qualification training in June 2008 and received the 1130 designator as a Naval Special Warfare Officer, entitled to wear the [Special Warfare Insignia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Warfare_Insignia "Special Warfare Insignia"). Crenshaw served for ten years[&#8202;](#ref-back-bid-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-bid) and five [tours of duty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tours_of_duty "Tours of duty"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-16-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-16) reaching the rank of [lieutenant commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_commander_(United_States) "Lieutenant commander (United States)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-ops-1)[\[11\]](#refnote-ops) His first deployment was to [Fallujah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallujah "Fallujah"), Iraq, where he joined [SEAL Team Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEAL_Team_Three "SEAL Team Three").[&#8202;](#ref-back-TXtribune2018-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-TXtribune2018) He was based out of Naval Amphibious Base Coronado in [Coronado, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronado,_California "Coronado, California").[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18)

Crenshaw lost his right eye in 2012 during his third deployment when he was hit by an [IED explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvised_explosive_device "Improvised explosive device") in Afghanistan's [Helmand Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmand_Province "Helmand Province"). The blast destroyed his eye, and he required surgery to save the vision in his left eye.[&#8202;](#ref-back-bid-1)[\[15\]](#refnote-bid) He remained in the Navy for four years after the injury, and served his fourth and fifth tours of duty in [Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain "Bahrain") and South Korea.[&#8202;](#ref-back-bid-2)[\[15\]](#refnote-bid)[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto1-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-auto1)

As a Navy SEAL, Crenshaw was awarded two [Bronze Star Medals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Star_Medal "Bronze Star Medal"), one with ["V" device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22V%22_device "\"V\" device"), the [Purple Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_Heart "Purple Heart"), and the [Navy and Marine Corps Commendation Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy_and_Marine_Corps_Commendation_Medal "Navy and Marine Corps Commendation Medal") with [valor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22V%22_device "\"V\" device").[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-20) He medically retired from military service in 2016 with the rank of [lieutenant commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_commander_(United_States) "Lieutenant commander (United States)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-bid-3)[\[15\]](#refnote-bid)[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto1-1)[\[19\]](#refnote-auto1)

## U.S. House of Representatives

### Elections

#### 2018

See also: [2018 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas §District 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2018 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas")

In 2018, Crenshaw ran for the [United States House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives "United States House of Representatives") in [Texas's 2nd congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas's_2nd_congressional_district "Texas's 2nd congressional district"), which includes northern and western Houston, including [Kingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingwood,_Texas "Kingwood, Texas"), [Humble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humble,_Texas "Humble, Texas"), [Atascocita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atascocita,_Texas "Atascocita, Texas"), [Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring,_Texas "Spring, Texas"), and the [Rice University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_University "Rice University") area, to succeed the retiring [Ted Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Poe "Ted Poe").[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-21) He announced his candidacy in November 2017.[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22) Crenshaw credited national security analyst [John Noonan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Noonan_(analyst) "John Noonan (analyst)") for encouraging him to run for Congress.[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23) In a February 2018 interview, he said that border security and immigration reform would be two of his campaign issues.[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24)

Crenshaw and [Kevin Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Roberts_(politician) "Kevin Roberts (politician)") advanced from the nine-candidate first round of the [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States) "Republican Party (United States)") [primary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_primary "Partisan primary") to face each other in a [runoff election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_election "Runoff election");[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-25) Crenshaw received 155 votes more than [Kathaleen Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathaleen_Wall "Kathaleen Wall"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-26-0)[\[26\]](#refnote-26) a candidate backed by Senator [Ted Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Cruz "Ted Cruz")[&#8202;](#ref-back-27-0)[\[27\]](#refnote-27) and Governor [Greg Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Abbott "Greg Abbott").[&#8202;](#ref-back-28-0)[\[28\]](#refnote-28) The lead-up to the runoff election was contentious.[&#8202;](#ref-back-29-0)[\[29\]](#refnote-29) A [super PAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_PAC "Super PAC") funded by Roberts' brother-in-law, [Mark Lanier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Lanier "Mark Lanier"), focused on Crenshaw's 2015 criticisms of then-presidential candidate [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), despite Roberts having also been critical of Trump in the past. The ads also compared Crenshaw's policy proposals to those of President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama") and Senator [Bernie Sanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernie_Sanders "Bernie Sanders").[&#8202;](#ref-back-30-0)[\[30\]](#refnote-30) Gaining the endorsement of Senator [Tom Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Cotton "Tom Cotton"), Crenshaw received national attention, appearing in print and television, including on [Laura Ingraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Ingraham "Laura Ingraham")'s show on [Fox Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Business "Fox Business").[&#8202;](#ref-back-31-0)[\[31\]](#refnote-31)

Crenshaw won the runoff to advance to the November general election.[&#8202;](#ref-back-32-0)[\[32\]](#refnote-32)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Runoff-results-0)[\[33\]](#refnote-Runoff-results) On November 6, he defeated Democratic nominee [Todd Litton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todd_Litton "Todd Litton"), 52.8% to 45.6%.[&#8202;](#ref-back-TXSOS11062018-0)[\[34\]](#refnote-TXSOS11062018)[&#8202;](#ref-back-35-0)[\[35\]](#refnote-35) After the election, Crenshaw called for the depoliticization of comedy and sports and expressed a desire that political rhetoric be toned down.[&#8202;](#ref-back-36-0)[\[36\]](#refnote-36)

On the November 3 episode of _[Saturday Night Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturday_Night_Live "Saturday Night Live")_, comedian [Pete Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Davidson "Pete Davidson") joked about the appearances of multiple candidates in the 2018 midterm elections and described Crenshaw as looking like a "hit man in a porno movie" while adding that he lost his eye in "war or whatever". The joke received widespread criticism[&#8202;](#ref-back-37-0)[\[37\]](#refnote-37)[&#8202;](#ref-back-38-0)[\[38\]](#refnote-38) and on the following episode, Davidson and Crenshaw appeared on air together. Davidson offered an apology, which Crenshaw accepted. Crenshaw also used the segment to advocate for [veterans' issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_issues_affecting_military_veterans "Health issues affecting military veterans").[&#8202;](#ref-back-wapo-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-wapo)[&#8202;](#ref-back-auto-0)[\[39\]](#refnote-auto) Crenshaw and others have speculated that the joke may have helped him win,[&#8202;](#ref-back-40-0)[\[40\]](#refnote-40)[&#8202;](#ref-back-41-0)[\[41\]](#refnote-41) as well as aided later fundraising.[&#8202;](#ref-back-HousChron10162020-0)[\[42\]](#refnote-HousChron10162020)
Two years later, Davidson rescinded his apology, saying he had been "forced to apologize", adding that "I didn't think I did anything wrong." In response, Crenshaw called it "a little sad" that Davidson "can't stop thinking" about him.[&#8202;](#ref-back-43-0)[\[43\]](#refnote-43)

#### 2020

See also: [2020 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas §District 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2020 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas")

Crenshaw was reelected in 2020, defeating Democratic nominee Sima Ladjevardian from Houston, Texas[&#8202;](#ref-back-44-0)[\[44\]](#refnote-44) with 55.6% of the vote to Ladjevardian's 42.8%.[&#8202;](#ref-back-45-0)[\[45\]](#refnote-45) During the campaign, he spent over $11 million through October 16, 2020, making it one of the most expensive Congressional races in the country.[&#8202;](#ref-back-HousChron10162020-1)[\[42\]](#refnote-HousChron10162020)

#### 2022

See also: [2022 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas §District 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2022 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas")

Crenshaw was reelected in 2022, defeating Democratic nominee Robin Fulford by a nearly two-to-one margin.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Texas_Second_Congressional_District_Election_Results-0)[\[46\]](#refnote-Texas_Second_Congressional_District_Election_Results)

#### 2024

See also: [2024 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas §District 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2024 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas")

Crenshaw was reelected in 2024, defeating Democratic nominee Peter Filler by 66% to 34%.[&#8202;](#ref-back-47-0)[\[47\]](#refnote-47)

#### 2026

See also: [2026 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas §District 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Texas#District_2 "2026 United States House of Representatives elections in Texas")

Crenshaw, the only House Republican from Texas seeking re-election without a Trump endorsement,[&#8202;](#ref-back-nytwithout-0)[\[48\]](#refnote-nytwithout)[&#8202;](#ref-back-todaywithout-0)[\[49\]](#refnote-todaywithout) lost the Republican primary to [Steve Toth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Toth "Steve Toth"), who campaigned to his right.[&#8202;](#ref-back-50-0)[\[50\]](#refnote-50)[&#8202;](#ref-back-51-0)[\[51\]](#refnote-51) Even though Crenshaw raised $1.3 million more than his opponent, Crenshaw lost to Toth[&#8202;](#ref-back-52-0)[\[52\]](#refnote-52) in a redrawn district where newly added territory in [Montgomery County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_County,_Texas "Montgomery County, Texas") brought in more of Toth’s supporters, according to reports.[&#8202;](#ref-back-53-0)[\[53\]](#refnote-53)

### Tenure

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Dan_Crenshaw%2C_16_July_2020.jpg/500px-Dan_Crenshaw%2C_16_July_2020.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dan_Crenshaw,_16_July_2020.jpg) Crenshaw meeting with [Sonny Perdue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Perdue "Sonny Perdue") and [John Cornyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cornyn "John Cornyn") in 2020

Crenshaw spoke at the [2020 Republican National Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Republican_National_Convention "2020 Republican National Convention") on August 26, 2020.[&#8202;](#ref-back-54-0)[\[54\]](#refnote-54)[&#8202;](#ref-back-55-0)[\[55\]](#refnote-55)

The Inspector General of the [Department of Veterans Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Veterans_Affairs "Department of Veterans Affairs") implicated Crenshaw and V.A. Secretary [Robert Wilkie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wilkie "Robert Wilkie") in a 2020 report as having engaged in a campaign of disparagement toward a female veteran who reported sexual assault to the Navy. Crenshaw said, "The Democrats created this narrative".[&#8202;](#ref-back-56-0)[\[56\]](#refnote-56)[&#8202;](#ref-back-57-0)[\[57\]](#refnote-57)

In March 2021, _[The Daily Beast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Beast "The Daily Beast")_ reported that Crenshaw had violated the [Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge (STOCK) Act of 2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STOCK_Act "STOCK Act"), a federal transparency and conflict-of-interest law, by failing to properly disclose stock trades that he made in March 2020.[&#8202;](#ref-back-58-0)[\[58\]](#refnote-58)

Crenshaw is publicly critical of the [Freedom Caucus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Caucus "Freedom Caucus"), whom he regards as divisive "performance artists" for constantly attacking moderate Republicans.[&#8202;](#ref-back-mediaite-0)[\[59\]](#refnote-mediaite)

In February 2025, a [hot mic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_mic "Hot mic") captured Crenshaw stating that he wanted to "fucking kill" conservative commentator [Tucker Carlson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucker_Carlson "Tucker Carlson").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Carlson-0)[\[60\]](#refnote-Carlson) Carlson and Crenshaw have previously feuded; Carlson has dubbed Crenshaw "Eyepatch [McCain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCain "John McCain")" and one of the "dumbest" Republicans, while Crenshaw has called Carlson a "cowardly, know-nothing elitist".[&#8202;](#ref-back-61-0)[\[61\]](#refnote-61) Crenshaw's comment sparked backlash from other conservatives, including Rep. [Marjorie Taylor Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Taylor_Greene "Marjorie Taylor Greene") and [Elon Musk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk "Elon Musk").[&#8202;](#ref-back-62-0)[\[62\]](#refnote-62) Carlson responded "Why don't you come sit for an interview and we'll see how you do? I'll send you my address."[&#8202;](#ref-back-Carlson-1)[\[60\]](#refnote-Carlson)

On February 24, 2026, Crenshaw voted against the ROTOR Act, which sought to require the installation of "specific location transmission technology" in all aircraft and repeal "certain military exemptions from the technology requirements". The bill was introduced by Texas senator [Ted Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Cruz "Ted Cruz") in response to the [January 29, 2025, crash over the Potomac River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Potomac_River_mid-air_collision "2025 Potomac River mid-air collision") near [Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan_Washington_National_Airport "Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport").[&#8202;](#ref-back-63-0)[\[63\]](#refnote-63)

Wade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roe_v._Wade "Roe v. Wade")_ was a "bad precedent to set", and that abortion rights "should be decided by the states".[&#8202;](#ref-back-youtube.com-1)[\[68\]](#refnote-youtube.com) When the Supreme Court decision _[Dobbs v. The issue goes back to the states, back to the people."[&#8202;](#ref-back-70-0)[\[70\]](#refnote-70)

### Gun rights

Crenshaw opposes [gun control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_control "Gun control") measures.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate-0)[\[71\]](#refnote-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate)[&#8202;](#ref-back-72-0)[\[72\]](#refnote-72) In response to the shootings in [Dayton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Dayton_shooting "2019 Dayton shooting") and [El Paso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_El_Paso_shooting "2019 El Paso shooting"), after an initial comment to possibly explore [red flag laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_flag_laws "Red flag laws") as a possible solution to gun violence,[&#8202;](#ref-back-73-0)[\[73\]](#refnote-73) Crenshaw argued that such laws should be discussed at state level rather than nationally, saying, "What you're essentially trying to do with a red flag law is enforce the law before the law has been broken, and that's a really difficult thing to do" and "if there's such a threat that they're threatening somebody with a weapon already, then they've already broken the law, so why do you need this other law?"[&#8202;](#ref-back-74-0)[\[74\]](#refnote-74) after the 2022 [Robb Elementary School shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uvalde_school_shooting "Uvalde school shooting"). In January 2025 Crenshaw introduced the " [Preventing Unjust Red Flag Laws Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/223/cosponsors)," which stops federal money being given to states which have red flag laws. This bill is presently with the House Judiciary Committee. Crenshaw has also said that raising the legal age to purchase a firearm to 21 is ineffective but supports expanding background checks to include juvenile criminal history.[&#8202;](#ref-back-75-0)[\[75\]](#refnote-75)

In 2020 and 2022, Crenshaw received an "A+" rating and endorsement from the [NRA Political Victory Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NRA_Political_Victory_Fund "NRA Political Victory Fund").[&#8202;](#ref-back-76-0)[\[76\]](#refnote-76)[&#8202;](#ref-back-77-0)[\[77\]](#refnote-77)[&#8202;](#ref-back-78-0)[\[78\]](#refnote-78)

### Healthcare

Crenshaw favors repealing the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act "Affordable Care Act") (Obamacare), describing it as an "unmitigated disaster".[&#8202;](#ref-back-84-0)[\[84\]](#refnote-84) During his 2018 campaign, he advocated allowing [Medicare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicare_(United_States) "Medicare (United States)") to negotiate drug prices, becoming one of a handful of Republicans to endorse what was primarily a [progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism "Progressivism") idea.[&#8202;](#ref-back-STAT_backpedaled-0)[\[85\]](#refnote-STAT_backpedaled) By 2019, however, Crenshaw had retreated from this position.[&#8202;](#ref-back-STAT_backpedaled-1)[\[85\]](#refnote-STAT_backpedaled)

On May 24, 2019, Crenshaw co-sponsored a bill to extend time limits for claims under the [September 11th Victim Compensation Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11th_Victim_Compensation_Fund "September 11th Victim Compensation Fund") Act.[&#8202;](#ref-back-86-0)[\[86\]](#refnote-86)[&#8202;](#ref-back-87-0)[\[87\]](#refnote-87)[&#8202;](#ref-back-88-0)[\[88\]](#refnote-88)

Crenshaw has introduced legislation to fund research into the use of [psychedelic therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelic_therapy "Psychedelic therapy") as a treatment option for military members suffering from [PTSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Post-traumatic stress disorder") and traumatic brain injuries.[&#8202;](#ref-back-89-0)[\[89\]](#refnote-89)

This section is an excerpt from [Hyde Amendment § Crenshaw Amendment (2025)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyde_Amendment#Crenshaw_Amendment_(2025) "Hyde Amendment").\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hyde_Amendment&action=edit)\]

The Crenshaw Amendment was a legislative provision introduced by Republican Rep. Dan Crenshaw of Texas which took the same approach as the Hyde Amendment, but with gender transition procedures.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Hyde_Amendment_notus-0)[\[90\]](#refnote-Hyde_Amendment_notus) Dubbed "the new Hyde Amendment" by Crenshaw,[&#8202;](#ref-back-Hyde_Amendment_notus-1)[\[90\]](#refnote-Hyde_Amendment_notus) it would have prohibited the use of Federal funds in [Medicaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicaid "Medicaid"), the [Children's Health Insurance Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_Health_Insurance_Program "Children's Health Insurance Program") (CHIP) and the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act "Affordable Care Act") from paying "for specified gender transition procedures" for both children and adults alike. The provision would have defined these procedures to mean "those that are intended to change the body of an individual to no longer correspond to the individual's biological sex (male or female), including specified surgeries, implants, and medications (e.g., hormones)," and exclude "procedures that are provided to an individual under the age of 18 with the consent of a parent or [legal guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_guardian "Legal guardian") and that are intended to (1) rectify early puberty, genetic disorders, or chromosomal abnormalities; (2) reverse prior gender transition procedures; or (3) prevent imminent death or impairment of a major bodily function."[&#8202;](#ref-back-Hyde_Amendment_H.R.1_-_One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act-0)[\[91\]](#refnote-Hyde_Amendment_H.R.1_-_One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act) This legislation was included in the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act "One Big Beautiful Bill Act") as passed by the House of Representatives on May 22, 2025.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Hyde_Amendment_H.R.1_-_One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act-1)[\[91\]](#refnote-Hyde_Amendment_H.R.1_-_One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act)[&#8202;](#ref-back-92-0)[\[92\]](#refnote-92) On June 26, 2025, the [Senate Parliamentarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Parliamentarian "Senate Parliamentarian") ruled that the amendment violated the [Byrd Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byrd_Rule "Byrd Rule"), meaning that it would need to get 60 votes in the Senate in order to remain in the bill. Instead of bringing it up for a vote, Senate Republicans chose to remove the Crenshaw Amendment from the final bill entirely, shortly before voting to pass the final bill.[&#8202;](#ref-back-93-0)[\[93\]](#refnote-93) If it had passed, the amendment would have taken effect in 2027.[&#8202;](#ref-back-94-0)[\[94\]](#refnote-94)[&#8202;](#ref-back-95-0)[\[95\]](#refnote-95) In the aftermath of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the Trump Administration took administrative action to prevent Medicaid from paying "for specified sex trait modification" for children in Medicaid starting in 2026.[&#8202;](#ref-back-96-0)[\[96\]](#refnote-96)[&#8202;](#ref-back-97-0)[\[97\]](#refnote-97)

### Donald Trump

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/President_Trump_Delivers_Remarks_at_TPUSA_%2849259747427%29.jpg/500px-President_Trump_Delivers_Remarks_at_TPUSA_%2849259747427%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:President_Trump_Delivers_Remarks_at_TPUSA_(49259747427).jpg) Crenshaw with President [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump") in December 2019

According to _[Politico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politico "Politico")_, Crenshaw "voted with Donald Trump most of the time but isn't a loyalist. He's a stalwart conservative willing to criticize other conservatives."[&#8202;](#ref-back-98-0)[\[98\]](#refnote-98)

Although Crenshaw had criticized some of Trump's statements in a 2015 [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook "Facebook") post, he became a "staunch defender" of Trump after the 2016 election.[&#8202;](#ref-back-99-0)[\[99\]](#refnote-99)[&#8202;](#ref-back-100-0)[\[100\]](#refnote-100) He voted against both articles of impeachment the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives "United States House of Representatives") brought against Trump in 2019.[&#8202;](#ref-back-101-0)[\[101\]](#refnote-101)

In 2020, Crenshaw defended the Trump administration's response to the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States "COVID-19 pandemic in the United States").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Axios_watch_messaging-0)[\[102\]](#refnote-Axios_watch_messaging)[&#8202;](#ref-back-103-0)[\[103\]](#refnote-103) In a video Trump retweeted, Crenshaw rebutted criticisms that the Trump administration had been slow in responding to the virus.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Axios_watch_messaging-1)[\[102\]](#refnote-Axios_watch_messaging)

Crenshaw spoke at the [2020 Republican National Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Republican_National_Convention "2020 Republican National Convention"), calling the United States "a country of heroes."[&#8202;](#ref-back-104-0)[\[104\]](#refnote-104)[&#8202;](#ref-back-105-0)[\[105\]](#refnote-105) He was one of few convention speakers not to mention Trump.[&#8202;](#ref-back-106-0)[\[106\]](#refnote-106)[&#8202;](#ref-back-107-0)[\[107\]](#refnote-107)

Crenshaw criticized the [2021 U.S. Capitol attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_States_Capitol_attack "2021 United States Capitol attack") perpetrated by Trump supporters, and said that Trump should have ordered the rioters to stop. During the siege, he urged the protesters to "Stop this bullshit right now" on [Twitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitter "Twitter").[&#8202;](#ref-back-108-0)[\[108\]](#refnote-108)[&#8202;](#ref-back-109-0)[\[109\]](#refnote-109) Crenshaw condemned the rioting and some of his fellow congressional Representatives for "saying constantly this is our time to fight."[&#8202;](#ref-back-110-0)[\[110\]](#refnote-110) While not naming any politicians, Crenshaw stated they were "lying to millions" and scattered when there was an actual threat to the Capitol. He deemed efforts to fight the [Electoral College vote certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_States_Electoral_College_vote_count "2021 United States Electoral College vote count") unconstitutional, and voted against the objections to the electoral vote in both Arizona and Pennsylvania, but defended Senators [Ted Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Cruz "Ted Cruz") and [Josh Hawley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Hawley "Josh Hawley") against allegations that they stoked the riot.[&#8202;](#ref-back-111-0)[\[111\]](#refnote-111)[&#8202;](#ref-back-112-0)[\[112\]](#refnote-112)[&#8202;](#ref-back-113-0)[\[113\]](#refnote-113) Crenshaw voted against the [Trump impeachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump "Second impeachment of Donald Trump") on January 13, 2021.[&#8202;](#ref-back-114-0)[\[114\]](#refnote-114) In a statement, Crenshaw said that while Trump's words had encouraged "unconstitutional theories that risk the stability of our nation", he had voted against the second impeachment because he felt Democrats had "rushed" the process and that impeaching a president who only had seven days left in office would serve little purpose and inflame further tensions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-115-0)[\[115\]](#refnote-115)

After [Liz Cheney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Cheney "Liz Cheney") was censured for voting to impeach Trump, Crenshaw asserted in an interview that the Republican Party needed "to move on" from claims the 2020 election was stolen, but also accused the media of continuing to weaponize the issue, arguing both were ignoring larger issues such as the economy, the COVID pandemic and illegal immigration. He also said, "I do not think Trump is the devil, and I won't say that; I don't think he's Jesus either."[&#8202;](#ref-back-116-0)[\[116\]](#refnote-116) Crenshaw also supported Representative [Adam Kinzinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Kinzinger "Adam Kinzinger"), who sat on the [January 6 select committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Select_Committee_on_the_January_6_Attack "United States House Select Committee on the January 6 Attack") and is publicly critical of Trump, and criticized far-right members of the [Freedom Caucus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Caucus "Freedom Caucus") as "performance artists" and "grifters".[&#8202;](#ref-back-mediaite-1)[\[59\]](#refnote-mediaite)

In 2022, on his podcast _Hold These Truths_, Crenshaw criticized Republicans who contested the results of the 2020 presidential election, saying of their efforts, "It was always a lie. The whole thing was always a lie. And it was a lie meant to rile people up".[&#8202;](#ref-back-117-0)[\[117\]](#refnote-117)

### Election reform

In 2019, Crenshaw voiced opposition to the [For the People Act of 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_the_People_Act_of_2019 "For the People Act of 2019"), saying it would "limit free speech drastically". He also said the bill would use taxpayer money to "legalize" the kind of electoral fraud that he alleges occurred on the Republican side in the [2018 election for North Carolina's District 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_North_Carolina's_9th_congressional_district_election "2018 North Carolina's 9th congressional district election").[&#8202;](#ref-back-politifact_wrong_about_HR1-0)[\[118\]](#refnote-politifact_wrong_about_HR1) [PolitiFact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PolitiFact "PolitiFact") rated Crenshaw's assertion about the North Carolina race "false", adding, "nothing in the bill that expands who can collect absentee ballots, allows people to fill out ballots for others, or loosens witnessing procedures for absentee ballots", as happened in that election.[&#8202;](#ref-back-politifact_wrong_about_HR1-1)[\[118\]](#refnote-politifact_wrong_about_HR1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-119-0)[\[119\]](#refnote-119) Crenshaw argued that the bill did not include a federal ban on ballot harvesting, and supported the [American Civil Liberties Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union "American Civil Liberties Union")'s opposition to it over new campaign contribution revisions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-politifact_wrong_about_HR1-2)[\[118\]](#refnote-politifact_wrong_about_HR1)

### Environment

During Crenshaw's 2018 campaign, his website made brief mention of [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming "Global warming"), applauding Trump for withdrawing from the [Paris Climate Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Agreement "Paris Agreement").[&#8202;](#ref-back-eenews-0)[\[120\]](#refnote-eenews) Crenshaw called the agreement "costly and meaningless", [virtue signaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue_signalling "Virtue signalling"), and bad policy.[&#8202;](#ref-back-eenews-1)[\[120\]](#refnote-eenews) He also said, "We must use our money to develop better infrastructure."[&#8202;](#ref-back-eenews-2)[\[120\]](#refnote-eenews) In 2018, Crenshaw called for a debate on the causes of [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change"), adding, "We can't start off the conversation saying the climate is settled. What isn't clear is how our actions will serve to reverse that warming trend, and what the cost-benefit outcome would be. Regardless, we should continue pursuing new [green energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_energy "Green energy") solutions that lessen our impact on the environment and create cleaner air and water."[&#8202;](#ref-back-businessinsider_130_deny_climate_change-1)[\[121\]](#refnote-businessinsider_130_deny_climate_change) In 2020, he criticized [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy "Solar energy") and [wind energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_energy "Wind energy") as "silly solutions" that "don't work," and instead advocated expanding [nuclear energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power") and [carbon capture technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_capture_and_storage "Carbon capture and storage").[&#8202;](#ref-back-122-0)[\[122\]](#refnote-122)

During the [2021 Texas power crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Texas_power_crisis "2021 Texas power crisis"), Crenshaw argued that the [Green New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_New_Deal "Green New Deal") would lead to similar crises.[&#8202;](#ref-back-123-0)[\[123\]](#refnote-123)

### Immigration

In 2016, Crenshaw harshly criticized then-candidate Trump's "insane rhetoric" toward Muslims and "hateful" speech.[&#8202;](#ref-back-124-0)[\[124\]](#refnote-124) During Crenshaw's 2018 campaign, he defended Trump's proposal to [build a border wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall "Mexico–United States border wall") on the [Mexico–United States border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border "Mexico–United States border").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate-1)[\[71\]](#refnote-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate) In a May 2019 appearance on _[The View](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_View_(talk_show) "The View (talk show)")_, he claimed that 80%–90% of asylum seeker requests "don't have a valid asylum claim"; news outlet PolitiFact called the claim "false", writing that although only 20% to 30% of asylum claims are granted, "experts said there are many reasons why cases might be closed or requests might be denied, regardless of the merits of a claim."[&#8202;](#ref-back-125-0)[\[125\]](#refnote-125) In 2021, Crenshaw accused the Biden administration of provoking a crisis on the southern border by having a moratorium on deportations and reversing Trump's policies on asylum and illegal immigration.[&#8202;](#ref-back-126-0)[\[126\]](#refnote-126) He has also argued that [Mexican drug cartels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_drug_cartels "Mexican drug cartels") have fueled illegal immigration by taking advantage of the U.S. asylum process to smuggle people into the country.[&#8202;](#ref-back-127-0)[\[127\]](#refnote-127)

Crenshaw supports enforcing physical barriers and implementing technology at the southern border to prevent illegal immigration. He has also expressed a belief that people who try to enter the U.S. illegally "aren't bad people" but "they are breaking the law, and they're contributing to an unsustainable system" and are "cutting in front of the line of all the legal immigrants."[&#8202;](#ref-back-128-0)[\[128\]](#refnote-128)

Crenshaw voted for the Further Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2020, which authorizes DHS to nearly double the available H-2B visas for the remainder of FY 2020.[&#8202;](#ref-back-129-0)[\[129\]](#refnote-129)[&#8202;](#ref-back-130-0)[\[130\]](#refnote-130) He also voted for the Consolidated Appropriations Act (H.R. 1158), which effectively prohibits ICE from cooperating with Health and Human Services to detain or remove illegal alien sponsors of unaccompanied alien children (UACs).[&#8202;](#ref-back-131-0)[\[131\]](#refnote-131)

### Social issues

Despite having previously expressing support for the legalization of [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage "Same-sex marriage"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-mcclatchy_wants_to_lead-0)[\[132\]](#refnote-mcclatchy_wants_to_lead)[&#8202;](#ref-back-133-0)[\[133\]](#refnote-133) Crenshaw voted against the [Respect for Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respect_for_Marriage_Act "Respect for Marriage Act"), even after amendments allowing religious exemptions. Calling same-sex marriage a "tough issue",[&#8202;](#ref-back-134-0)[\[134\]](#refnote-134) Crenshaw believes that government should not be involved in regulating marriage. In 2015, he took issue with people trying to suggest Christianity is as shocking and as violent as Islam, saying, "the worst thing modern Christianity stands for is anti-homosexual marriage, which is a far cry from sex slaves, [sharia law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia_law "Sharia law") and beheadings."[&#8202;](#ref-back-135-0)[\[135\]](#refnote-135)[&#8202;](#ref-back-136-0)[\[136\]](#refnote-136)

In the case of a 7-year-old [transgender child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgender_youth "Transgender youth") who was the subject of a custody battle between a supportive mother and an unsupportive father, Crenshaw opined in favor of the father. The child, who had identified as a girl from the age of 3 years old, was seeking a [gender transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_transition "Gender transition"). Following a judge's decision to grant custody to the mother, Crenshaw called the outcome "heart-breaking" and added, "\[&#8202;](#ref-back-137-0)[a\] 7-year-old can't possibly make this decision or understand it. Parents should know better. I hope this father receives the public support he needs."[\[137\]](#refnote-137)

Crenshaw opposes federal funding to "subsidize college in general", but supports it in cases of vocational training.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate-2)[\[71\]](#refnote-Chron_Crenshaw,_Litton_debate) He opposes [cancel culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancel_culture "Cancel culture"),[&#8202;](#ref-back-138-0)[\[138\]](#refnote-138) and athletes [kneeling during the national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._national_anthem_protests_(2016-present) "U.S. national anthem protests (2016-present)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-139-0)[\[139\]](#refnote-139) He called Senator [Tammy Duckworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tammy_Duckworth "Tammy Duckworth") unpatriotic for wanting a discussion on which statues to remove, including those of [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington "George Washington").[&#8202;](#ref-back-140-0)[\[140\]](#refnote-140)

### Foreign policy

Crenshaw supports cooperation with and support for Israel.[&#8202;](#ref-back-JInsider05072020-1)[\[7\]](#refnote-JInsider05072020)[&#8202;](#ref-back-141-0)[\[141\]](#refnote-141) During some of his public appearances, he has been targeted by anti-semitic white nationalists, known as [Groypers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groypers "Groypers"), for his pro-Israel views.[&#8202;](#ref-back-142-0)[\[142\]](#refnote-142)[&#8202;](#ref-back-143-0)[\[143\]](#refnote-143) He voted to provide Israel with support following the [2023 Hamas attack on Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Hamas_attack_on_Israel "2023 Hamas attack on Israel").[&#8202;](#ref-back-144-0)[\[144\]](#refnote-144)[&#8202;](#ref-back-145-0)[\[145\]](#refnote-145) Crenshaw has received $233,000 from [AIPAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIPAC "AIPAC") as of 2025.[&#8202;](#ref-back-146-0)[\[146\]](#refnote-146)

In 2019, Crenshaw co-sponsored a resolution opposing Trump's decision to withdraw U.S. troops from Syria, saying that it would embolden the Turkish military's [assault on the Kurdish forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Turkish_offensive_into_north-eastern_Syria "2019 Turkish offensive into north-eastern Syria").[&#8202;](#ref-back-147-0)[\[147\]](#refnote-147) He supported Trump's decision to kill Iranian major general [Qasem Soleimani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qasem_Soleimani "Qasem Soleimani").[&#8202;](#ref-back-148-0)[\[148\]](#refnote-148)[&#8202;](#ref-back-149-0)[\[149\]](#refnote-149)

In April 2020, Crenshaw and Senator [Tom Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Cotton "Tom Cotton") introduced a bill that would allow civil suits against foreign states in incidents related to injury or death. The legislation came in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and calls for the [Chinese government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_China "Government of China") to be held accountable for "allow\[&#8202;](#ref-back-150-0)[ing\] this virus to spread".[\[150\]](#refnote-150)

In 2022, Crenshaw voiced support for a [$40 billion aid package to Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_military_aid_to_Ukraine_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War "List of military aid to Ukraine during the Russo-Ukrainian War"). [Fox News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_News "Fox News") political commentator [Tucker Carlson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucker_Carlson "Tucker Carlson") criticized him for this, calling Crenshaw "Eyepatch [McCain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCain "John McCain")"—a remark that itself drew much criticism.[&#8202;](#ref-back-151-0)[\[151\]](#refnote-151)[&#8202;](#ref-back-152-0)[\[152\]](#refnote-152)

## Personal life

Crenshaw married Tara Blake in 2013.[&#8202;](#ref-back-wapo-2)[\[6\]](#refnote-wapo) He is a [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist "Methodist")[&#8202;](#ref-back-160-0)[\[160\]](#refnote-160) and hosts _Hold These Truths_, a [podcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast") he launched in February 2020.[&#8202;](#ref-back-161-0)[\[161\]](#refnote-161) They have one daughter, Susan Crenshaw, born on September 12, 2023, named after his late mother.[&#8202;](#ref-back-162-0)[\[162\]](#refnote-162)[&#8202;](#ref-back-163-0)[\[163\]](#refnote-163)[&#8202;](#ref-back-164-0)[\[164\]](#refnote-164)

In April 2021, Crenshaw suffered a [detached retina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retinal_detachment "Retinal detachment") in his functional left eye, undergoing emergency surgery. As he recovered, he expected to be virtually blind for about a month. He said, "I don't have a 'good eye,' but half a good eye."[&#8202;](#ref-back-165-0)[\[165\]](#refnote-165)

## Recognition

In 2020, [_Fortune_ magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_(magazine) "Fortune (magazine)") included Crenshaw in its [40 Under 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/40_Under_40 "40 Under 40") in the "Government and Politics" category, writing that he "wears his service to his country on his face."[&#8202;](#ref-back-166-0)[\[166\]](#refnote-166)
