## Abstract

### Background

Cancer remains a major public health issue globally, with a growing burden due to population aging and lifestyle changes. This study provides the estimates of cancer burden in China for 2024.

### Methods

Cancer data of year 2019 from 919 population-based cancer registries across China submitted to the National Cancer Center were selected, with 106 registries providing continuous data from 2010 to 2019 being used to predict the burden of cancer in 2024. The Segi’s world standard population was used to estimate the age-standardized rates. Joinpoint regression analysis was used to evaluate the trends from 2000 to 2019 by calculating the annual percentage changes (APC) and average annual percentage changes (AAPC).

### Results

In 2024, an estimated 5,150,000 new cancer cases and 2,580,000 cancer deaths occurred in China. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 207.70 per 100,000 population and the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) was 90.90 per 100,000 population. Lung cancer was the most diagnosed cancer, followed by thyroid, colorectal, breast, and liver cancers. Collectively these top five cancers accounted for 59.13 % of all new cases. The leading cause of cancer death was lung cancer, followed by liver cancer, colorectal cancer, stomach cancer, and esophageal cancer, which together comprised of 66.75 % of all cancer deaths. The age-specific incidence rates increased with age for most cancer sites in men, but for cancers of breast, thyroid, ovarian, and cervix in women, the rates increased significantly in young and middle ages. The age-specific mortality rate increased with age and showed a higher level in men than in women. From 2000 to 2019, the ASIR for all cancers combined increased significantly (AAPC = 1.6 %), while the ASMR decreased (AAPC = −1.4 %). Cancers of the esophagus, stomach, and liver showed declining incidence and mortality trends, whereas thyroid, prostate, and cervical cancers exhibited significant increases in incidence.

### Conclusions

The cancer burden in China continued to be substantial, characterized by a coexistence of cancer patterns of both developing and developed countries. The results highlight the necessity for targeted policies within the “Healthy China 2030" initiative to address sex and age disparities, as well as evolving cancer patterns.

## 1\. Introduction

Cancer continues to be a major public health issue globally, representing a leading cause of morbidity and mortality that imposes substantial social, economic, and healthcare burdens. [1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0001), [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0002) As one of the world’s most populous country, China faces unique challenges in cancer control, driven by its rapid demographic aging, urbanization, and evolving lifestyle factors. [3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0003), [4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0004), [5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0005), [6](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0006) The year 2024 marked a critical moment for assessing the progress of national cancer prevention and control against cancer, with updated estimates providing important data for policymakers, researchers, and healthcare providers. These estimates are derived from qualified population-based cancer registry data, modeling trends to inform strategic interventions under initiatives like “Healthy China 2030”, which aim to reduce premature mortality from non-communicable diseases.

The 2024 estimates utilized comprehensive cancer data of 2019 from 919 population-based cancer registries across China, with 106 registries providing continuous monitoring data from 2010 to 2019, ensuring high-quality and representative coverage of the national population. The methods used in this analysis was consistent with those used to estimate the cancer burden in 2022, [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0007) emphasizing comparability and validity. The National Cancer Center (NCC) routinely undertakes the collection, quality control, and report of cancer registration data for the Chinese population. All hospitals and healthcare institutions within the administrative areas where cancer registration is covered must report cancer case statistics. As a routine work of the NCC, this report provides the latest estimated new cancer incidence and death data in China in 2024, and updates the trend of cancer incidence and mortality from 2000 to 2019, emphasizing the importance of evidence-based strategies to address ongoing and emerging challenges.

## 2\. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data source

The cancer incidence and mortality data were derived from the cancer registration data of 2019 submitted by 919 qualified cancer registries from 32 provinces (autonomous regions and municipalities), as received by the NCC in 2024. The quality control was carried out according to the criteria of International Agency for Research on Cancer/International Association of Cancer Registries (IARC/IACR) [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0008), [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0009) and the Guideline for Chinese Cancer Registration. [10](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0010)

Among these 919 cancer registries, 106 had continuous, stable, and reliable quality data covering the period from 2010 to 2019 that can be continuously accepted by the China Cancer Registry Annual Report. High-quality data from these registries were thus selected to estimate the trend by region (urban/rural), sex, cancer site and age group (0–4 years, 5–9 years,…, 80–84 years, and 85 years and above) and predict the incidence and mortality rates in 2024.

The data from 22 consecutive population-based cancer registries were used to analyze the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of all cancers and selected major cancers from 2000 to 2019 for the long temporal trends. These registries covered approximately 3.34 % of the national population of year 2024. Detailed information about these registries has been displayed in previous studies. [11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0011)

The population by region (urban/rural) and sex for 2024 was provided by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. The age-specific population in 2024 was estimated based on the population age structure of the Seventh National Census conducted in 2020, combined with the birth and death risks of previous years.

### 2.2. Statistical analysis

According to the data of 106 cancer registries with continuous data from 2010 to 2019, stratified by region (urban or rural area) and sex, the Bayesian age-period-cohort model [12](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0012), [13](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0013), [14](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0014) was used to fit the annual percentage changes (APC) for incidence and mortality models by cancer site and age group (0–4 years, 5–9 years,…, 80–84 years, and 85 years and above).

Based on the data of 919 qualified population-based cancer registries in 2019, this study calculated the incidence and mortality of each cancer site according to urban/rural area, sex and age group (0–4 years, 5–9 years,…, 80–84 years, 85 years and above), which can represent the nationwide age-specific incidence rates of different cancers within each age frame. The age-specific incidence rate of different cancers under urban and rural areas and sex stratification in 2024 was then estimated using the age-specific incidence rate multiplied by the corresponding APC fitted by 106 cancer registries. Based on the national population data of 2024, the incidence and mortality of different cancers by urban/rural area, sex, and age group were calculated and the data of individual cancers were then added up to obtain the national estimated results.

The Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.4.0) was used to calculated the APC and the average annual percent change (AAPC) for three fixed intervals (2000–2019, 2010–2019 and 2015–2019). [15](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0015) A maximum of three joinpoints were restricted to for all models. SAS software (Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The age-standardized rates for incidence and mortality were estimated with the Segi’s world standard population. The results of this study report summarize the estimated results of urban and rural areas, presented by cancer site and sex.

## 3\. Results

### 3.1. Estimated numbers of new cancer cases and cancer incidence rates

In 2024, approximately 5,150,572 new cancer cases were estimated to occur in China, with a crude incidence rate of 366.57 per 100,000 population, and the ASIR using the Segi’s population was 207.7 per 100,000 population. Lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer, with an estimation of about 1,175,915 cases, accounting for 22.83 % of all cancer cases in the year. This was followed by thyroid cancer (573,829 cases, 11.14 %), colorectal cancer (561,786 cases, 10.91 %), breast cancer (380,030 cases, 7.38 %) and liver cancer (354,143 cases, 6.88 %). Collectively, these top five cancers represented 59.13 % of all new cancer diagnoses ( [Table 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#tbl0001)).

By sex, lung cancer remained the leading cancer in men, with an estimated 703,775 cases (26.34 % of all male cancer cases), followed by colorectal cancer (337,908 cases, 12.65 %), liver cancer (259,390 cases, 9.71 %), stomach cancer (235,018 cases, 8.80 %), and prostate cancer (163,941 cases, 6.14 %), with these top five cancers accounting for 63.62 % of all male cases. In women, lung cancer was also the most common (472,140 cases, 19.05 %), but thyroid cancer followed closely (419,502 cases, 16.93 %), with breast cancer (375,808 cases, 15.16 %), colorectal cancer (223,878 cases, 9.03 %), and cervical cancer (144,911 cases, 5.85 %) ranking the third to fifth highest incidences, respectively. These top five cancers together accounted for 66.01 % of all female cancer cases ( [Table 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#tbl0001)).

[Table 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#tbl0002) shows the estimated total numbers of deaths for all cancers and cancer types stratified by sex. An estimated 2,582,164 cancer deaths occurred in China in 2024, with a crude mortality rate of 183.78 per 100,000 population, and an ASMR of 90.90 per 100,000 population. Lung cancer was still the leading cause of cancer death, with approximately 743,336 deaths (28.79 % of all deaths). The other most lethal cancers were liver cancer (303,783 deaths, 11.76 %), colorectal cancer (255,708 deaths, 9.90 %), stomach cancer (248,675 deaths, 9.63 %), and esophageal cancer (172,055 deaths, 6.66 %). ### 3.3. Age-specific incidence and mortality

Cancer incidence and mortality rates increased with age, with incidence peaking in the 80–84 years age group and mortality peaking in the 85+ years age group. The age groups 60–64 and 50–54 years accounted for the highest number of new cases in males and females, respectively, while the 60–64 and 75–79 years age groups had the most cancer deaths in both males and females. Before the age of 55 years, the incidence rate was higher in females compared to males. Beyond this age range, however, it was higher in males than in females. The mortality rate was higher in males than females in every age group ( [Fig. 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#fig0001)).

### 3.5. Trends in cancer incidence and mortality

From 2000 to 2019, the ASIR for all cancers combined in China increased at an AAPC of 1.6 %. This rise was driven by significant increases in specific cancers, such as thyroid cancer, which showed the fastest upward trend (AAPC = 15.9 %), prostate cancer (AAPC = 6.9 %), cervical cancer (AAPC = 6.9 %), female breast cancer (AAPC = 3.2 %), and colorectal cancer (AAPC = 2.2%). Conversely, esophageal (AAPC = −4.5 %), stomach (AAPC = −2.7 %), and liver (AAPC = −2.3 %) cancers showed decreasing trends. The incidence rate of lung cancer saw a slight rise in males (0.8%), but notably increased by 3.8 % annually in women ( [Table 5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#tbl0005), [Fig. 3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#fig0003)).

The ASMR for all cancers combined decreased at an AAPC of −1.4 % from 2000 to 2019. ## 4\. Discussion

Cancer remains a major public health issue globally, with its burden reflecting the complex interplay of demographic transitions, changing lifestyles, [6](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0006) and the effectiveness of prevention and control measures. The 2024 estimates of cancer incidence and mortality in China provide crucial insights into the current state and evolving trends of cancer in the country, highlighting both progress and persistent challenges. These data were essential for informing policy decisions, guiding resource allocation, and shaping future strategies under initiatives such as “Healthy China 2030”.

Due to the distribution characteristics of age-specific incidence rate and mortality of cancer between men and women, the overall age-specific morbidity and mortality of cancer between men and women are also significantly different. Before the age of 55, the age-specific incidence rate of cancer in men is lower than that in women, but it increased significantly since then, while the age-specific mortality of men is almost higher than that in women in all age groups. There was a strong positive correlation between age and the incidence and mortality rates of different types of cancer. In general, the incidence rate increased with age, but the incidence rate of hormone-related cancers in women, such as breast cancer, thyroid cancer, ovarian cancer, cervical cancer, etc., increased significantly in young and middle-aged women, but decreased in population over 60 years old. These age-specific incidence patterns highlighted the disproportionate burden of specific cancers in different age groups, and the urgent need for age- and sex-specific prevention, early detection and treatment strategies.

In 2024, China was projected to have approximately 5.15 million new cancer cases and 2.58 million cancer deaths. The rising overall incidence (AAPC = 1.6 %), driven notably by cancers such as thyroid, colorectal, breast and prostate cancers, underscored a shifting disease profile influenced by factors including lifestyle changes, diagnostic intensity, and an ageing population. Conversely, the concurrent decline in overall mortality (AAPC = −1.4 %), largely attributable to falling death rates from historically common cancers like esophageal, stomach and liver cancers, may reflect the success of primary prevention and improved clinical management. However, the persistent high incidence and mortality from lung cancer, alongside rising mortality for colorectal and pancreatic cancers, highlighted critical gaps. These data highlight the urgent need for a balanced strategy that not only maintains effective tobacco and infection control but also intensifies efforts toward early detection programs for emerging cancers and ensuring access to advanced treatments for malignancies with poor prognoses. The trends for most common cancers in China is significantly different from the trends in developed countries in regions such as in Europe and America, [16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0016), [17](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0017), [18](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0018) indicating a necessity for tailored prevention and control policies and measures that account for the distinct characteristics of each cancer type.

The cancer incidence and mortality trends from 2000 to 2019 offer essential insights. The overall ASIR increase was driven by rising rates of thyroid, prostate, cervical, breast, and colorectal cancers, which align with patterns seen in developed countries and are largely attributed to Westernized lifestyles (e.g., dietary changes, physical inactivity, and obesity), enhanced diagnostic intensity, and overdiagnosis, particularly for thyroid cancer. Conversely, significant declines in esophageal, stomach, and liver cancers reflect successful primary prevention efforts, including reduced aflatoxin exposure, improved water quality, hepatitis B vaccination, [19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0019) and screening initiatives in high-risk areas. [20](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0020) The modest rise of lung cancer incidence in men but significant rise in women indicate the continuous impact of tobacco use—with China boasting over 350 million smokers—alongside the emerging threats posed by air pollution and indoor cooking fumes for non-smoking females.

Mortality showed a declining trend (ASMR AAPC = −1.4 %), largely due to reductions in upper gastrointestinal cancers (e.g., esophageal, stomach, and liver cancers) and lung cancer. This trend may underscore the impact of early detection programs (e.g., endoscopy for upper gastrointestinal cancers) and improved treatment accessibility. However, rising mortalities for pancreatic, kidney cancers and lymphoma indicate gaps in screening and clinical management for these malignancies. Previous studies have also revealed a contrast in the cancer burden between urban and rural areas—with urban areas experiencing higher incidence and rural areas facing higher mortality— a disparity that highlights inequities in healthcare accessibility and awareness. [21](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0021), [22](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0022), [23](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0023) The observed non-linear trends in cancers such as bladder cancer incidence and leukemia mortality may likely reflect the complex interplay of shifting risk factors, advancements in medical practice, and public health interventions over the past two decades in China, particularly the introduction of targeted therapies and improved supportive care, which may have markedly improved the survival outcomes for several leukemia subtypes. These fluctuating trends underscore the necessity for continuous monitoring to evaluate the effectiveness of cancer control and treatment measures.

Compared with other countries worldwide, [1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0001), [24](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0024) the cancer patterns in China exhibited a dual burden: declining infection-related cancers (e.g., liver and stomach cancers) mirrored trends in developed countries, while rising obesity- and lifestyle-related cancers (e.g., colorectal and breast cancers) aligned with patterns in transitioning economies. The ASIR in China (207.7 per 100,000) was lower than that of the United States (approximately 300 per 100,000) but slightly higher than the global average level. The decline in mortality for upper gastrointestinal cancers was greater in China than in many other countries, reflecting the success of targeted screening programs and vaccination initiatives. However, in China, the disparities in healthcare accessibility and outcomes between urban and rural areas was more significant compared to high-income countries.

China’s efforts to reduce cancer burden have seen significant successes, particularly through programs like endoscopic screening for esophageal and stomach cancers in high-risk areas. Vaccination against HBV has dramatically reduced liver cancer incidence, [19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0019), [25](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0025) and the recent introduction of HPV vaccination is expected to control the incidence of cervical cancer. [26](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0026) However, challenges persist in constraining the risk factors of other cancers. For instance, tobacco is one of the very important risk factors for cancer, [27](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0027), [28](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667005425001164#bib0028) but tobacco control remains inadequate, with only partial implementation of smoke-free laws in cities like Beijing and Shanghai. The increasing burden of obesity-related cancers calls for public health campaigns promoting healthy lifestyles. Future strategies should focus on addressing regional disparities, enhancing early detection capabilities, and strengthening primary prevention efforts tailored to local needs.

The successes of initiatives such as “Healthy China 2030″ and cancer screening programs are evident in reducing mortality, but sustained efforts are needed. Priority areas include strengthening tobacco control, expanding HPV and HBV vaccination, promoting healthy lifestyles, and ensuring equitable access to screening and treatment—especially in rural areas. Future research should concentrate on monitoring emerging risks, evaluating interventions, and addressing regional disparities to achieve the goals of reduced cancer burden by 2030.

The advantage of this study is that, firstly, it is currently the most representative data in China. The estimates were derived from data collected by 919 population-based cancer registries across China, with 106 registries providing continuous data from 2010 to 2019. These qualified registries cover approximately 44.89 % of the national population, and their extensive data coverage coupled with rigorous quality control measures ensure that the estimations for 2024 are highly representative of China’s cancer burden. Secondly, this research presents in detail the characteristics of incidence and mortality rates of different cancer type by sex and age group, as well as the temporal trends of major cancers, providing reference for understanding the effectiveness of cancer prevention and control in China. However, limitations include underrepresentation in western regions where data completeness and representativeness were lower, and potential delays in data reporting.

## 5\. Conclusions

The results of the 2024 cancer burden estimation and trend analysis in China illuminate both the strides achieved and the ongoing challenges encountered in cancer prevention and control. Although the mortality rate declines in recent years, the future cancer incidence may continue to rise in China, driven by the aging population and changes in risk factors. The coexistence of cancer pattens of developed and developing countries in China also prompts the country to address regional differences and evolving risks in accordance with real-world conditions. Continuous efforts in tobacco control, vaccination, screening, and health promotion are crucial for further reducing the burden of cancer. Policymakers must prioritize healthcare equity, allocate resources towards data collection and research endeavors, strengthen interdepartmental collaboration to achieve the goals of “Healthy China 2030” and secure a healthier tomorrow for all. To manage the growing cancer burden and enhance survival rates in China, it is essential to sustain current strategies like targeted prevention, early detection, and treatment plans, while also increasing the investment in clinical treatment and fundamental research.
