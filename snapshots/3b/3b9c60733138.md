# China-Europe rail freight: transit times, costs and when to choose rail

China-Europe rail sits exactly between the two classic modes: around 25 days door to door against 50 to 60 by sea, at a cost far below air freight. It becomes relevant when ocean is too slow for your replenishment cycle but air is too expensive, on regular and plannable flows. Its limits are capacity, the geopolitical sensitivity of the corridors, and the goods that cannot travel by rail.

Updated on October 16, 2026

Between 50 days by ship and 6 days by plane, there is a third option most importers never seriously evaluate: the train. China-Europe rail freight occupies a precise niche, and for certain flow profiles it is the best trade-off available.

## Where rail sits, at a glance

|  | Ocean | **Rail** | Air |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Door-to-door transit | 50 to 60 days | **~25 days** | 5 to 8 days |
| Relative cost | lowest | **intermediate** | highest |
| Frequency | weekly | **weekly** | daily |
| Suitable volume | any volume | **full container or groupage** | small dense volumes |

Rail replaces neither sea nor air. It fills the gap between them, and that gap is exactly where many supply chains hurt: too slow by sea for a replenishment, too expensive by air for routine volume.

## When rail is the right choice

Four situations where it makes sense:

- **Regular, plannable replenishment.** A four to six week ordering cycle is well served by a weekly 25-day departure, where ocean forces you to order two months ahead.
- **Medium to high value products.** Saving 25 to 35 days of tied-up inventory offsets a good share of the premium over sea.
- **Reducing ocean exposure.** Opening a second mode on the same corridor is a simple hedge against port congestion or a diversion.
- **Carbon constraints.** The gain against an air shipment is massive, and it is often the fastest lever to cut the emissions of an urgent flow.

Conversely, rail is hard to justify on low-value goods with no deadline pressure, on very irregular volumes, or when the cargo falls into a restricted category.

## The limits to know before committing

1. **Capacity.** It is far narrower than ocean. In periods of strong demand, space books out in advance and rates rise quickly.
2. **Geopolitical sensitivity.** Land corridors cross several countries; a political decision or a border closure can change the routing, the transit time and the cost. That is the main risk to factor in.
3. **Restricted goods.** Much of dangerous goods, some lithium batteries, certain food products and frost-sensitive items do not travel, or not everywhere.
4. **Arrival.** A train that reaches Europe quickly but whose devanning and clearance take a week loses part of its advantage. That is the point to lock down.

## What to lock down at the arrival end

The transit time advertised on a rail service is often terminal to terminal. The question that matters is the European leg: where is the container stripped, who clears it, and how long before the goods are on your dock?

That is the segment on which OVRSEA runs its Zhengzhou-Liège service: one departure a week, devanning on dock at Saint-Quentin-Fallavier with customs clearance integrated on site, then final distribution in 24 to 48 hours. Upstream, OVRSEA has 6 offices in China (Shanghai, Ningbo, Shenzhen, Tianjin and Hong Kong), with 70+ people on the ground consolidating goods from multiple suppliers before departure. Because clearance happens where the container is stripped, arrival does not give back the days rail has saved.

## How to test rail without switching everything

The right approach is incremental: take one regularly rotating SKU, compare real total cost over three shipments - freight, tied-up inventory, time to availability - against your usual ocean service, and see whether saving three to five weeks changes anything about your stock level. If it does, rail deserves a permanent share of your mix. If not, stay on the water without regret.

## FAQ

How long does a China-Europe train take?

Count roughly 25 days door to door depending on the Chinese origin and the European destination, against 50 to 60 days by sea between the same points. Rail is not as fast as air (5 to 8 days) but it halves the ocean transit, with weekly regularity on established services.

Is China-Europe rail more expensive than ocean?

Yes, noticeably, but it stays well below air freight. The right calculation is not price per kilo but total cost: on medium or high value products, the 25 to 35 days of inventory saved against ocean offset part of the price gap. On low-value goods with no deadline pressure, ocean remains unbeatable.

Which goods cannot travel by China-Europe rail?

Restrictions cover much of dangerous goods, lithium batteries depending on their configuration, certain food products, and frost-sensitive cargo, since temperatures can be extreme on part of the route. Have feasibility validated product by product before planning a flow.

Is rail greener than ocean?

Rail emits far less CO2 than air, with no real comparison. Against ocean the advantage is less clear-cut: sea transport remains very efficient per tonne-kilometre, but the rail route is shorter. Ask for a per-shipment calculation using the GLEC methodology rather than relying on a general rule.

Is the China-Europe rail corridor reliable today?

Established services run with regular weekly departures, but rail remains more exposed than sea to geopolitical events and to changes in transit policy depending on the corridors used. Treat it as a complement within a multimodal strategy, not as your single supply route.
