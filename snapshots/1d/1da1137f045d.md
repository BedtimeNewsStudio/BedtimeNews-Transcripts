# India moves to localise tunnel-boring gear with ₹1.2 billion push

India is preparing a ₹1.2 billion incentive scheme to build domestic capacity for tunnel boring machines, lifts and fire-fighting gear. The policy is designed to cut reliance on Chinese imports, attract fresh investment and support a wider manufacturing base for the infrastructure sector.

![](https://etimg.etb2bimg.com/photo/133453503.cms?mod=174486) India’s [infrastructure](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/infrastructure) boom is creating an unusual policy priority: who makes the machines that dig the tunnels, lift the towers and protect the buildings that define the next wave of urban growth. The answer, for now, still points overseas. But that may change soon, with the Centre preparing a large incentive package to build those capabilities at home. First reported by [Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/why-india-is-betting-1-2-billion-on-homegrown-tunnel-boring-machines-and-other-construction-equipment/articleshow/133397270.cms).

According to [Reuters](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/reuters), the government is close to clearing a ₹1.2 billion incentive scheme for [manufacturing](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/manufacturing) high-value construction and infrastructure equipment in India. The plan is intended to draw about ₹1.8 billion in fresh investment over seven years, while reducing the country’s dependence on imported machinery, especially from [China](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/china), for critical projects.

**A policy bet tied to metro lines, highways and high-rises**

The proposed support package is expected to cover [tunnel boring machines](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/tunnel+boring+machines), fire-fighting systems and elevators used in tall buildings, Reuters reported, citing two government sources. The logic is straightforward: India’s road, metro, airport and urban construction pipeline is expanding fast, but the domestic supply base for many specialised machines remains shallow.

That gap matters most in tunnelling. Tunnel boring machines are among the most complex pieces of civil engineering equipment in use, and India has relied heavily on imports for them. In practice, that has left key projects exposed to supply disruptions, foreign export controls and procurement bottlenecks.

The scheme is expected to include local value-addition thresholds for products that are currently fully imported, Reuters reported. In effect, the government is trying to do more than simply assemble imported parts in India. It wants a deeper manufacturing ecosystem, with domestic sourcing, engineering and fabrication built into the incentive structure.

**Why tunnel boring machines are at the centre of the plan**

China has been one of the major suppliers of tunnelling and boring equipment used in metro rail and highway work in India. That dependence became more visible after the 2020 border clashes between Indian and Chinese troops, when New Delhi tightened rules on Chinese investment and public procurement.

The supply picture worsened in 2024, when China delayed customs clearances for tunnel boring machine shipments to India, according to the Reuters report. Data cited by the agency shows that imports of tunnelling machinery from China fell from $18 million in 2022-23 to $3 million in 2023-24, before dropping further to $500,000 in 2024-25. The figure then recovered to $800,000 in 2025-26.

That trajectory has turned a niche equipment category into a strategic one. For India, the issue is not only cost, but also control over timelines in projects where delays can ripple through financing, contractor schedules and public infrastructure delivery.

**Who stands to gain from domestic manufacturing**

The proposed framework could open opportunities for a range of Indian manufacturers, with state-run [BEML](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/beml) seen as a possible beneficiary because it has plans to make tunnel boring machines domestically, Reuters reported. Other companies that could gain from a larger local market include [Larsen & Toubro](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/larsen+%26+toubro) and [Johnson Lifts](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/johnson+lifts).

The wider industrial message is equally important. If the scheme works as intended, it could help Indian suppliers move up the technology ladder in a segment that has long been dominated by foreign makers. It may also encourage ancillary industries, from precision engineering to heavy fabrication, to invest in capabilities that have been uneconomic without policy support.

For equipment makers, the real prize is not just a subsidy cheque. It is the chance to enter a market that is likely to grow steadily as India keeps spending on urban transport, highways, airports, industrial corridors and building safety systems.

**Budget promise now moving towards execution**

The incentive plan was first flagged in the 2026-27 [Union Budget](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/union+budget) as part of a broader effort to strengthen domestic production of advanced construction and infrastructure equipment. Finance minister Nirmala Sitharaman said at the time that the category could include “lifts in a multi-story apartment, fire-fighting equipment, large and small, to tunnel-boring equipment for building metros and high-altitude roads.”

That framing matters because it shows the policy is not limited to one machine or one sector. It is aimed at a wider manufacturing base for the built environment, from residential towers to transport corridors. In a country where infrastructure spending has become a central growth lever, the ability to supply these machines locally can have a direct bearing on project economics.

An ET report last month said inter-ministerial discussions on the scheme had been completed and approvals were expected by the end of August. Reuters has now reported that the [Union Cabinet](https://construction.economictimes.indiatimes.com/tag/union+cabinet) is likely to take up the proposal soon, suggesting the process is nearing the final stage.

**A larger industrial shift is under way**

The proposed scheme arrives at a time when India’s construction and infrastructure equipment market is estimated at about ₹1 trillion, or roughly $10.5 billion, and is expected to expand further as public and private capital flows into transport and urban projects. A Boston Consulting Group-CII report cited in the Reuters story said India’s share of the global mining and construction equipment industry has risen from about 2.5 per cent to 4 per cent and could reach around 6.5 per cent over the next five years.

That growth projection is significant because it suggests India is no longer just a large buyer of equipment. It is becoming a more meaningful manufacturing and demand centre in a global industry that is being reshaped by infrastructure-led growth, supply-chain diversification and localisation policies.

Still, the challenge will be execution. Building tunnel boring machines and other advanced equipment at scale requires not only capital, but also design expertise, supplier depth, quality control and long production cycles. Incentives can help bridge the economics, but they cannot substitute for engineering capability.

If the Cabinet approves the plan, the next test will be whether the policy can convert import dependence into domestic capacity without raising costs or slowing project delivery. For an infrastructure market that is set to keep expanding, that outcome could prove more valuable than the subsidy itself.
