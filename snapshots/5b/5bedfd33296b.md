Carbonaceous sulfur hydride

Chemical compound

**Carbonaceous sulfur hydride** ( **CSH**) is a potential [superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room-temperature_superconductor "Room-temperature superconductor") that was announced in October 2020 by the lab of [Ranga Dias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranga_P._Dias "Ranga P. Dias") at the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester "University of Rochester"), in a _[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")_ paper that was later retracted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) It was reported to have a [superconducting transition temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_transition_temperature "Superconducting transition temperature") of 15°C (59°F) at a pressure of 267 [GPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_(unit) "Pascal (unit)") (38.7millionpsi), which would have made it the highest-temperature superconductor discovered.[&#8202;](#ref-back-almost-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-almost) The paper faced criticism due to its non-standard data analysis calling into question its conclusions,[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-:0)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-:1)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:2-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-:2)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:3-0)[\[6\]](#refnote-:3)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:4-0)[\[7\]](#refnote-:4) and in September 2022 it was [retracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retraction_in_academic_publishing "Retraction in academic publishing") by [_Nature_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)").[&#8202;](#ref-back-:5-0)[\[8\]](#refnote-:5) In July 2023 a second paper by the authors was retracted from _[Physical Review Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review_Letters "Physical Review Letters")_ due to suspected data fabrication, and in September 2023 a third paper by the authors about N-doped lutetium hydride was retracted from _Nature_.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:6-0)[\[9\]](#refnote-:6)

CSH is an uncharacterized ternary [polyhydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhydride "Polyhydride") compound of [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon"), [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") and [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") with a chemical formula that is thought to be CH8S. Measurements under extreme pressure are difficult, and in particular the elements are too light for an X-ray determination of crystal structure ( [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography")).[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider)

## Background

Main article: [Room-temperature superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room-temperature_superconductor "Room-temperature superconductor")

Prior to 1911, all known [electrical conductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductor "Electrical conductor") exhibited [electrical resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance "Electrical resistance"), due to collisions of the [charge carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_carrier "Charge carrier") with atoms in the material. Researchers discovered that in certain materials at low temperatures, the charge carriers interact with [phonons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonon "Phonon") in the material and form [Cooper pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_pair "Cooper pair"), as described by [BCS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCS_theory "BCS theory"). This process results in the formation of a [superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductor "Superconductor"), with zero electrical resistance. During the transition to the superconducting state, the [magnetic field lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_line "Magnetic field line") are expelled from the interior of the material, which allows for the possibility of [magnetic levitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_levitation "Magnetic levitation"). The effect has historically been known to occur at only low temperatures, but researchers have spent decades attempting to find a material that could operate at room temperature.[&#8202;](#ref-back-quanta-0)[\[10\]](#refnote-quanta)

## Synthesis

The material is a ternary [polyhydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhydride "Polyhydride") compound of [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon"), [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") and [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") with a chemical formula that is thought to be CH8S. As of October 2020, the material's molecular structure remains uncharacterized, as extreme pressures and the light elements used are unsuitable for most measurements, such as X-ray determination.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-2)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider)

The material was reportedly synthesized by compressing [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane") (CH4), [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide "Hydrogen sulfide") (H2S) and hydrogen (H2) in a [diamond anvil cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_anvil_cell "Diamond anvil cell") and illuminating with a 532nm green laser.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-3)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) A starting compound of [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon") and [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur "Sulfur") is synthesized with a 1:1 [molar ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_ratio "Molar ratio"), formed into balls less than five microns in diameter, and placed into a [diamond anvil cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_anvil_cell "Diamond anvil cell"). Hydrogen gas is then added and the system is compressed to 4.0GPa (0.58millionpsi) and illuminated with a 532nm laser for several hours. It was reported that the crystal is not stable under 10GPa (1.5millionpsi) and can be destroyed if left at room temperature overnight.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-4)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) Other researchers were skeptical that such materials could serve as room temperature superconductors, as the absence of [van Hove singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Hove_singularity "Van Hove singularity") or similar peaks in the electronic density of states of more than 3000 candidate phases rules out conventional superconductivity.[&#8202;](#ref-back-11-0)[\[11\]](#refnote-11)

## Claims of superconductivity

Superconductivity for sulfur hydrides without carbon was first reported in 2015.[&#8202;](#ref-back-12-0)[\[12\]](#refnote-12)

On 14 October 2020, a paper by Elliot Snider, et al. from the Dias lab was published, claiming that carbonaceous sulfur hydride was a [room-temperature superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room-temperature_superconductor "Room-temperature superconductor").[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-5)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) Two years later, the paper was retracted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-13-0)[\[13\]](#refnote-13)[&#8202;](#ref-back-14-0)[\[14\]](#refnote-14) The claims in the paper included a superconducting state at temperatures as high as 15°C (59°F),[&#8202;](#ref-back-15-0)[\[15\]](#refnote-15)[&#8202;](#ref-back-bbc-0)[\[16\]](#refnote-bbc) almost 30°C (86°F) higher than the existing record holder for [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity").[&#8202;](#ref-back-almost-1)[\[2\]](#refnote-almost)[&#8202;](#ref-back-Science-0)[\[17\]](#refnote-Science) This state was claimed to be observable only at the very high pressure of 267 [GPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_(unit) "Pascal (unit)") (38.7millionpsi), a million times the pressure in a typical car tire.[&#8202;](#ref-back-bbc-1)[\[16\]](#refnote-bbc) The report was published in _Nature_ and received significant media coverage.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Science-1)[\[17\]](#refnote-Science)[&#8202;](#ref-back-18-0)[\[18\]](#refnote-18)[&#8202;](#ref-back-19-0)[\[19\]](#refnote-19)[&#8202;](#ref-back-20-0)[\[20\]](#refnote-20)[&#8202;](#ref-back-21-0)[\[21\]](#refnote-21)[&#8202;](#ref-back-quanta-1)[\[10\]](#refnote-quanta)[&#8202;](#ref-back-22-0)[\[22\]](#refnote-22)

### Criticism and retraction

The validity of these results was called into question[&#8202;](#ref-back-:0-1)[\[3\]](#refnote-:0) by [Jorge E. Hirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_E._Hirsch "Jorge E. Hirsch")[&#8202;](#ref-back-:1-1)[\[4\]](#refnote-:1) as well as others.[&#8202;](#ref-back-:4-1)[\[7\]](#refnote-:4)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:2-1)[\[5\]](#refnote-:2) Unavailability of the data prompted an editor's note on the original paper.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-6)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) additional criticism focused on the measurements of [AC susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_susceptibility#In_the_frequency_domain "Magnetic susceptibility")[&#8202;](#ref-back-:3-1)[\[6\]](#refnote-:3)[&#8202;](#ref-back-23-0)[\[23\]](#refnote-23)
used to test the superconductivity as the more definitive [Meissner effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meissner_effect "Meissner effect") was too hard to observe at the scale of the experiments.

As of 2022, no other lab had been able to reproduce the result, and the criticisms of the data analysis in the paper had not been addressed. On February 15, 2022, Nature added a cautionary Editor's Note to the article,[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-7)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider) and on 26 September 26, 2022, retracted the article entirely.[&#8202;](#ref-back-Snider-8)[\[1\]](#refnote-Snider)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:5-1)[\[8\]](#refnote-:5) By the end of 2023 two other papers from the lab had been retracted from _Physical Review Letters_ and _Nature_, due to suspicions of data fabrication.[&#8202;](#ref-back-24-0)[\[24\]](#refnote-24)[&#8202;](#ref-back-:6-1)[\[9\]](#refnote-:6) At this point other publications by the lab were scrutinized more closely and as of March 2024 a total of nine of their papers had been retracted.[&#8202;](#ref-back-25-0)[\[25\]](#refnote-25)
