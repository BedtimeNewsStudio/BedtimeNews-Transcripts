# What Are Lego’s New Building Blocks? Brick-And-Mortar Stores, Primarily In China

Lego built its global toy empire on plastic bricks. Now it is betting on bricks and mortar.

The Danish toy giant is adding physical stores to drive growth and investing heavily in locations in China and other emerging markets.

Toy experts say the building blocks CEO Niels Christiansen is putting in place stack up to a smart strategy, but like a just-opened Lego box containing the new 4,784-piece Star Wars Imperial Destroyer, it will take some time before the end result is visible.

Lego, in reporting its [results](https://www.lego.com/en-us/aboutus/news-room/2019/september/interim-results-2019/) for the first half of the year, said last week that while global consumer sales were up 5% and revenue was up 2%, adjusting for currency fluctuations, net profit was down 12% due to “significant investments in long-term growth.”

Those investments include opening 160 stores worldwide this year. Half of those new stores - 80 - will be in China, bringing its total for that country to more than 140 stores.

With this strategy, Lego is thinking both vertically—by investing in stores where it controls its distribution—and globally—by shifting focus from low-growth North America and Western Europe to markets like China and India.

Lego will have nearly 600 stores by the end of this year, a move that give it more power to showcase its products and create customer experiences, and that also protects it from blows like the Toys ‘R’ Us bankruptcy and liquidation and other retail failures.

Premium brands such as Lego increasingly are moving to open their own stores in order to control the relationship with the customer, and to keep the profit from the sales, said Gerald Storch, CEO of consulting firm Storch Advisors and former executive at major retail companies, including Toys ‘R’ Us, where he was chairman and CEO from 2006 to 2013.

“Lego has had stores for a number of years, but they appear to be reinforcing their commitment to this direction,” he said.

Having branded Lego stores in China is a smart move, Storch said, for multiple reasons.

“In toys the brands need to go where the growth is, and the most rapid growth clearly is in Asia,” he said.

In China, buying from a Lego store eases fears of counterfeit products. “One of the things Chinese customers love when they buy a product at a Lego store is they know it’s not fake. Because the market in China is full of counterfeit goods,” Storch said.

But the store-building strategy does come with some risks, he added.

“With verticalization, they also then incur the entire cost structure for the distribution, including all the rents on these stores,” he said. “As long as a brand is hot and people want it, then that’s a good bet, but should they stumble then the rent becomes an anchor and a huge problem.”

He thinks Lego is well-positioned now to take that risk, despite some stumbles in the past. “It’s not just bricks anymore. It’s themed product, both their own as well as licenses. So they seem to have learned their lesson and are in a very strong place in the global stage,” he said.

Richard Gottlieb, CEO of Global Toy Experts and publisher of Global Toy News, says Lego is putting its bricks-and-mortar dollars in the right place with the push for more stores in China.

“China is the one place in the world where I think we’re going to see a lot of consumer growth because of the huge child population,” Gottlieb said.

Chinese consumers have demonstrated they love the Lego brand, and like to buy directly from Lego, Gottlieb said. He participated in a toy industry panel in China where Chinese panelists said Chinese consumers would be embarrassed to own toy bricks that were not Lego bricks. “It showed the love and respect for brands the Chinese have and the good job Lego has done,” he said.

Lego, more than many other brands, has a lot of reasons to love brick and mortar, Jim Silver, a veteran toy industry journalist and CEO of toy review website TTPM, said.

The stores, he said, are places where Lego can showcase the brand and display a broader assortment of its products than Walmart or Target will carry.

“So many of the Lego consumers are collectors. They’re looking for pieces that might not sell in the volume mass retailers are looking for,” Silver said. “If you go into the big mass retailers, they’re only carrying a fraction of the Lego line.

Lego has already seen their stores succeed in China and elsewhere in Asia, and all of the large toy manufacturers are trying to increase their sales there, as the importance of the U.S. market is shrinking.

“If you go back 10 years ago, 70-75% of the business was the United States. Now as the global market has expanded, for the big companies the United State is 50% or less of their total business,” Silver said.

Lego is also investing in virtual building blocks like its Hidden Side augmented reality line that lets kids use smartphones to see and catch ghosts inhabiting play sets they have constructed out of Lego bricks.

“It’s basically Pokemon Go meets Lego,” said Silver. The augmented reality play is updated monthly, in a smart use of technology, he said. “They’ve taken a building set and added a lot of play to it.”

Lego, which recently celebrated the 60th anniversary of its iconic plastic bricks, has a proven track record of brand building.

But just like when you build the 6,000-plus piece Lego Hogwarts Castle, Christiansen and his team will need to make sure they get the foundation right, be patient, and keep connecting the pieces even if the end result isn’t immediately visible.
