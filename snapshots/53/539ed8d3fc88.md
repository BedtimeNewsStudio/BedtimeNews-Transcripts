[Jump to content](#bodyContent)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Major contributors to [space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris "Space debris") include the explosion of upper stages and satellite collisions.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa2009-0)[\[1\]](#refnote-nasa2009)

## Overview

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=List_of_space_debris_producing_events&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")\]

There were 190 known satellite breakups between 1961 and 2006.[&#8202;](#ref-back-iaf2010-0)[\[2\]](#refnote-iaf2010)
By 2015, the total had grown to 250 on-orbit fragmentation events.[&#8202;](#ref-back-esa20150304-0)[\[3\]](#refnote-esa20150304)

As of 2012[\[update\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=List_of_space_debris_producing_events&action=edit) there were an estimated 500,000 pieces of debris in orbit,[&#8202;](#ref-back-strat-0)[\[4\]](#refnote-strat) with 300,000 pieces below 2000km ( [LEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit")).[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa2009-1)[\[1\]](#refnote-nasa2009) Of the total, about 20,000 are tracked.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa2009-2)[\[1\]](#refnote-nasa2009) Also, about sixteen old Soviet nuclear space reactors are known to have released an estimated 100,000 [NaK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium%E2%80%93potassium_alloy#Coolant "Sodium–potassium alloy") liquid metal coolant droplets 800–900km up,[&#8202;](#ref-back-ieee2009-0)[\[5\]](#refnote-ieee2009) which range in size from 1–6cm.[&#8202;](#ref-back-ieee2009-1)[\[5\]](#refnote-ieee2009)

The greatest risk to space missions is from untracked debris between 1 and 10cm in size.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa2009-3)[\[1\]](#refnote-nasa2009) Large pieces can be tracked and avoided, and impact from smaller pieces are usually survivable.[&#8202;](#ref-back-nasa2009-4)[\[1\]](#refnote-nasa2009)
