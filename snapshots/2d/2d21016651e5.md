**Ulsan**, metropolitan city, southeastern [South Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Korea). Ulsan has the status of a metropolitan city under the direct control of the central government, with administrative status equal to that of a province. At the eastern end of the [Taebaek Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taebaek-Mountains), facing the East Sea ( [Sea of Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sea-of-Japan)), on Ulsan Bay, it lies about 45 miles (72 km) north-northeast of [Busan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Busan). It is the heart of the country’s special industrial area known as the Ulsan Industrial District.

Until 1962, when the city was connected by rail and [highway](https://www.britannica.com/technology/highway) with [Seoul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Seoul), Busan, [Daegu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Daegu), and [Daejeon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Daejeon), it was primarily a fishing port and a market center for agricultural products (especially [pears](https://www.britannica.com/plant/pear)) from the Ulsan plain and the delta of the Taehwa River. By the end of the first five-year economic plan (1966), the city had become an open port with major manufacturing plants, and by the late 20th century it was one of the country’s most significant industrial hubs. A [free-trade zone](https://www.britannica.com/money/free-trade-zone) was established in the early 21st century. Among the city’s major industries are automobile manufacturing, [petrochemicals](https://www.britannica.com/science/petrochemical), and shipbuilding. An airport located on the outskirts of Ulsan provides domestic service. Munsu Stadium was the venue for some of the 2002 [football (soccer)](https://www.britannica.com/sports/football-soccer) [World Cup](https://www.britannica.com/sports/World-Cup-football) championship matches. Pop. (2020) 1,135,423.

### Where is South Korea, and what is its capital?

South Korea is a country in [East Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Asia) that occupies the southern part of the Korean peninsula. It is bordered by [North Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Korea) to the north, the [Yellow Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yellow-Sea) to the west, and the [East Sea (Sea of Japan)](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sea-of-Japan-East-Sea) to the east. Its capital and largest city is [Seoul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Seoul), a major political, economic, and cultural center located in the northwestern part of the country.

### Why are North Korea and South Korea divided?

North and South Korea are divided largely because of [Japanese colonial rule](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Korea-Under-Japanese-Rule), [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II), and the [Cold War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Cold-War). Japan ruled Korea from 1910 until its surrender in 1945 at the end of World War II; afterward, the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) and the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) divided the peninsula along the 38th parallel in an arrangement that was intended to be temporary. As Cold War tensions intensified, however, separate governments were established in 1948. The [Korean War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Korean-War) (1950–53) hardened the division, ending with an armistice that established the [demilitarized zone](https://www.britannica.com/place/demilitarized-zone-Korean-peninsula) (DMZ) between the two countries.

### What type of government does South Korea have?

South Korea is a democratic [republic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/republic-government) with a presidential system of government. Under the country’s 1987 constitution, sovereignty resides in the people, and state authority is divided among executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The president serves as head of state and leads the executive branch, while legislative power is vested in the unicameral National Assembly.

### What language is spoken in South Korea?

The official language of South Korea is [Korean](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Korean-language), which is spoken by nearly the entire population. Korean is primarily written using [Hangul](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hangul-Korean-alphabet), an alphabet developed in the 15th century during the reign of King [Sejong](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sejong-Korean-ruler). Although Korean is also spoken in [North Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Korea), differences in vocabulary, pronunciation, and usage have developed between the two countries.

### How did South Korea become a major economy?

After the [Korean War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Korean-War) (1950–53), South Korea transformed from a largely agrarian economy into a major industrial and technological power. Beginning under Pres. [Park Chung-Hee](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Park-Chung-Hee) in the 1960s, the government pursued export-oriented industrialization and invested heavily in infrastructure and education. Large family-controlled conglomerates known as [_chaebol_ s](https://www.britannica.com/money/chaebol), including [Samsung](https://www.britannica.com/money/Samsung-Electronics) and [Hyundai](https://www.britannica.com/money/Hyundai-Group), became major engines of manufacturing and economic growth. South Korea’s rapid economic development became known as the “miracle on the Han River.”

**South Korea**, [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state) in East [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia). It occupies the southern portion of the Korean peninsula. The country is bordered by the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea ( [North Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Korea)) to the north, the East Sea ( [Sea of Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sea-of-Japan-East-Sea)) to the east, the [East China Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-China-Sea) to the south, and the [Yellow Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yellow-Sea) to the west; to the southeast it is separated from the Japanese island of [Tsushima](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tsushima) by the [Korea Strait](https://www.britannica.com/place/Korea-Strait). South Korea makes up about 45 percent of the peninsula’s land area. The capital is [Seoul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Seoul).

Geologically, South Korea consists in large part of Precambrian rocks (i.e., more than about 540 million years old) such as granite and gneiss. The country is largely mountainous, with small valleys and narrow coastal plains. The [Taebaek Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taebaek-Mountains) run in roughly a north-south direction along the eastern coastline and northward into North Korea, forming the country’s drainage divide. From them several mountain ranges branch off with a northeast-southwest orientation. The most important of these are the [Sobaek Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sobaek-Mountains), which undulate in a long S-shape across the peninsula. None of South Korea’s mountains are very high: the Taebaek Mountains reach an elevation of 5,604 feet (1,708 meters) at [Mount Seorak](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Seorak) in the northeast, and the Sobaek Mountains reach 6,283 feet (1,915 meters) at [Mount Jiri](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Chiri). The highest peak in South Korea, the extinct [volcano](https://www.britannica.com/science/volcano) [Mount Halla](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Halla) on [Jeju Island](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jeju-Island), is 6,398 feet (1,950 meters) above [sea level](https://www.britannica.com/science/sea-level).

South Korea has two volcanic islands—Jeju, off the peninsula’s southern tip, and [Ulleung](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ulleung-Island), about 85 miles (140 km) east of the mainland in the East Sea—and a small-scale lava [plateau](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/plateau) in [Gangwon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gangwon-Province-South-Korea) province. In addition, South Korea claims and occupies a group of rocky islets—known variously as Liancourt Rocks, Dok Islands (Korean), and Take Islands (Japanese)—some 55 miles (85 km) southeast of Ulleung Island; these islets also have been claimed by [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan).

South Korea’s three principal rivers, the Han, Geum, and Nakdong, all have their sources in the Taebaek Mountains, and they flow between the ranges before entering their lowland plains. Nearly all the country’s rivers flow westward or southward into either the Yellow Sea or the East China Sea; only a few short, [swift](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/swift) rivers drain eastward from the Taebaek Mountains. The [Nakdong River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nakdong-River), South Korea’s longest, runs southward for 325 miles (523 km) to the Korea Strait. Streamflow is highly variable, being greatest during the wet summer months and considerably less in the relatively dry winter.

## Soils

Most of South Korea’s soils derive from granite and gneiss. Sandy and brown-colored soils are common, and they are generally well-leached and have little humus content. Podzolic soils (ash-gray forest soils), resulting from the cold of the long winter season, are found in the highlands.

## Climate

The greatest influence on the climate of the Korean peninsula is its proximity to the main Asian landmass. This produces the marked summer-winter temperature extremes of a continental climate while also establishing the northeast Asian monsoons (seasonal winds) that affect precipitation patterns. The annual range of temperature is greater in the north and in interior regions of the peninsula than in the south and along the coast, reflecting the relative decline in continental influences in the latter areas.

South Korea’s climate is [characterized](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/characterized) by a cold, relatively dry winter and a hot, humid summer. The coldest average monthly temperatures in winter drop below freezing except along the southern coast. The average January temperature at Seoul is in the low 20s °F (about −5 °C), while the corresponding average at [Busan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Busan), on the southeast coast, is in the mid-30s °F (about 2 °C). By contrast, summer temperatures are relatively uniform across the country, the average monthly temperature for August (the warmest month) being in the high 70s °F (about 25 °C).

Annual precipitation ranges from about 35 to 60 inches (900 to 1,500 mm) on the mainland. [Daegu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Daegu), on the east coast, is the driest area, while the southern coast is the wettest; southern Jeju Island receives more than 70 inches (1,800 mm) annually. Up to three-fifths of the annual precipitation is received in June–August, during the summer monsoon, the annual distribution being more even in the extreme south. Occasionally, late-summer typhoons ( [tropical cyclones](https://www.britannica.com/science/tropical-cyclone)) cause heavy showers and storms along the southern coast. Precipitation in winter falls mainly as snow, with the heaviest amounts occurring in the Taebaek Mountains. The [frost-free season](https://www.britannica.com/topic/growing-season) ranges from 170 days in the northern highlands to more than 240 days on Jeju Island.
